Delirium (confusion)

🏠 Home / Senior health / Memory loss and dementia

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Acute Onset: Delirium is a sudden, severe confusion and rapid changes in brain function that occur with physical or mental illness.
  • Fluctuating Course: Symptoms often worsen at night (sundowning) and can vary significantly throughout the day.
  • Reversible Condition: Unlike dementia, delirium is often temporary and reversible if the underlying cause is identified and treated promptly.
  • Medical Emergency: It is a serious condition requiring immediate medical attention to prevent complications and improve outcomes.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Sudden Mental Status Change: An abrupt shift in attention, awareness, and cognition, developing over hours to days.
  • Difficulty Focusing: Inability to maintain or shift attention, easily distracted, or appearing 'out of it'.
  • Disorientation: Confusion about time, place, or person.
  • Fluctuating Consciousness: Levels of alertness can range from extreme drowsiness to hyper-alertness and agitation.
  • Perceptual Disturbances: May include hallucinations (seeing or hearing things that aren't there) or delusions (false beliefs).
  • Sleep-Wake Cycle Disruption: Insomnia at night and drowsiness during the day, or complete reversal of sleep patterns.
  • Emotional Lability: Rapid shifts in mood, such as anxiety, fear, irritability, or apathy.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Identify Underlying Causes: Promptly investigate and treat potential triggers such as infections, dehydration, medication side effects, electrolyte imbalances, pain, or withdrawal.
  • Medication Review: Carefully assess all current medications, especially those with anticholinergic properties, benzodiazepines, or opioids, which can precipitate or worsen delirium.
  • Environmental Modifications: Maintain a calm, well-lit environment, ensure consistent routines, provide familiar objects, and minimize excessive noise or sensory overload.
  • Hydration and Nutrition: Ensure adequate fluid intake and provide regular, easily digestible meals to prevent dehydration and malnutrition.
  • Promote Mobility: Encourage safe ambulation and activity to prevent deconditioning and improve circulation, if medically appropriate.
  • Sensory Aids: Ensure patients have and use their glasses and hearing aids to optimize sensory input and reduce misinterpretations.
  • Avoid Physical Restraints: Physical restraints can increase agitation, fear, and worsen delirium; use only as a last resort for safety.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Maintain Hydration: Crucial to ensure consistent fluid intake to prevent dehydration, a common and reversible cause of delirium. Offer water, clear broths, or diluted juices frequently.
  • Nutritional Support: Provide small, frequent, nutrient-dense meals that are easy to chew and swallow, especially if appetite is poor. Consider fortified foods or supplements if intake is insufficient.
  • Monitor Electrolytes: Be aware that dietary intake and fluid balance directly impact electrolyte levels; imbalances (e.g., hyponatremia, hypernatremia) can trigger or exacerbate delirium.
  • Avoid Deliriogenic Substances: Restrict or eliminate alcohol, excessive caffeine, and other substances that can alter mental status or interact negatively with medications.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Stay Calm and Reassuring: Approach the individual with a gentle, consistent, and supportive demeanor, avoiding confrontation or arguments.
  • Frequent Reorientation: Regularly remind the person of the time, place, and situation using clear, simple language and familiar cues (e.g., clock, calendar, family photos).
  • Simplify Communication: Use short, direct sentences and allow ample time for processing and response. Avoid complex questions or multiple commands.
  • Maintain Routine: Establish and adhere to a predictable daily schedule for meals, hygiene, and activities to provide structure and reduce confusion.
  • Ensure Safety: Implement fall precautions, remove potential hazards from the environment, and provide close supervision to prevent self-harm or wandering.
  • Report Changes Promptly: Document and immediately communicate any new or worsening symptoms, changes in behavior, or potential triggers to the medical team.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Comprehensive Assessment: A thorough medical evaluation is paramount to identify and address the underlying cause(s) of delirium, which may include infection, metabolic derangement, medication effects, or organ failure.
  • Medication Optimization: Meticulously review the patient's medication regimen, discontinuing or reducing doses of deliriogenic drugs and optimizing necessary medications.
  • Multidisciplinary Care: Collaborate with nursing, pharmacy, physical therapy, and family to implement a holistic management plan focusing on non-pharmacological interventions first.
  • Prognosis and Education: Discuss the transient nature of delirium with patients and families, differentiating it from dementia and outlining the expected course and potential for recovery.
  • Prevention Strategies: Implement proactive measures for at-risk patients, such as the Hospital Elder Life Program (HELP) protocols, to reduce incidence in hospitalized older adults.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Assessment Tools: Utilize validated tools like the Confusion Assessment Method (CAM) or the Delirium Rating Scale (DRS) for systematic and objective assessment of delirium.
  • Non-Pharmacological Interventions: Prioritize environmental modifications, reorientation, sleep hygiene, early mobilization, and sensory enhancement as first-line interventions.
  • Meticulous Documentation: Accurately record mental status changes, potential triggers, interventions implemented, and patient responses to track the course of delirium and inform care.
  • Family Engagement: Educate families about delirium, its causes, and their crucial role in providing comfort, reorientation, and familiar presence to the patient.
  • Prevention Bundles: Implement evidence-based delirium prevention bundles, focusing on risk factor modification in vulnerable populations.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • ICD-10 Codes: F05 (Delirium, not induced by alcohol and other psychoactive substances), R41.0 (Disorientation, unspecified), R41.82 (Altered mental status, unspecified).
  • Pathophysiology: Involves a complex interplay of neurotransmitter imbalances (e.g., cholinergic deficiency, dopaminergic excess), systemic inflammation, neuronal injury, and impaired cerebral metabolism.
  • Risk Factors: Advanced age, pre-existing cognitive impairment (dementia), multiple comorbidities, sensory deficits, polypharmacy, surgery, infection, dehydration, and critical illness.
  • Pharmacological Management: Judicious use of low-dose antipsychotics (e.g., haloperidol, risperidone) for severe agitation or psychotic symptoms, primarily when non-pharmacological methods fail and safety is a concern. Benzodiazepines are generally avoided unless delirium is due to alcohol or sedative withdrawal.
