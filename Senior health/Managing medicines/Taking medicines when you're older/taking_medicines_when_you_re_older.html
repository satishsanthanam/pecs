Taking medicines when you're older

🏠 Home / Senior health / Managing medicines

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Increased Sensitivity: Older adults often experience heightened sensitivity to medications due to age-related physiological changes affecting drug metabolism and elimination.
  • Polypharmacy Risks: Managing multiple medications (polypharmacy) significantly increases the risk of adverse drug reactions, drug-drug interactions, and medication non-adherence.
  • Altered Drug Effects: Changes in body composition, kidney function, and liver function can alter how drugs are absorbed, distributed, metabolized, and excreted, leading to different effects than in younger individuals.
  • Regular Review Essential: Annual or more frequent medication reviews by a healthcare provider are crucial to optimize therapy, identify unnecessary medications, and prevent potential harm.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Dizziness and Falls: Medications, especially those affecting the central nervous system or blood pressure, can increase the risk of dizziness, unsteadiness, and falls.
  • Cognitive Changes: New or worsening confusion, memory problems, or delirium can be a side effect of certain medications or drug interactions.
  • Fatigue and Weakness: Unexplained tiredness or muscle weakness may indicate an adverse drug reaction or an inappropriate medication regimen.
  • Gastrointestinal Disturbances: New onset constipation, diarrhea, nausea, or appetite changes can often be linked to medication side effects.
  • Mood Alterations: Increased anxiety, depression, or irritability may sometimes be attributed to medication effects rather than underlying psychological conditions.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Never Self-Adjust Doses: Always consult a healthcare provider before changing medication dosages or stopping any prescribed drug.
  • Maintain a Comprehensive List: Keep an up-to-date list of all medications, including over-the-counter drugs, supplements, and herbal remedies, to share with all healthcare providers.
  • Understand Each Medication: Know the purpose, correct dosage, timing, and potential side effects of every medication you take.
  • Proper Storage: Store medications as directed, away from extreme temperatures and moisture, and out of reach of children and pets.
  • Check Expiry Dates: Regularly check and discard expired medications safely, as their potency or safety can be compromised.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Grapefruit Interactions: Avoid grapefruit and grapefruit juice with certain medications like statins, calcium channel blockers, and some immunosuppressants, as it can increase drug levels.
  • Vitamin K and Warfarin: Maintain a consistent intake of vitamin K-rich foods (e.g., leafy greens) if taking warfarin, as fluctuations can affect its anticoagulant effect.
  • Dairy and Antibiotics: Avoid taking certain antibiotics (e.g., tetracyclines, fluoroquinolones) with dairy products or calcium supplements, as they can reduce drug absorption.
  • Alcohol Avoidance: Refrain from consuming alcohol when taking medications that cause drowsiness, affect the liver, or have known interactions (e.g., benzodiazepines, opioids, metronidazole).
  • Adequate Hydration: Ensure sufficient fluid intake, especially when taking medications that can affect kidney function or cause dehydration.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Medication Organization: Assist with setting up pill organizers or reminders to ensure medications are taken correctly and on schedule.
  • Observe for Changes: Monitor for any new or worsening symptoms, behavioral changes, or signs of adverse drug reactions, and report them promptly to a healthcare provider.
  • Facilitate Communication: Help the older adult communicate effectively with their doctors and pharmacists, ensuring all questions are asked and understood.
  • Manage Refills: Keep track of prescription refill schedules to prevent interruptions in medication therapy.
  • Safe Disposal: Understand and follow guidelines for the safe disposal of unused or expired medications to prevent accidental ingestion or environmental contamination.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Individualized Prescribing: Tailor medication regimens to the individual's specific health status, functional capacity, and treatment goals, rather than relying on standard adult doses.
  • Deprescribing Consideration: Regularly evaluate the necessity of each medication, actively considering deprescribing when benefits no longer outweigh risks or when medications are no longer indicated.
  • Monitor Renal and Hepatic Function: Routinely assess kidney and liver function, as age-related decline in these organs significantly impacts drug clearance and metabolism.
  • Start Low, Go Slow: When initiating new medications, begin with the lowest effective dose and titrate slowly, carefully monitoring for therapeutic effects and adverse reactions.
  • Comprehensive Medication Review: Conduct thorough medication reconciliation at every transition of care and during routine visits to identify discrepancies and potential issues.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Beers Criteria Application: Utilize the Beers Criteria for Potentially Inappropriate Medication Use in Older Adults as a guide to identify medications that should generally be avoided or used with caution.
  • STOPP/START Criteria: Apply the Screening Tool of Older Persons' Prescriptions (STOPP) to identify potentially inappropriate medications and the Screening Tool to Alert doctors to Right Treatment (START) for potential prescribing omissions.
  • Patient Education Strategies: Develop effective patient and caregiver education plans, using clear, simple language and visual aids to enhance understanding and adherence.
  • Adherence Assessment: Systematically assess medication adherence and identify barriers, such as cost, complexity of regimen, or cognitive impairment.
  • Advocacy for Review: Advocate for regular interdisciplinary medication reviews, collaborating with pharmacists and physicians to optimize pharmacotherapy for older patients.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Pharmacokinetics in Aging: Refers to age-related changes in drug absorption, distribution, metabolism (hepatic), and excretion (renal), impacting drug concentrations and effects.
  • Pharmacodynamics in Aging: Describes altered physiological responses to drugs at the receptor or cellular level in older adults, leading to increased sensitivity or altered efficacy.
  • Polypharmacy: The concurrent use of multiple medications, often defined as five or more, which increases the risk of adverse drug events and drug interactions.
  • Adverse Drug Reactions (ADRs): Unintended and harmful responses to a medication at normal doses, which are more common and often more severe in older adults.
  • Medication Reconciliation: The process of creating the most accurate list possible of all medications a patient is taking, including drug name, dosage, frequency, and route, and comparing it against the physician's admission, transfer, and/or discharge orders.
  • Deprescribing: The systematic process of identifying and discontinuing medications where the potential harms outweigh the potential benefits within an individual's care goals, often involving a gradual reduction.
