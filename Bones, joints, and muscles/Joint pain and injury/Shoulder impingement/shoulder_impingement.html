Shoulder impingement

🏠 Home / Bones, joints, and muscles / Joint pain and injury

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Definition: Shoulder impingement syndrome occurs when the tendons of the rotator cuff and/or the subacromial bursa become compressed and irritated as they pass through the subacromial space.
  • Common Causes: Often results from repetitive overhead activities, poor posture, or anatomical variations of the acromion bone.
  • Mechanism: The compression typically happens during arm elevation, leading to inflammation, pain, and restricted movement.
  • Affected Structures: Primarily involves the supraspinatus tendon and the subacromial bursa, which can become inflamed (tendinitis, bursitis).

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Pain with Overhead Activities: Sharp or aching pain when lifting the arm above shoulder height or reaching overhead.
  • Night Pain: Discomfort that worsens at night, especially when lying on the affected shoulder.
  • Pain Reaching Behind Back: Difficulty and pain with internal rotation and adduction, such as reaching for a seatbelt or bra strap.
  • Weakness: Perceived or actual weakness in the affected arm, particularly during lifting or rotating movements.
  • Catching or Grinding Sensation: A feeling of catching, popping, or grinding within the shoulder joint during movement.
  • Limited Range of Motion: Progressive restriction in the active and passive range of motion of the shoulder.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Avoid Aggravating Activities: Temporarily cease or modify activities that exacerbate shoulder pain, especially repetitive overhead movements.
  • Maintain Proper Posture: Be mindful of shoulder and upper back posture to optimize the subacromial space and reduce compression.
  • Ergonomic Adjustments: Ensure workstations and daily activities are set up ergonomically to minimize strain on the shoulder.
  • Gradual Return to Activity: When recovering, reintroduce activities slowly and progressively, guided by pain levels and professional advice.
  • Early Intervention: Seek medical evaluation promptly if shoulder pain persists or worsens to prevent chronic issues or progression to rotator cuff tears.
  • Proper Warm-up and Cool-down: Engage in appropriate warm-up exercises before physical activity and cool-down stretches afterward to prepare and recover shoulder tissues.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Anti-inflammatory Foods: Incorporate foods rich in omega-3 fatty acids (e.g., fatty fish, flaxseeds, walnuts) to support the body's natural anti-inflammatory processes.
  • Antioxidant-Rich Produce: Consume a variety of colorful fruits and vegetables (e.g., berries, leafy greens, bell peppers) high in antioxidants to aid in cellular repair and reduce oxidative stress.
  • Adequate Protein Intake: Ensure sufficient intake of lean proteins (e.g., chicken, fish, legumes, tofu) to support muscle and tendon repair and recovery.
  • Hydration: Maintain optimal hydration by drinking plenty of water throughout the day to support joint lubrication and overall tissue health.
  • Limit Inflammatory Foods: Consider reducing intake of highly processed foods, excessive sugars, and unhealthy fats that may contribute to systemic inflammation.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Do Not Push Through Pain: Continuing activities that cause pain can worsen the impingement and potentially lead to more severe damage, such as a rotator cuff tear.
  • Adherence to Therapy: Strict adherence to prescribed physical therapy exercises and activity modifications is crucial for effective recovery and preventing recurrence.
  • Watch for Worsening Symptoms: Report any increase in pain, loss of motion, or new weakness to your healthcare provider immediately.
  • Avoid Self-Diagnosis and Treatment: Proper diagnosis by a medical professional is essential, as other conditions can mimic shoulder impingement.
  • Risk of Chronic Pain: Untreated or poorly managed impingement can lead to chronic pain, stiffness, and functional limitations.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Comprehensive Diagnosis: Diagnosis involves a thorough physical examination, specific provocative tests (e.g., Neer's, Hawkins-Kennedy), and often imaging studies like X-rays (to assess bone spurs) or MRI (to visualize soft tissues).
  • Conservative Management First: Initial treatment typically focuses on conservative measures including rest, activity modification, NSAIDs, physical therapy, and sometimes corticosteroid injections to reduce inflammation.
  • Physical Therapy Focus: Rehabilitation aims to improve rotator cuff strength, scapular stability, and overall shoulder mechanics to decompress the subacromial space.
  • Surgical Considerations: If conservative treatments fail after several months, surgical options such as subacromial decompression (arthroscopic acromioplasty) may be considered to create more space for the tendons.
  • Individualized Treatment Plan: Each patient's treatment plan is tailored based on the severity of impingement, underlying causes, and individual activity levels.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Patient Assessment: Assess pain levels using a validated scale, evaluate active and passive range of motion, and identify aggravating and alleviating factors.
  • Education on Activity Modification: Instruct patients on proper body mechanics, ergonomic adjustments, and avoidance of overhead activities that exacerbate symptoms.
  • Medication Management: Educate patients on the correct use, dosage, and potential side effects of prescribed NSAIDs or other pain relievers.
  • Physical Therapy Adherence: Reinforce the importance of consistent adherence to physical therapy exercises and home exercise programs for optimal recovery.
  • Post-Injection Care: Provide instructions for post-corticosteroid injection care, including temporary rest and monitoring for infection or increased pain.
  • Pre/Post-Operative Care: For surgical candidates, provide comprehensive pre-operative teaching and post-operative care instructions, including pain management, wound care, and sling use.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Anatomy: Rotator cuff (supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor, subscapularis), subacromial bursa, acromion, coracoacromial ligament.
  • Pathophysiology: Mechanical compression of rotator cuff tendons (especially supraspinatus) and/or subacromial bursa against the undersurface of the acromion and coracoacromial ligament during arm elevation.
  • Clinical Tests: Neer's Impingement Test, Hawkins-Kennedy Impingement Test, Painful Arc Test.
  • Imaging: X-ray (to rule out bony abnormalities like spurs), MRI (to assess soft tissue inflammation, tendinopathy, or tears).
  • Differential Diagnoses: Rotator cuff tear, adhesive capsulitis (frozen shoulder), biceps tendinitis, cervical radiculopathy, glenohumeral osteoarthritis.
  • ICD-10 Codes: M75.40 (Impingement syndrome of unspecified shoulder), M75.41 (Impingement syndrome of right shoulder), M75.42 (Impingement syndrome of left shoulder).
