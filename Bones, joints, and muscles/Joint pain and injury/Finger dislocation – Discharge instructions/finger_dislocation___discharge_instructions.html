Finger dislocation – Discharge instructions

🏠 Home / Bones, joints, and muscles / Joint pain and injury

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Understanding Your Injury: A finger dislocation occurs when the bones at a joint are forced out of their normal alignment. Your finger has been successfully reduced, meaning the bones have been put back into place.
  • Importance of Immobilization: A splint or buddy taping is crucial to protect the joint while it heals and to prevent re-dislocation. Follow instructions precisely regarding its wear.
  • Expected Recovery: Initial healing typically takes 3-6 weeks, but full recovery of strength and range of motion can take several months with consistent rehabilitation.
  • Follow-up Appointments: Adhere strictly to your scheduled follow-up appointments with your physician or hand therapist to monitor healing and adjust your care plan.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Persistent or Worsening Pain: While some discomfort is normal, increasing pain that is not relieved by medication or rest requires medical evaluation.
  • Increased Swelling or Bruising: Significant or spreading swelling, or new bruising, could indicate further injury or complication.
  • Numbness or Tingling: Any new or worsening sensation of numbness, tingling, or "pins and needles" in the finger could signal nerve compression.
  • Inability to Move Finger: If you cannot move your finger as instructed by your therapist or experience new stiffness that prevents movement, seek advice.
  • Signs of Infection: Redness, warmth, pus, or fever around the injury site are signs of potential infection and require immediate medical attention.
  • Re-dislocation: If you feel or see the joint shift out of place again, contact your healthcare provider immediately.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Splint Care: Keep your splint clean and dry. Do not remove it unless specifically instructed by your healthcare provider. Report any skin irritation or pressure points from the splint.
  • Activity Restrictions: Avoid strenuous activities, heavy lifting, or any movements that could put stress on the injured finger. Protect the finger from bumps or impacts.
  • Pain Management: Take prescribed pain medication as directed. Over-the-counter options like ibuprofen or acetaminophen can also help manage discomfort and swelling.
  • Ice Application: Apply ice packs to the injured area for 15-20 minutes every 2-3 hours during the first 48-72 hours to reduce swelling. Ensure a cloth barrier is between ice and skin.
  • Elevation: Keep your hand elevated above heart level, especially during the first few days, to minimize swelling.
  • Hand Hygiene: Maintain good hand hygiene to prevent infection, especially if there are any open wounds or pin sites.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Protein Intake: Ensure adequate protein consumption (e.g., lean meats, fish, eggs, legumes, dairy) to support tissue repair and collagen synthesis essential for healing.
  • Vitamin C & Zinc: Incorporate foods rich in Vitamin C (e.g., citrus fruits, berries, bell peppers) and Zinc (e.g., nuts, seeds, whole grains) which are vital cofactors in wound healing and immune function.
  • Calcium & Vitamin D: Support bone health by consuming calcium-rich foods (e.g., dairy, fortified plant milks, leafy greens) and ensuring sufficient Vitamin D intake (e.g., fatty fish, fortified foods, sunlight exposure).
  • Hydration: Maintain optimal hydration by drinking plenty of water throughout the day, which is crucial for overall cellular function and nutrient transport to healing tissues.
  • Anti-inflammatory Foods: Consider incorporating foods with natural anti-inflammatory properties, such as omega-3 fatty acids (e.g., salmon, flaxseed) and colorful fruits and vegetables.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Monitoring for Complications: Assist the patient in monitoring for signs of infection, neurovascular compromise (numbness, pallor, coldness), or re-dislocation.
  • Medication Adherence: Help ensure the patient takes prescribed medications as directed, especially pain relievers and any antibiotics if indicated.
  • Splint Integrity: Regularly check the splint for proper fit, signs of damage, or areas causing skin irritation. Report concerns to the healthcare team.
  • Activity Modification: Help the patient modify daily activities to protect the injured finger and prevent accidental re-injury.
  • Transportation for Follow-up: Ensure the patient attends all scheduled follow-up appointments with the physician or hand therapist.
  • Emergency Contact: Be aware of who to contact and when to seek emergency medical care if complications arise.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Adherence to Rehabilitation: Consistent engagement in prescribed hand therapy exercises is paramount for restoring full range of motion, strength, and function.
  • Preventing Stiffness: Early, controlled motion, as guided by a therapist, is crucial to prevent chronic stiffness and improve long-term outcomes.
  • Risk of Re-injury: The dislocated joint may be more prone to re-dislocation or instability, especially in the initial healing phase. Protect the finger diligently.
  • Long-term Considerations: While most finger dislocations heal well, some patients may experience residual stiffness, mild pain, or an increased risk of post-traumatic arthritis over time.
  • Open Communication: Do not hesitate to contact your healthcare provider with any concerns or questions regarding your recovery.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Neurovascular Assessment: Regularly assess the injured digit for capillary refill, sensation (light touch, two-point discrimination), and motor function to rule out neurovascular compromise.
  • Pain Assessment & Management: Utilize a pain scale (e.g., 0-10) to assess pain levels and educate patients on appropriate analgesic use and non-pharmacological pain relief strategies (RICE).
  • Splint/Buddy Taping Education: Provide clear, step-by-step instructions on splint care, skin integrity checks, and the rationale for immobilization. Demonstrate proper buddy taping technique if applicable.
  • Activity of Daily Living (ADL) Modification: Educate patients on adaptive techniques for ADLs to protect the injured finger and promote independence while healing.
  • Rehabilitation Reinforcement: Reinforce the importance of prescribed exercises and provide contact information for hand therapy referrals.
  • Infection Surveillance: Instruct patients on signs and symptoms of infection and when to report them, especially if there are any open wounds.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Phalangeal Dislocation: Displacement of the bones (phalanges) forming the finger joints. Common types include Proximal Interphalangeal (PIP) and Distal Interphalangeal (DIP) joint dislocations.
  • Metacarpophalangeal (MCP) Joint Dislocation: Dislocation occurring at the joint between the metacarpal bone in the hand and the proximal phalanx of the finger.
  • Closed Reduction: A non-surgical procedure where the dislocated bones are manually manipulated back into their correct anatomical position.
  • Volar Plate Injury: Often associated with PIP joint dislocations, this is an injury to the thick ligament on the palm side of the joint, crucial for stability.
  • Collateral Ligament Injury: Damage to the ligaments on the sides of the finger joint, which provide stability against sideways forces.
  • Post-traumatic Arthritis: Long-term complication where damage to the joint cartilage from the dislocation can lead to degenerative joint disease.
  • Boutonnière Deformity: A potential complication of PIP joint dislocation, characterized by hyperextension of the DIP joint and flexion of the PIP joint.
