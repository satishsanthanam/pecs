Shoulder arthroscopy

🏠 Home / Bones, joints, and muscles / Bone and joint surgery

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Minimally Invasive Procedure: Shoulder arthroscopy is a surgical technique that uses a small camera (arthroscope) and specialized instruments inserted through tiny incisions to diagnose and treat problems inside and around the shoulder joint.
  • Common Conditions Treated: This procedure is frequently performed for rotator cuff tears, labral tears (e.g., SLAP lesions, Bankart lesions), shoulder impingement syndrome, removal of loose bodies, treatment of shoulder instability, and repair of biceps tendon issues.
  • Benefits Over Open Surgery: Patients typically experience less pain, smaller scars, reduced blood loss, and a potentially faster recovery time compared to traditional open shoulder surgery.
  • Outpatient or Short Stay: Most shoulder arthroscopies are performed on an outpatient basis, meaning you go home the same day, or require a short overnight hospital stay.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Persistent Shoulder Pain: Chronic pain in the shoulder that does not improve with conservative treatments like rest, medication, or physical therapy.
  • Limited Range of Motion: Difficulty lifting the arm, reaching overhead, or performing daily activities due to stiffness or pain in the shoulder joint.
  • Shoulder Weakness: Noticeable loss of strength when attempting to move or lift objects with the affected arm.
  • Instability or Dislocation: A feeling of the shoulder 'giving out,' 'slipping,' or recurrent dislocations.
  • Clicking, Popping, or Grinding: Audible or palpable sensations within the shoulder joint during movement, often indicative of cartilage or labral damage.
  • Night Pain: Pain that worsens at night, especially when lying on the affected side, which can disrupt sleep.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Medication Review: Inform your surgeon about all medications, supplements, and herbal remedies you are taking. You will likely need to stop blood thinners (e.g., aspirin, warfarin, NSAIDs) several days to a week before surgery to minimize bleeding risk.
  • Pre-Operative Fasting: Adhere strictly to NPO (nil per os) instructions, typically no food or drink after midnight on the day of surgery, to prevent aspiration during anesthesia.
  • Arrange for Assistance: Plan for a responsible adult to drive you home after surgery and assist with daily activities for at least the first 24-48 hours, as you will be under the effects of anesthesia and pain medication.
  • Post-Operative Immobilization: Expect to wear a sling for a prescribed period (often 2-6 weeks) to protect the repair and allow initial healing. Do not remove it unless instructed by your surgeon or physical therapist.
  • Avoid Heavy Lifting & Reaching: Strictly avoid lifting heavy objects, pushing, pulling, or reaching overhead with the affected arm during the initial recovery phase to prevent re-injury.
  • Infection Monitoring: Watch for signs of infection at the incision sites, including increased redness, swelling, warmth, pus-like discharge, or fever. Report these immediately to your medical team.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Pre-Procedure Fasting: You must strictly follow the NPO (nothing by mouth) guidelines provided by your surgical team, typically no food or drink (including water, gum, and candy) after midnight on the day before your surgery.
  • Post-Anesthesia Reintroduction: After surgery, start with clear liquids (water, clear broth, apple juice) and gradually progress to light, easily digestible foods (toast, crackers, plain rice) as tolerated to minimize nausea and vomiting from anesthesia.
  • Avoid Heavy/Greasy Foods: For the first 24-48 hours post-surgery, avoid heavy, fatty, or spicy foods that can upset your stomach while recovering from anesthesia.
  • Maintain Hydration: Drink plenty of fluids once cleared to do so to aid in recovery and help flush anesthesia from your system, but avoid alcohol for at least 24-48 hours post-surgery or while taking pain medication.
  • Fiber-Rich Foods: Incorporate fiber-rich foods to prevent constipation, which can be a side effect of pain medications.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Transportation & Supervision: Ensure the patient has a reliable ride home post-surgery and someone to stay with them for at least 24 hours to monitor for adverse effects of anesthesia and assist with basic needs.
  • Medication Management: Help the patient adhere to their prescribed pain medication schedule and other medications, ensuring they understand dosages and timing.
  • Sling Assistance: Assist the patient with proper application and removal of the sling, ensuring it is worn correctly as instructed by the medical team.
  • Ice Pack Application: Help apply ice packs to the shoulder as directed to reduce swelling and pain, ensuring a barrier (e.g., towel) is used between the ice and skin.
  • Monitor for Complications: Be vigilant for signs of complications such such as excessive bleeding from incision sites, severe or worsening pain not relieved by medication, fever, chills, numbness or tingling in the hand/fingers, or difficulty breathing. Contact the surgical team immediately if any of these occur.
  • Activity Restriction Enforcement: Gently remind and assist the patient in adhering to activity restrictions, preventing them from using the affected arm for tasks like reaching, lifting, or pushing.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Realistic Expectations: While arthroscopy is minimally invasive, recovery is a process. Patients must understand that full recovery can take several months, especially for complex repairs like rotator cuff or labral tears.
  • Adherence to Rehabilitation: The success of shoulder arthroscopy heavily relies on diligent adherence to the prescribed physical therapy program. Skipping or rushing therapy can compromise outcomes and increase the risk of re-injury.
  • Pain Management Strategy: We will develop a comprehensive pain management plan. It's crucial to take medications as prescribed and communicate any uncontrolled pain or side effects promptly.
  • Follow-Up Appointments: Regular follow-up visits are essential to monitor healing progress, assess range of motion and strength, and adjust the rehabilitation plan as needed.
  • Understanding Risks: While generally safe, all surgeries carry risks including infection, bleeding, nerve damage, stiffness, and the possibility of needing further surgery. These will be discussed in detail during your consultation.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Pre-Operative Assessment: Conduct thorough patient assessment including medical history, allergies, current medications, and baseline neurovascular status of the affected extremity. Verify NPO status.
  • Patient Education: Educate patients on the surgical procedure, post-operative pain management, sling use, activity restrictions, and signs/symptoms of complications to report.
  • Pain Management: Administer prescribed analgesics, assess pain levels regularly using a validated scale, and evaluate the effectiveness of interventions. Consider multimodal pain approaches.
  • Neurovascular Checks: Perform frequent neurovascular assessments (color, temperature, sensation, capillary refill, pulse) of the affected extremity post-operatively to detect any compromise.
  • Wound Care: Monitor incision sites for signs of infection (redness, swelling, warmth, drainage), reinforce dressings as needed, and educate on proper home wound care.
  • Discharge Planning: Ensure patient and caregiver understand discharge instructions, medication regimen, physical therapy referrals, and follow-up appointments. Confirm safe transportation.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Arthroscope: A small fiber-optic camera used to visualize the inside of a joint.
  • Rotator Cuff: A group of four muscles and their tendons that stabilize the shoulder joint and allow for rotation and lifting of the arm (supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor, subscapularis).
  • Labrum: A ring of cartilage that surrounds the glenoid (shoulder socket), deepening it and providing stability to the shoulder joint.
  • Subacromial Decompression: A procedure to remove bone spurs or inflamed tissue from beneath the acromion to relieve impingement.
  • Glenohumeral Joint: The main ball-and-socket joint of the shoulder, formed by the head of the humerus and the glenoid fossa of the scapula.
  • Anesthesia: Typically general anesthesia, often combined with a regional nerve block (e.g., interscalene block) for post-operative pain control.
  • Post-Operative Rehabilitation: A structured program of physical therapy exercises designed to restore range of motion, strength, and function to the shoulder.
