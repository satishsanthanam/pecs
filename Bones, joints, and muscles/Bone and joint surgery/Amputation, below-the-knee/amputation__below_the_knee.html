Amputation, below-the-knee

🏠 Home / Bones, joints, and muscles / Bone and joint surgery

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Definition: Below-the-knee amputation (BKA), also known as transtibial amputation, is the surgical removal of the lower leg, typically preserving the knee joint.
  • Primary Causes: Often performed due to severe peripheral artery disease (PAD), uncontrolled diabetes leading to gangrene or non-healing ulcers, severe trauma, or overwhelming infection.
  • Surgical Goal: To remove diseased or damaged tissue, alleviate pain, prevent the spread of infection, and create a functional residual limb suitable for prosthetic fitting and rehabilitation.
  • Prognosis: Successful rehabilitation aims to restore mobility and independence, with prosthetic use being a key component of recovery.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Severe Limb Pain: Unrelenting pain in the affected limb, often indicative of ischemia or severe infection.
  • Non-Healing Wounds/Ulcers: Chronic sores, particularly on the foot or lower leg, that do not respond to conventional treatment and show signs of tissue death.
  • Gangrene: Presence of dead tissue, often appearing black or discolored, with a foul odor, indicating severe lack of blood supply or infection.
  • Absent Pulses: Lack of palpable pulses in the foot or ankle, signifying severe peripheral arterial disease.
  • Coldness and Numbness: Persistent coldness, pallor, and loss of sensation in the lower limb due to compromised circulation.
  • Systemic Infection: Signs of widespread infection such as fever, chills, and elevated white blood cell count, often originating from a localized limb infection.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Pre-operative Medical Optimization: Rigorous management of underlying conditions like diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and kidney dysfunction to minimize surgical risks.
  • Infection Control: Aggressive treatment of any existing infections prior to surgery, and strict aseptic techniques during and after the procedure.
  • Residual Limb Care: Meticulous wound care, proper stump wrapping or compression sock application to reduce edema and shape the limb for prosthesis.
  • Contracture Prevention: Regular range-of-motion exercises and proper positioning to prevent hip and knee flexion contractures, which can hinder prosthetic fitting.
  • Fall Prevention: Implementing safety measures to prevent falls, especially during early mobilization and prosthetic training.
  • DVT Prophylaxis: Administration of anticoagulants and early ambulation to prevent deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Pre-operative Fasting: Strict adherence to NPO (nil per os) guidelines for a specified period before surgery to prevent aspiration during anesthesia.
  • Post-operative Protein Intake: Increased intake of lean proteins (e.g., chicken, fish, beans, eggs) to support wound healing and tissue regeneration.
  • Adequate Hydration: Maintaining good fluid intake to support overall recovery, kidney function, and prevent constipation, especially when on pain medication.
  • Blood Glucose Management: For diabetic patients, strict control of blood sugar levels through diet to optimize wound healing and prevent complications.
  • Fiber-Rich Foods: Incorporating fruits, vegetables, and whole grains to prevent constipation, a common side effect of opioid pain medications.
  • Vitamin and Mineral Support: Ensuring sufficient intake of vitamins C and A, and zinc, which are crucial for immune function and tissue repair.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Vital Sign Monitoring: Closely monitor blood pressure, heart rate, respiratory rate, and temperature for signs of hemorrhage, shock, or infection.
  • Pain Management: Administer prescribed analgesics promptly and assess pain levels regularly, including for phantom limb pain.
  • Wound Assessment: Observe the residual limb dressing for excessive drainage, signs of infection (redness, warmth, swelling, purulent discharge), or dehiscence.
  • Drain Management: Monitor output from surgical drains (e.g., Hemovac, Jackson-Pratt), ensuring patency and accurate measurement.
  • Positioning and Mobility: Assist with proper positioning to prevent contractures and facilitate early, safe mobilization as per physical therapy orders.
  • Psychological Support: Provide emotional support, listen to concerns, and refer to counseling or support groups as needed for body image and grief.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Multidisciplinary Approach: Successful outcomes require a collaborative team including surgeons, prosthetists, physical therapists, occupational therapists, pain specialists, and psychologists.
  • Early Rehabilitation: Initiating rehabilitation as soon as medically stable is crucial for optimal functional recovery and prosthetic readiness.
  • Patient Education: Comprehensive education on residual limb care, prosthetic use, and potential complications empowers patients in their recovery journey.
  • Long-Term Follow-up: Regular follow-up appointments are essential for monitoring residual limb health, prosthetic fit, and addressing any long-term issues like skin breakdown or neuromas.
  • Realistic Expectations: Setting realistic expectations regarding recovery timelines, functional limitations, and the adjustment period to prosthetic use is vital for patient morale.
  • Addressing Comorbidities: Continued aggressive management of underlying conditions (e.g., diabetes, vascular disease) is paramount to prevent further limb loss or systemic complications.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Nursing Care Plans: Develop individualized care plans focusing on pain management, wound care, mobility, psychological adjustment, and patient education.
  • Residual Limb Wrapping: Master the technique of elastic bandage wrapping or application of compression socks to reduce edema and shape the limb conically.
  • Phantom Limb Sensation vs. Pain: Differentiate between non-painful phantom sensations (e.g., feeling the missing limb is still there) and phantom limb pain, and apply appropriate interventions.
  • Monitoring for Complications: Vigilantly assess for signs of hemorrhage, infection, deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, and skin breakdown on the residual limb.
  • Patient Education: Educate patients and caregivers on self-care techniques, signs of complications to report, and the importance of adherence to rehabilitation protocols.
  • Psychosocial Support: Understand the psychological impact of limb loss and provide empathetic support, facilitating access to mental health resources.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Surgical Techniques: Myodesis (muscle-to-bone attachment) and myoplasty (muscle-to-muscle attachment) are common techniques used to stabilize muscles and provide a padded residual limb.
  • Prosthetic Classification: Transtibial prostheses are categorized by suspension type (e.g., sleeve, suction, pin lock) and foot type (e.g., SACH, energy-storing).
  • Common Complications: Phantom limb pain, residual limb pain, neuromas, skin breakdown, infection, contractures, and heterotopic ossification.
  • Rehabilitation Phases: Includes pre-prosthetic training (wound healing, edema control, strengthening), prosthetic training (donning/doffing, gait training), and advanced activity training.
  • Functional Outcome Measures: Tools like the Amputee Mobility Predictor (AMPPRO/AMPnoPRO) and Prosthetic Limb Users Survey of Mobility (PLUS-M) assess functional status.
  • ICD-10 Codes: Relevant codes include Z89.41- (Acquired absence of lower leg), T87.3- (Complications of amputation stump), and specific codes for underlying causes.
