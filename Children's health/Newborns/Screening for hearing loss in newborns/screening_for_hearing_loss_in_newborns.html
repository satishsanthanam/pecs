Screening for hearing loss in newborns

🏠 Home / Children's health / Newborns

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Universal Screening: All newborns should undergo hearing screening before hospital discharge, ideally within the first month of life.
  • Early Detection: Prompt identification of hearing loss is critical for preventing delays in speech, language, and cognitive development.
  • Screening Methods: Common methods include Otoacoustic Emissions (OAEs) and Automated Auditory Brainstem Response (AABR) tests.
  • Non-invasive: These tests are quick, painless, and can often be performed while the baby is sleeping or quiet.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Risk Factors for Hearing Loss: Clinical indications prompting or increasing the likelihood of hearing loss include a family history of permanent childhood hearing loss.
  • Congenital Infections: Maternal infections during pregnancy such as cytomegalovirus (CMV), rubella, toxoplasmosis, or herpes.
  • Craniofacial Anomalies: Presence of physical abnormalities affecting the head or face, particularly those involving the ear.
  • Low Birth Weight: Birth weight less than 1500 grams.
  • Hyperbilirubinemia: Severe jaundice requiring exchange transfusion.
  • Ototoxic Medication Exposure: Use of certain medications (e.g., aminoglycosides) in the mother during pregnancy or in the neonate.
  • NICU Stay: Prolonged stay (typically >5 days) in a Neonatal Intensive Care Unit.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Follow-Up is Paramount: A failed screening does not confirm hearing loss but necessitates immediate referral for comprehensive diagnostic audiological evaluation.
  • Quiet Environment: Ensure the screening is conducted in a calm, quiet room to minimize external noise interference with test results.
  • Infant State: Ideally, the infant should be quiet, fed, and preferably asleep during the screening for optimal accuracy, especially for OAEs.
  • Avoid Contamination: Ensure the ear canal is clear of vernix caseosa or fluid, which can obstruct the probe and affect OAE results.
  • Parental Education: Provide clear, empathetic information to parents about the screening process, potential results, and the importance of follow-up.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Pre-Screening Feeding: Ensure the newborn is fed and content prior to the screening to promote a calm and cooperative state, which is crucial for accurate results.
  • Comfort and Calm: A well-fed baby is less likely to cry or move excessively, which can interfere with the delicate measurements of OAE and AABR tests.
  • No Specific Restrictions: There are no dietary restrictions or special preparations required for the newborn hearing screening itself.
  • Post-Screening: Normal feeding routines can be resumed immediately after the screening.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Do Not Delay Follow-Up: If a newborn fails the initial hearing screening, it is imperative to schedule a rescreening or a full diagnostic audiological evaluation promptly.
  • Communicate Clearly: Explain to parents that a failed screening does not automatically mean their baby has hearing loss, but rather indicates the need for further assessment.
  • Referral Pathway: Be prepared to provide parents with clear information on the next steps, including contact details for audiology services.
  • Emotional Support: Acknowledge parental anxiety and offer reassurance while emphasizing the importance of early diagnosis and intervention.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Advocate for UNHS: Physicians should strongly advocate for universal newborn hearing screening as a standard of care for all infants.
  • Interpret Results: Understand the implications of 'pass' and 'refer' results and guide parents through the subsequent diagnostic pathway if a referral is indicated.
  • Multidisciplinary Collaboration: Work closely with audiologists, otolaryngologists, and early intervention specialists to ensure comprehensive care for infants identified with hearing loss.
  • Genetic Counseling: Consider referral for genetic counseling if a congenital hearing loss is confirmed, especially if there is a family history or syndromic features.
  • Early Intervention: Emphasize that intervention services, such as hearing aids or cochlear implants, should begin as early as possible to maximize developmental outcomes.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Nurse's Role in Screening: Nurses are often on the front line of performing newborn hearing screenings, requiring proficiency in OAE and AABR equipment operation.
  • Parental Education: Provide clear, concise, and empathetic education to parents about the screening process, its purpose, and the significance of the results.
  • Documentation: Meticulously document screening results, any factors affecting the screening (e.g., crying, vernix), and follow-up plans in the infant's medical record.
  • Troubleshooting: Be able to identify common reasons for 'refer' results (e.g., probe fit, ambient noise, infant movement) and adjust technique as needed.
  • Referral Coordination: Assist in coordinating follow-up appointments for infants who refer on screening, ensuring timely access to diagnostic services.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Otoacoustic Emissions (OAEs): Sounds generated by the outer hair cells of the cochlea in response to auditory stimulation, indicating healthy inner ear function.
  • Auditory Brainstem Response (ABR): An electrophysiological test measuring the electrical activity in the auditory nerve and brainstem in response to sound, assessing the integrity of the auditory pathway.
  • '1-3-6' Rule: A guideline for early hearing detection and intervention: screen by 1 month, diagnose by 3 months, and initiate intervention by 6 months of age.
  • Risk Factors for Congenital Hearing Loss: Includes but is not limited to family history, congenital infections, craniofacial anomalies, low birth weight, hyperbilirubinemia, and ototoxic medication exposure.
  • Prevalence: Approximately 1 to 3 per 1,000 newborns are born with permanent hearing loss, making universal screening essential.
