Bacterial meningitis

🏠 Home / Children's health / Infections and vaccines

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Serious Infection: Bacterial meningitis is a severe infection of the membranes (meninges) surrounding the brain and spinal cord.
  • Medical Emergency: It is a life-threatening condition requiring immediate medical attention and treatment.
  • Common Causes: Caused by various bacteria, including *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, *Neisseria meningitidis*, and *Haemophilus influenzae* type b (Hib).
  • Transmission: Spreads through respiratory droplets and close contact with an infected person.
  • High Risk Group: Infants and young children are particularly vulnerable due to their developing immune systems.
  • Prevention: Childhood vaccinations are highly effective in preventing many types of bacterial meningitis.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Infants & Young Children: May present with fever, irritability, poor feeding, lethargy, a bulging soft spot (fontanelle), unusual crying, and a stiff body or neck.
  • Older Children: Typically experience sudden high fever, severe headache, stiff neck, nausea, vomiting, confusion, sensitivity to light (photophobia), and sometimes seizures.
  • Rash: A distinctive purplish-red rash (non-blanching) can occur, especially with meningococcal meningitis, indicating a medical emergency.
  • Rapid Progression: Symptoms can develop very quickly, often worsening within hours.
  • Neurological Changes: Look for changes in alertness, difficulty waking up, or focal neurological deficits.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Vaccination Schedule: Ensure all recommended childhood immunizations (e.g., Hib, pneumococcal conjugate, meningococcal vaccines) are up-to-date.
  • Hand Hygiene: Practice and teach frequent, thorough handwashing, especially after coughing, sneezing, or using the restroom.
  • Avoid Close Contact: Minimize exposure to individuals who are sick, particularly those with respiratory infections.
  • Early Medical Care: Seek immediate medical evaluation if any symptoms suggestive of meningitis appear in a child.
  • Antibiotic Prophylaxis: Close contacts of a confirmed bacterial meningitis case may require preventative antibiotics as directed by a physician.
  • Droplet Precautions: In a healthcare setting, patients with suspected or confirmed bacterial meningitis require droplet isolation to prevent spread.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Hydration Focus: Maintain adequate fluid intake, especially with fever and vomiting; offer small, frequent sips of water, oral rehydration solutions, or diluted juices.
  • Easily Digestible Foods: Once nausea subsides, introduce bland, soft, and easily digestible foods like toast, rice, bananas, or applesauce.
  • Feeding Support: For infants or very ill children, alternative feeding methods (e.g., nasogastric tube) may be necessary if oral intake is insufficient or unsafe.
  • Avoid Irritants: During acute illness, avoid highly acidic, spicy, fatty, or sugary foods that could exacerbate gastrointestinal upset.
  • Monitor Swallowing: Assess swallowing ability, especially if neurological status is compromised, to prevent aspiration.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Emergency Protocol: Recognize bacterial meningitis as a medical emergency and transport the child to the nearest emergency department without delay.
  • Symptom Monitoring: Continuously observe for any worsening of symptoms, changes in consciousness, or the appearance/progression of a rash.
  • Medication Adherence: Administer all prescribed antibiotics and other medications exactly as directed by the healthcare provider, completing the full course.
  • Infection Control: Follow strict infection control measures, including hand hygiene and any recommended personal protective equipment, when caring for the child.
  • Post-Discharge Vigilance: Be aware of potential long-term complications such as hearing loss, developmental delays, or learning difficulties, and ensure follow-up appointments are kept.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Prompt Diagnosis: Rapid diagnosis via lumbar puncture (spinal tap) for cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis is paramount.
  • Empiric Treatment: Initiate immediate empiric intravenous antibiotics, often broad-spectrum, even before definitive bacterial identification.
  • Supportive Care: Provide comprehensive supportive care, including management of fever, seizures, cerebral edema, and fluid/electrolyte balance.
  • Prognosis Factors: Prognosis is highly dependent on the causative pathogen, the child's age, and the speed of diagnosis and treatment.
  • Potential Sequelae: Counsel parents on the significant risk of long-term neurological complications, including hearing impairment, cognitive deficits, and epilepsy.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Priority Assessment: Focus on neurological assessments (Glasgow Coma Scale, cranial nerves), vital signs, and fluid status.
  • Lumbar Puncture Support: Assist with lumbar puncture, ensuring proper positioning, sterile technique, and post-procedure monitoring for complications like CSF leak.
  • Antibiotic Management: Administer IV antibiotics on schedule, monitor for adverse drug reactions, and ensure correct dosage based on weight and age.
  • Infection Prevention: Implement and maintain strict droplet precautions; educate family members on infection control practices.
  • Parental Education: Provide clear, concise education to parents regarding the disease, treatment plan, potential complications, and the importance of follow-up care.
  • Vaccine Advocacy: Reinforce the critical role of childhood immunizations in preventing bacterial meningitis.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Key Pathogens: *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, *Neisseria meningitidis*, *Haemophilus influenzae* type b (Hib), Group B Streptococcus (neonates).
  • Diagnostic Criteria: CSF analysis (elevated WBCs with neutrophil predominance, low glucose, high protein), Gram stain, bacterial culture, PCR.
  • Empiric Antibiotics: Common regimens include ceftriaxone or cefotaxime, often with vancomycin; ampicillin for neonates.
  • Adjunctive Therapy: Dexamethasone may be used in specific cases (e.g., Hib meningitis) to reduce inflammatory complications.
  • Complications: Hydrocephalus, subdural effusions, cerebral vasculitis, hearing loss, cognitive impairment, seizures, SIADH.
  • Preventative Vaccines: Pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV13), Hib vaccine, Meningococcal conjugate vaccines (MenACWY, MenB).
