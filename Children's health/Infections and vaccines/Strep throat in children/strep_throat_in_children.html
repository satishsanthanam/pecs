Strep throat in children

🏠 Home / Children's health / Infections and vaccines

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Bacterial Infection: Strep throat is caused by Group A Streptococcus (GAS) bacteria, not a virus.
  • Common in Children: Most prevalent in school-aged children (5-15 years old), especially during colder months.
  • Highly Contagious: Spreads easily through respiratory droplets from coughing, sneezing, or sharing items.
  • Requires Antibiotics: Timely antibiotic treatment is crucial to prevent serious complications like rheumatic fever.
  • Not Always a Rash: While some children develop a rash (scarlet fever), many do not.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Sudden Sore Throat: Often severe, with pain when swallowing.
  • Fever: Typically 101°F (38.3°C) or higher.
  • Red and Swollen Tonsils: May have white patches, streaks of pus, or tiny red spots (petechiae) on the soft or hard palate.
  • Headache: A common accompanying symptom.
  • Stomach Ache, Nausea, or Vomiting: More common in younger children.
  • Body Aches: General malaise and muscle soreness.
  • Swollen Lymph Nodes: Tender, enlarged glands in the front of the neck.
  • Rash (Scarlet Fever): A fine, sandpaper-like rash that may appear 1-2 days after the sore throat starts.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Complete Antibiotic Course: Ensure the child takes the full course of antibiotics, even if symptoms improve, to eradicate the bacteria and prevent complications.
  • Prevent Spread: Teach proper hand hygiene, cover coughs/sneezes, and avoid sharing eating utensils, drinks, or food.
  • Isolate When Contagious: Keep the child home from school or daycare until they have been on antibiotics for at least 24 hours and are fever-free.
  • Monitor for Complications: Watch for signs of rheumatic fever (joint pain, rash, heart issues) or kidney problems (dark urine, swelling) after the infection.
  • Discard Toothbrushes: Replace toothbrushes after 2-3 days of antibiotic treatment to prevent reinfection.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Soft, Easy-to-Swallow Foods: Offer foods like soups, yogurt, mashed potatoes, scrambled eggs, and smoothies to minimize throat irritation.
  • Cool Liquids: Provide plenty of cool or lukewarm fluids such as water, diluted juices, or herbal teas to soothe the throat and prevent dehydration.
  • Avoid Acidic and Spicy Foods: Steer clear of citrus juices, tomatoes, spicy dishes, and crunchy foods that can irritate a sore throat.
  • Popsicles and Ice Chips: Can help numb the throat and provide hydration.
  • Ensure Hydration: Encourage frequent small sips of fluids to keep the throat moist and aid recovery.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Administer Medications as Prescribed: Follow the exact dosage and schedule for antibiotics and pain relievers.
  • Monitor for Worsening Symptoms: Seek medical attention if the child develops difficulty breathing, severe difficulty swallowing, a new rash, or signs of dehydration.
  • Fever Management: Use acetaminophen or ibuprofen (age-appropriate) for fever and pain relief as directed by a healthcare provider.
  • Maintain Hygiene: Regularly clean and disinfect surfaces and items the child frequently touches.
  • Educate Family Members: Inform close contacts about the contagious nature of strep throat and encourage vigilance for symptoms.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Accurate Diagnosis is Key: A rapid strep test or throat culture is essential for confirming strep throat, as viral sore throats do not require antibiotics.
  • Antibiotic Selection: Penicillin or amoxicillin are typically the first-line treatments, with alternatives for penicillin-allergic patients.
  • Preventing Resistance: Avoid unnecessary antibiotic use for viral infections to combat antibiotic resistance.
  • Follow-Up if No Improvement: If symptoms do not improve within 24-48 hours of starting antibiotics, a re-evaluation is necessary.
  • Educate on Complications: Counsel parents on the rare but serious complications of untreated strep throat, such as acute rheumatic fever and post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Pathophysiology: Understand the mechanism of Group A Streptococcus infection, including its adherence to pharyngeal tissue and toxin production.
  • Nursing Assessment: Perform thorough throat examinations, palpate cervical lymph nodes, assess for rash (scarlet fever), and evaluate hydration status.
  • Patient Education: Provide clear instructions on antibiotic adherence, symptom management, infection control measures, and recognition of potential complications.
  • Pain Management: Implement nursing interventions for pain relief, including medication administration and non-pharmacological comfort measures.
  • Infection Control: Emphasize droplet precautions in clinical settings and reinforce hand hygiene for both patients and caregivers.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Etiology: Caused by Streptococcus pyogenes (Group A Streptococcus).
  • Diagnostic Methods: Rapid Antigen Detection Test (RADT) for quick results, followed by throat culture for confirmation, especially in negative RADT cases.
  • Treatment Protocols: Standard treatment involves a 10-day course of oral penicillin V or amoxicillin; azithromycin or cephalexin for penicillin-allergic patients.
  • Potential Sequelae: Acute Rheumatic Fever (ARF), Post-Streptococcal Glomerulonephritis (PSGN), peritonsillar abscess, and cervical lymphadenitis.
  • Incubation Period: Typically 2-5 days.
  • Transmission: Primarily via respiratory droplets; less commonly through direct contact with skin lesions.
