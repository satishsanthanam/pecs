Asthma in children – Discharge instructions

🏠 Home / Children's health / Allergies and asthma

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Understanding Your Child's Asthma: Asthma is a chronic lung condition causing inflammation and narrowing of the airways, leading to breathing difficulties. It is manageable with proper care.
  • Asthma Action Plan: You have been provided with a personalized Asthma Action Plan. This plan details daily medications, how to manage worsening symptoms, and when to seek emergency care. Keep it accessible and review it regularly.
  • Medication Adherence: Administer all prescribed medications exactly as directed, even when your child feels well. Long-term control medications prevent flare-ups, while quick-relief medications treat acute symptoms.
  • Identifying Triggers: Learn what triggers your child's asthma (e.g., dust mites, pollen, pet dander, smoke, cold air, exercise, viral infections) and take steps to minimize exposure.
  • Recognizing Worsening Symptoms: Be vigilant for signs that your child's asthma is getting worse, as outlined in your Asthma Action Plan. Early intervention is key to preventing severe attacks.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Persistent Coughing: Especially at night, during exercise, or after exposure to triggers.
  • Wheezing: A high-pitched whistling sound, particularly when breathing out.
  • Shortness of Breath: Difficulty catching breath, rapid breathing, or gasping.
  • Chest Tightness: A feeling of pressure or squeezing in the chest.
  • Retractions: Skin pulling in around the ribs, neck, or collarbone with each breath, indicating increased effort to breathe.
  • Difficulty Speaking or Eating: Inability to speak in full sentences or difficulty feeding in infants, signaling severe respiratory distress.
  • Fatigue or Irritability: Due to increased work of breathing or lack of oxygen.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Environmental Control: Minimize exposure to known triggers. This includes regular cleaning to reduce dust mites, using allergen-proof covers, avoiding smoking in the home, and keeping pets out of the child's bedroom if they are an allergen.
  • Proper Medication Administration: Ensure correct technique for inhaler use, especially with a spacer or nebulizer. Review technique with your healthcare provider regularly.
  • Medication Storage: Store all medications as directed, away from extreme temperatures and out of reach of children. Check expiry dates regularly.
  • Flu and Pneumonia Vaccinations: Ensure your child receives annual influenza vaccinations and recommended pneumococcal vaccines to prevent respiratory infections that can trigger asthma exacerbations.
  • Emergency Preparedness: Always have quick-relief medication (e.g., albuterol) readily available. Know the location of the nearest emergency department and have emergency contact numbers handy.
  • Avoidance of Irritants: Protect your child from strong odors, chemical fumes, and air pollution, which can irritate airways.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Adequate Hydration: Encourage your child to drink plenty of fluids to help thin mucus and make it easier to clear from the airways.
  • Allergenic Food Awareness: If specific food allergies have been identified as asthma triggers, strictly avoid those foods. Consult with an allergist or dietitian for guidance on managing food allergies.
  • Medication-Related Dietary Needs: If your child is prescribed oral corticosteroids, administer them with food to minimize stomach upset.
  • Nutritional Support: Focus on a balanced diet rich in fruits, vegetables, and whole grains to support overall immune function and lung health. Avoid processed foods and excessive sugar.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Follow Asthma Action Plan: Adhere strictly to the individualized Asthma Action Plan provided by your healthcare team. This is your primary guide for managing your child's asthma at home.
  • Medication Schedule: Establish a consistent routine for administering daily controller medications. Use reminders if necessary.
  • Monitor Symptoms: Keep a symptom diary to track your child's asthma symptoms, medication use, and peak flow readings (if applicable). This helps identify patterns and assess treatment effectiveness.
  • Emergency Contacts: Know when to call your doctor, when to go to urgent care, and when to call 911, as detailed in your Asthma Action Plan.
  • Follow-up Appointments: Schedule and attend all recommended follow-up appointments with your child's pediatrician or asthma specialist to review the action plan and adjust treatment as needed.
  • Educate Caregivers: Ensure all caregivers (e.g., school nurses, grandparents, babysitters) are familiar with your child's Asthma Action Plan, medication administration, and emergency procedures.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Proactive Management is Key: Asthma is a chronic condition requiring ongoing management, not just treatment during attacks. Consistent use of controller medications is crucial for preventing exacerbations and maintaining lung function.
  • Individualized Treatment: Each child's asthma is unique. The prescribed Asthma Action Plan and medications are tailored to your child's specific needs and triggers. Do not adjust doses or stop medications without consulting your physician.
  • Early Intervention: Recognizing and treating worsening symptoms early can prevent severe asthma attacks and reduce the need for emergency visits or hospitalizations. Trust your instincts as a parent.
  • Open Communication: Maintain open communication with your healthcare team. Report any concerns, side effects, or changes in your child's asthma symptoms promptly.
  • Quality of Life: With proper management, children with asthma can lead full, active lives. Our goal is to achieve optimal asthma control, allowing your child to participate in all activities without limitations.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Comprehensive Patient Education: Nurses play a vital role in educating parents/caregivers on the Asthma Action Plan, proper inhaler/spacer technique, trigger avoidance, and symptom recognition. Utilize teach-back methods to ensure understanding.
  • Assessment of Respiratory Distress: Develop keen assessment skills to identify signs of respiratory distress in pediatric patients, including accessory muscle use, retractions, nasal flaring, and altered mental status.
  • Medication Reconciliation: Ensure accurate medication reconciliation upon discharge, verifying all prescribed medications, dosages, and administration routes with the family.
  • Psychosocial Support: Address the psychosocial impact of chronic illness on the child and family. Provide resources for support groups or counseling as needed.
  • Advocacy for School Health: Collaborate with families to ensure schools have a copy of the child's Asthma Action Plan and necessary medications for emergencies.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Pharmacology of Asthma Medications: Understand the mechanisms of action for controller medications (e.g., inhaled corticosteroids, long-acting beta-agonists, leukotriene modifiers) and quick-relief medications (e.g., short-acting beta-agonists).
  • Peak Expiratory Flow (PEF) Monitoring: Familiarity with PEF meters and the interpretation of green, yellow, and red zones as indicators of lung function and asthma control.
  • Asthma Pathophysiology: Review the inflammatory processes, bronchoconstriction, and mucus production that characterize asthma, leading to airway obstruction.
  • Spirometry: Knowledge of spirometry as a diagnostic tool for asthma and its role in monitoring lung function over time, including forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1) and forced vital capacity (FVC).
  • Allergen Immunotherapy: Awareness of allergen immunotherapy (allergy shots or sublingual tablets) as a potential treatment option for children with allergic asthma.
  • Differential Diagnosis: Consider other conditions that mimic asthma symptoms in children, such as bronchiolitis, foreign body aspiration, or vocal cord dysfunction.
