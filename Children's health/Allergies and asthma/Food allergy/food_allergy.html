Food allergy

🏠 Home / Children's health / Allergies and asthma

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Definition: A food allergy is an immune system reaction that occurs shortly after eating a certain food. Even a tiny amount of the allergy-triggering food can cause signs and symptoms.
  • Prevalence in Children: Food allergies are common in children, with approximately 1 in 13 children affected. The 'top 9' allergens (milk, eggs, peanuts, tree nuts, soy, wheat, fish, shellfish, sesame) account for most reactions.
  • Severity: Reactions can range from mild (hives, stomach upset) to severe and life-threatening (anaphylaxis), requiring immediate medical intervention.
  • Genetic Predisposition: Children with a family history of allergies, asthma, or eczema are at a higher risk of developing food allergies.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Skin Reactions: Hives (urticaria), eczema flare-ups, redness, swelling (angioedema) of the face, lips, tongue, or throat.
  • Gastrointestinal Issues: Nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, abdominal pain, or cramping.
  • Respiratory Symptoms: Wheezing, shortness of breath, coughing, nasal congestion, or difficulty swallowing.
  • Cardiovascular Signs (Anaphylaxis): Dizziness, lightheadedness, fainting, a sudden drop in blood pressure, or a weak, rapid pulse.
  • Oral Allergy Syndrome: Itching or tingling in the mouth after eating certain raw fruits, vegetables, or nuts, often associated with pollen allergies.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Strict Allergen Avoidance: Meticulously avoid all identified food allergens in the child's diet and environment.
  • Label Reading: Always read food labels carefully for allergen statements and 'may contain' warnings, as ingredients can change.
  • Cross-Contamination Prevention: Use separate utensils, cutting boards, and cooking surfaces to prevent allergen transfer during food preparation.
  • Emergency Action Plan: Develop and share a written emergency action plan with all caregivers, including schools, daycares, and family members.
  • Epinephrine Auto-Injector (EpiPen): Ensure the child always has two prescribed epinephrine auto-injectors readily available and that caregivers know how to use them.
  • Inform All Caregivers: Educate teachers, school nurses, babysitters, and family members about the child's allergies, symptoms, and emergency procedures.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Elimination of Allergens: Strictly remove all confirmed allergenic foods from the child's diet. This is the cornerstone of management.
  • Nutrient Replacement: Consult with a registered dietitian to ensure the child receives adequate nutrition if major food groups are restricted.
  • Safe Food Choices: Focus on naturally allergen-free foods and products specifically labeled as free from the child's allergens.
  • Dining Out Safely: Communicate clearly with restaurant staff about the child's allergies and potential for cross-contamination.
  • Introducing New Foods: For infants at risk, discuss with a pediatrician when and how to introduce potentially allergenic foods, often under medical guidance.
  • Ingredient Scrutiny: Be vigilant about hidden allergens in processed foods, sauces, and baked goods.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Recognize Anaphylaxis: Be aware that anaphylaxis can progress rapidly and may not always present with all symptoms. Any two body systems affected (e.g., skin and respiratory) or a sudden drop in blood pressure warrants immediate action.
  • Administer Epinephrine Promptly: If anaphylaxis is suspected, administer the epinephrine auto-injector without delay. It is the first-line treatment.
  • Call Emergency Services: After administering epinephrine, always call 911 (or local emergency number) immediately, even if symptoms improve.
  • Positioning: Lay the child flat with legs elevated if they are dizzy or faint. If they are having breathing difficulties, allow them to sit up.
  • Monitor Closely: Observe the child for at least 4-6 hours after an allergic reaction, as a biphasic reaction (a second wave of symptoms) can occur.
  • Medical Alert Identification: Encourage the child to wear a medical alert bracelet or necklace detailing their allergies.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Accurate Diagnosis: Diagnosis typically involves a detailed history, skin prick tests, and/or specific IgE blood tests. Oral food challenges may be performed under strict medical supervision to confirm or rule out an allergy.
  • Management Plan: A comprehensive management plan includes strict allergen avoidance, an emergency action plan, and education for the family and caregivers.
  • Epinephrine Prescription: Prescribe and educate on the proper use of epinephrine auto-injectors for all patients at risk of anaphylaxis.
  • Referral to Allergist: Refer children with suspected or confirmed food allergies to a board-certified allergist for specialized care, diagnosis, and ongoing management.
  • Regular Follow-ups: Schedule regular follow-up appointments to monitor the child's allergy, review the action plan, and discuss potential for outgrowing allergies.
  • Emerging Therapies: Discuss emerging therapies like oral immunotherapy (OIT) with families, explaining risks and benefits, and referring to specialists when appropriate.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Patient Education: Educate parents and children on allergen identification, label reading, cross-contamination prevention, and the importance of carrying emergency medication.
  • Emergency Response: Be proficient in recognizing anaphylaxis, administering epinephrine, and initiating emergency protocols in school or clinical settings.
  • School Health Plans: Collaborate with families and physicians to develop and implement individualized healthcare plans (IHPs) and emergency care plans (ECPs) for students with food allergies.
  • Medication Management: Ensure proper storage, accessibility, and expiration date monitoring of epinephrine auto-injectors in school and daycare settings.
  • Dietary Counseling: Provide basic guidance on safe food choices and refer families to registered dietitians for comprehensive nutritional planning.
  • Advocacy: Advocate for a safe and inclusive environment for children with food allergies in schools and community settings.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • IgE-Mediated Reactions: Type I hypersensitivity reactions involving IgE antibodies binding to mast cells and basophils, leading to histamine and mediator release.
  • Non-IgE Mediated Reactions: Delayed reactions often involving T-cells, such as Food Protein-Induced Enterocolitis Syndrome (FPIES) or allergic proctocolitis.
  • Anaphylaxis Pathophysiology: A severe, systemic allergic reaction characterized by rapid onset, involving multiple organ systems, and potentially leading to shock and death.
  • Diagnostic Modalities: Skin prick tests (SPT), specific IgE blood tests (RAST/ImmunoCAP), oral food challenges (OFC), and component-resolved diagnostics (CRD).
  • Oral Immunotherapy (OIT): A desensitization treatment involving controlled, increasing doses of the allergenic food to build tolerance, performed under strict medical supervision.
  • Epinephrine Mechanism: Alpha-1 adrenergic effects (vasoconstriction, increased blood pressure), Beta-1 adrenergic effects (increased heart rate), and Beta-2 adrenergic effects (bronchodilation, decreased mediator release).
