Smoke inhalation

🏠 Home / Children's health / Injuries, first aid, and CPR for children

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Children's Vulnerability: Children are particularly susceptible to smoke inhalation injury due to smaller airways, higher respiratory rates, and less developed physiological reserves.
  • Toxic Components: Smoke contains various toxic gases (e.g., carbon monoxide, hydrogen cyanide) and particulate matter that can cause systemic poisoning and direct airway damage.
  • Rapid Onset: Airway obstruction and respiratory distress can develop rapidly due to thermal injury, chemical irritation, and inflammation of the respiratory tract.
  • Delayed Effects: Pulmonary complications like ARDS, pneumonia, and bronchiolitis obliterans can manifest hours to days after initial exposure.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Respiratory Distress: Coughing, wheezing, stridor (high-pitched breathing sound), hoarseness, rapid breathing, and difficulty breathing are common.
  • Airway Soot: Presence of soot around the nose or mouth, singed nasal hairs, or carbonaceous sputum indicates direct smoke exposure.
  • Neurological Changes: Headache, dizziness, nausea, vomiting, confusion, lethargy, or loss of consciousness can signal carbon monoxide or cyanide poisoning.
  • Skin Findings: Cherry-red skin color (in severe CO poisoning, though often subtle), or burns on the face, neck, or torso may accompany smoke inhalation.
  • Eye Irritation: Red, watery eyes, or corneal abrasions from smoke particles.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Immediate Evacuation: Promptly remove the child from the smoke-filled environment to fresh air.
  • Emergency Services: Call emergency medical services (e.g., 911) immediately, even if symptoms appear mild, as internal injuries may not be visible.
  • Airway Protection: Be prepared for potential airway swelling and obstruction; do not attempt to induce vomiting.
  • Monitor for Deterioration: Closely observe the child for any worsening of respiratory symptoms, changes in consciousness, or signs of burns.
  • Fire Safety: Ensure working smoke detectors on every level of the home and practice a family fire escape plan.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Initial NPO Status: Maintain nothing by mouth (NPO) status initially due to the risk of aspiration from potential airway swelling or altered mental status.
  • Intravenous Fluids: Hydration will likely be managed via intravenous fluids to support circulation and organ function, especially if burns are present.
  • Gradual Reintroduction: Once the airway is stable, mental status is clear, and swallowing is assessed as safe, oral intake may be gradually reintroduced, starting with clear liquids.
  • Soft Diet Progression: If pharyngeal or laryngeal irritation is significant, a soft, non-irritating diet may be recommended before advancing to regular foods.
  • Avoid Irritants: Avoid acidic, spicy, or very hot foods and drinks that could further irritate an inflamed airway or esophagus during recovery.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Secure Airway: Prioritize airway assessment and management; be ready to assist with breathing or perform CPR if necessary.
  • Administer Oxygen: Provide 100% high-flow oxygen via a non-rebreather mask or bag-valve mask as soon as possible.
  • Monitor Vitals: Continuously monitor heart rate, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, and level of consciousness.
  • Remove Contaminated Clothing: Carefully remove any clothing that may be contaminated with smoke or chemicals to prevent further exposure.
  • Emotional Support: Provide calm reassurance and comfort to the child, as the experience can be very frightening.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Comprehensive Assessment: A thorough evaluation including respiratory, cardiovascular, and neurological systems is crucial, along with assessment for associated burns.
  • Oxygen Therapy: High-flow oxygen is the cornerstone of treatment for smoke inhalation, particularly for carbon monoxide poisoning.
  • Airway Management: Early intubation may be indicated for signs of impending airway obstruction, severe respiratory distress, or altered mental status.
  • Bronchodilators & Nebulizers: May be used to manage bronchospasm and reduce airway inflammation.
  • Monitoring for Complications: Vigilant monitoring for acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), pneumonia, and cardiac arrhythmias is essential.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Pathophysiology: Understand the mechanisms of injury including cellular hypoxia from CO, direct thermal injury, and chemical irritation leading to inflammation and pulmonary edema.
  • Nursing Assessments: Focus on frequent respiratory assessments (rate, effort, breath sounds, stridor), neurological status (GCS, pupil response), and skin integrity.
  • Interventions: Administer prescribed oxygen, maintain IV access, prepare for intubation, administer nebulized medications, and manage pain.
  • Patient Education: Educate parents on signs of respiratory distress, importance of follow-up, and fire prevention strategies.
  • Documentation: Meticulously document all assessments, interventions, and the child's response to treatment.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Carbon Monoxide (CO) Poisoning: A leading cause of death in fires, CO binds to hemoglobin with higher affinity than oxygen, causing tissue hypoxia.
  • Cyanide Poisoning: Can occur from combustion of plastics and synthetic materials, inhibiting cellular respiration.
  • Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS): A severe lung injury characterized by widespread inflammation and fluid accumulation in the lungs.
  • Carboxyhemoglobin (COHb): The compound formed when carbon monoxide binds to hemoglobin, measured via CO-oximetry.
  • Bronchoscopy: A procedure to visualize the airways, assess injury severity, and remove debris.
  • Hyperbaric Oxygen Therapy (HBOT): May be indicated for severe CO poisoning or specific neurological symptoms.
