Chest trauma

🏠 Home / Children's health / Injuries, first aid, and CPR for children

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Pediatric Vulnerability: Children's ribs are more pliable and their mediastinum more mobile, increasing the risk of significant internal organ injury (lungs, heart, great vessels) even with minimal external signs of trauma.
  • Common Mechanisms: Falls, motor vehicle accidents (especially as unrestrained passengers), sports injuries, and non-accidental trauma are frequent causes of chest injuries in children.
  • Respiratory Compromise: Chest trauma can rapidly lead to respiratory distress due to lung contusion, pneumothorax, hemothorax, or airway obstruction, demanding immediate attention.
  • Hidden Dangers: Internal bleeding or cardiac injury may not be immediately apparent, requiring a high index of suspicion and thorough assessment.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Respiratory Distress: Rapid breathing (tachypnea), difficulty breathing (dyspnea), grunting, nasal flaring, intercostal or subcostal retractions, and cyanosis.
  • Chest Pain and Tenderness: Localized pain, tenderness to palpation, or guarding of the chest wall.
  • Altered Breath Sounds: Diminished or absent breath sounds on one side, crackles, wheezing, or muffled heart sounds.
  • Circulatory Instability: Rapid heart rate (tachycardia), low blood pressure (hypotension), pallor, cool extremities, and altered mental status (irritability, lethargy).
  • Visible Injuries: Bruising, abrasions, lacerations, paradoxical chest wall movement (flail chest), or subcutaneous emphysema (crepitus under the skin).
  • Behavioral Changes: Unexplained irritability, lethargy, refusal to move, or unusual quietness in an infant or young child.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Immediate Medical Evaluation: Any child with suspected chest trauma requires urgent medical assessment, ideally at a pediatric trauma center.
  • Spinal Immobilization: Assume potential cervical spine injury in significant trauma until medically cleared, maintaining in-line stabilization during assessment and transport.
  • Airway Management: Ensure a patent airway; be prepared for suctioning or assisted ventilation if respiratory distress is severe.
  • Oxygen Supplementation: Administer high-flow oxygen via a non-rebreather mask or bag-valve mask as indicated to maintain adequate oxygen saturation.
  • Avoid Pressure on Open Wounds: Do not apply direct pressure to open chest wounds unless specifically instructed for hemorrhage control, as this can worsen pneumothorax.
  • Continuous Monitoring: Closely monitor vital signs, oxygen saturation, respiratory effort, and level of consciousness for any changes.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • NPO Status: Assume nothing by mouth (NPO) immediately upon presentation due to the high likelihood of requiring emergency surgery, intubation, or sedation.
  • Intravenous Fluids: Hydration and electrolyte balance will be maintained via intravenous fluid administration as directed by the medical team.
  • Oral Fluid Restriction: Avoid offering any oral fluids or food until the child is stable, awake, alert, and medical staff have cleared oral intake.
  • Gradual Reintroduction: Once cleared, oral intake should be gradually reintroduced, typically starting with clear liquids, then advancing to a soft diet as tolerated.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Call Emergency Services: Immediately call 911 or your local emergency number if a child sustains chest trauma.
  • Do Not Move the Child: Unless the child is in immediate danger (e.g., active fire, unsafe location), avoid moving them to prevent exacerbating potential spinal or internal injuries.
  • Maintain Airway: If the child is conscious, encourage them to maintain a position that keeps their airway open (e.g., sitting upright if tolerated and no spinal injury suspected).
  • Control External Bleeding: Apply direct, firm pressure with a clean cloth to any external bleeding wounds on the chest, if present and safe to do so.
  • Observe and Report: Note any changes in the child's breathing, skin color, level of consciousness, or new symptoms and report them to emergency responders.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Rapid Assessment is Key: A systematic primary and secondary survey (following ATLS principles) is crucial for identifying life-threatening injuries quickly in pediatric chest trauma.
  • Imaging Modalities: Chest X-ray, focused assessment with sonography for trauma (FAST exam), and CT scan are vital tools for diagnosing specific injuries like pneumothorax, hemothorax, pulmonary contusion, or diaphragmatic rupture.
  • Aggressive Pain Management: Adequate pain control is essential to improve respiratory mechanics and prevent atelectasis and pneumonia.
  • Ventilatory Support: Many children with significant chest trauma will require ventilatory support, ranging from supplemental oxygen to mechanical ventilation.
  • Consider Non-Accidental Trauma: Always maintain a high index of suspicion for child abuse, especially in cases with inconsistent histories, unexplained injuries, or specific injury patterns.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Age-Specific Assessment: Adapt assessment techniques and vital sign parameters to the child's age and developmental stage.
  • Respiratory Monitoring: Closely monitor respiratory rate, effort, oxygen saturation, and breath sounds; recognize subtle signs of respiratory decompensation.
  • Pain Assessment: Utilize age-appropriate pain scales (e.g., FLACC, Wong-Baker FACES) to accurately assess and manage pain.
  • Chest Tube Management: Understand the principles of chest tube insertion, maintenance, and troubleshooting for pneumothorax or hemothorax.
  • Family-Centered Care: Provide clear, empathetic communication and emotional support to the child and their family during a highly stressful time.
  • Fluid Resuscitation: Administer intravenous fluids cautiously, monitoring for signs of fluid overload, especially in children with pulmonary contusions.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Flail Chest: Segment of the chest wall that moves paradoxically with respiration due to multiple rib fractures, often requiring mechanical ventilation.
  • Pneumothorax: Air in the pleural space, leading to lung collapse; can be simple, open, or tension pneumothorax.
  • Hemothorax: Blood in the pleural space, often requiring chest tube drainage and potentially blood transfusion.
  • Pulmonary Contusion: Bruising of the lung tissue, leading to impaired gas exchange and potential for acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS).
  • Diaphragmatic Rupture: Tear in the diaphragm, allowing abdominal contents to herniate into the chest cavity, impairing lung function.
  • Cardiac Contusion: Bruising of the heart muscle, potentially leading to arrhythmias, heart failure, or pericardial effusion.
  • ATLS Guidelines: Advanced Trauma Life Support protocols provide a standardized approach to the assessment and management of trauma patients, including children.
