Topic

Removing objects stuck up the nose

🏠 Home / Children's health / Injuries, first aid, and CPR for children

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Common Childhood Incident: Small objects stuck in a child's nose are a frequent occurrence, especially in toddlers and preschoolers.
  • Variety of Objects: Common items include beads, small toy parts, food items (e.g., peas, beans), erasers, or small stones.
  • Potential Complications: If not removed, objects can cause local irritation, inflammation, infection, foul-smelling discharge, or, rarely, aspiration into the airway.
  • Prompt Attention Required: Early identification and safe removal are crucial to prevent discomfort and complications.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Unilateral Nasal Discharge: Often thick, discolored (yellow/green), and may have a foul odor from one nostril.
  • Nasal Obstruction: Difficulty breathing through the affected nostril, leading to mouth breathing.
  • Nasal Pain or Irritation: Child may complain of pain, rub their nose, or show signs of discomfort.
  • Epistaxis (Nosebleed): Especially if the object is sharp or has caused trauma to the nasal lining.
  • Repeated Sneezing: The body's attempt to expel the foreign object.
  • Irritability or Restlessness: Due to discomfort and difficulty breathing.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Do NOT Probe Blindly: Never use tweezers, cotton swabs, or fingers to try and remove the object, as this can push it further in or cause injury.
  • Avoid Forceful Blowing: Instructing a child to blow their nose too hard can potentially dislodge the object into the throat, risking aspiration.
  • Button Battery Emergency: If the object is suspected to be a button battery, seek immediate emergency medical attention. These can cause severe tissue damage rapidly.
  • Maintain Calm: Keep the child as calm as possible; agitation can make removal more difficult and increase the risk of aspiration.
  • Observe for Respiratory Distress: Watch for signs like rapid breathing, wheezing, or cyanosis, which indicate a potential airway compromise requiring urgent medical care.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Avoid Oral Intake Prior to Removal: Do not give the child food or drink until the object is safely removed, especially if medical intervention (e.g., sedation) might be required.
  • Risk of Aspiration: Eating or drinking could potentially dislodge the object into the airway, leading to choking or aspiration.
  • Post-Removal Hydration: Once the object is successfully removed and the child is stable, offer small sips of water or clear fluids to ensure hydration.
  • Gradual Food Reintroduction: After a period of observation and if the child is comfortable, gradually reintroduce soft, easy-to-swallow foods.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Attempt "Parent's Kiss" Technique (If Cooperative): For a cooperative child, block the unaffected nostril, place your mouth over the child's mouth, and deliver a short, sharp puff of air. This can sometimes dislodge the object.
  • Seek Professional Medical Help: If the object cannot be easily removed with the "parent's kiss" technique, if it's a battery, or if the child is distressed, seek immediate medical attention.
  • Keep Child Upright: Encourage the child to remain in an upright position to minimize the risk of the object moving further into the nasal passage or throat.
  • Do Not Panic: Your calm demeanor will help keep the child calm, which is crucial for safe management.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Specialized Removal Techniques: Physicians utilize specific instruments (e.g., alligator forceps, suction, balloon catheters) and techniques for safe and effective foreign body removal.
  • Assessment for Complications: A thorough examination will be performed to assess for mucosal injury, infection, or septal perforation.
  • Sedation for Uncooperative Children: For very young or uncooperative children, mild sedation may be necessary to ensure a safe and atraumatic removal procedure.
  • Post-Removal Care: Advise parents on monitoring for signs of infection, persistent discharge, or irritation and when to seek follow-up care.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Patient and Parent Education: Nurses are vital in educating parents about prevention strategies for foreign body insertions and appropriate first aid.
  • Assessment and Triage: Skillful assessment of the child's respiratory status and the nature of the foreign body is crucial for appropriate triage and intervention.
  • Comfort and Support: Providing a calm and reassuring environment for both the child and parents during the assessment and removal process.
  • Assisting with Procedures: Nurses assist physicians during foreign body removal, ensuring proper positioning, equipment readiness, and child safety.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Foreign Body Removal Modalities: Includes positive pressure technique (parent's kiss), instrumentation (forceps, suction), and balloon catheter extraction.
  • Differential Diagnosis: Distinguishing nasal foreign bodies from acute rhinitis, sinusitis, nasal polyps, or choanal atresia.
  • Potential Complications: Localized infection (rhinosinusitis), septal perforation, aspiration, epistaxis, and pressure necrosis (especially with button batteries).
  • Button Battery Protocol: Specific guidelines for urgent removal within 2 hours due to rapid liquefaction necrosis and potential for severe tissue damage.
