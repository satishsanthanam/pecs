Opioid withdrawal – ED discharge instructions

🏠 Home / Children's health / Substance use and misuse

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Understanding Opioid Withdrawal: Opioid withdrawal occurs when a child who has been physically dependent on opioids suddenly stops or significantly reduces their use. It is not life-threatening but can be extremely uncomfortable and distressing.
  • Importance of Medical Supervision: While discharged, continued monitoring and follow-up with a healthcare provider are crucial to manage symptoms safely and prevent complications.
  • Risk of Relapse and Overdose: Children experiencing withdrawal are at a very high risk of relapse. If they use opioids again after a period of abstinence, their tolerance will be lower, significantly increasing the risk of accidental overdose.
  • Not a Moral Failing: Opioid dependence is a medical condition, and withdrawal is a physiological response. Support and understanding are vital for the child's recovery journey.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Gastrointestinal Distress: Nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and abdominal cramping are common and can lead to dehydration.
  • Flu-like Symptoms: Muscle aches, joint pain, sweating, chills, runny nose, and excessive yawning may be present.
  • Autonomic Hyperactivity: Dilated pupils, goosebumps (piloerection), increased heart rate, and elevated blood pressure can occur.
  • Psychological and Behavioral Changes: Irritability, agitation, anxiety, restlessness, difficulty sleeping (insomnia), and dysphoria are frequently observed.
  • Cravings: Intense cravings for opioids can be a significant challenge, increasing the risk of relapse.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Secure All Medications: Ensure all prescribed medications are stored securely, out of reach of the child and other household members, to prevent misuse or accidental ingestion.
  • Remove Access to Illicit Substances: Thoroughly search the home and remove any illicit opioids or other substances to eliminate temptation and prevent relapse.
  • Monitor for Worsening Symptoms: Closely observe the child for any signs of severe dehydration, respiratory distress, or significant changes in mental status, which warrant immediate medical attention.
  • Avoid Self-Medication: Do not give the child any over-the-counter medications or herbal remedies without consulting a healthcare provider, as some may interact with prescribed treatments or worsen symptoms.
  • Overdose Prevention Education: Educate all caregivers and household members on recognizing the signs of opioid overdose (e.g., pinpoint pupils, slow/shallow breathing, unresponsiveness) and the proper use of naloxone, if prescribed.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Maintain Hydration: Encourage frequent sips of clear fluids such as water, oral rehydration solutions (e.g., Pedialyte), diluted fruit juices, or clear broths to prevent dehydration from vomiting and diarrhea.
  • Bland, Easily Digestible Foods: Offer small, frequent meals of bland foods like toast, crackers, rice, bananas, applesauce, and plain pasta. Avoid spicy, fatty, or heavily seasoned foods.
  • Avoid Caffeine and Sugary Drinks: Limit or avoid caffeinated beverages and highly sugary drinks, which can exacerbate gastrointestinal upset or anxiety.
  • Nutritional Support: If the child is unable to tolerate solid foods, focus on liquid nutrition and consult with a healthcare provider or dietitian for guidance on maintaining adequate caloric intake.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Administer Prescribed Medications: Follow the healthcare provider's instructions precisely for all prescribed medications (e.g., clonidine, buprenorphine, antiemetics) to manage withdrawal symptoms.
  • Create a Safe and Supportive Environment: Ensure the child is in a calm, quiet, and safe environment free from stressors and triggers that might prompt opioid use.
  • Monitor for Respiratory Depression: If buprenorphine or other sedating medications are prescribed, monitor for signs of respiratory depression (slow, shallow breathing, excessive drowsiness) and seek immediate medical help if observed.
  • Emergency Contact Plan: Have a clear plan for who to call and where to go in case of an emergency, including the healthcare provider's contact information and the nearest emergency department.
  • Encourage Open Communication: Foster an environment where the child feels comfortable discussing their feelings and challenges without judgment.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Comprehensive Follow-up is Paramount: Discharge instructions are only the first step. Immediate and consistent follow-up with a pediatrician, addiction specialist, or mental health professional is critical for long-term recovery.
  • Address Underlying Issues: Opioid use in children often stems from underlying mental health conditions (e.g., anxiety, depression, trauma) or social determinants. A holistic approach addressing these factors is essential.
  • Consider Medication-Assisted Treatment (MAT): For some adolescents, medication-assisted treatment with buprenorphine/naloxone may be an appropriate and effective strategy to reduce cravings and prevent relapse.
  • Family Involvement: Engaging the family in therapy and support groups is crucial, as substance use impacts the entire family system.
  • Harm Reduction Strategies: Educate families on harm reduction, including the availability and proper use of naloxone, to prevent fatal overdose.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Pediatric COWS Scale Adaptation: Utilize an age-appropriate adaptation of the Clinical Opiate Withdrawal Scale (COWS) to objectively assess withdrawal severity and guide symptom management.
  • Comfort Measures: Implement non-pharmacological comfort measures such as warm baths, gentle massage, quiet environment, and distraction techniques to alleviate distress.
  • Patient and Family Education: Provide clear, concise education on medication administration, expected withdrawal symptoms, warning signs requiring immediate medical attention, and the importance of follow-up appointments.
  • Referral Pathways: Facilitate seamless referrals to outpatient addiction treatment programs, mental health services, and social support agencies tailored for adolescents.
  • Fluid and Electrolyte Monitoring: Closely monitor for signs of dehydration and electrolyte imbalances, especially in younger children experiencing significant vomiting or diarrhea.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Pharmacological Management: Common medications used include alpha-2 adrenergic agonists (e.g., clonidine) to reduce autonomic symptoms, antiemetics for nausea/vomiting, and NSAIDs for muscle aches. Buprenorphine may be initiated in appropriate cases.
  • Differential Diagnosis: Differentiate opioid withdrawal from other conditions presenting with similar symptoms, such as gastroenteritis, anxiety disorders, or other substance withdrawals.
  • Risk Factors for Severe Withdrawal: Higher doses, longer duration of opioid use, co-occurring mental health conditions, and polysubstance use can increase the severity of withdrawal.
  • Toxicology Screening: Urine drug screens are essential at presentation to confirm opioid use and identify co-ingestions, guiding appropriate management.
  • Naloxone Prescription: Consider prescribing naloxone to the family upon discharge, along with comprehensive education on its use, especially if there is a risk of relapse.
