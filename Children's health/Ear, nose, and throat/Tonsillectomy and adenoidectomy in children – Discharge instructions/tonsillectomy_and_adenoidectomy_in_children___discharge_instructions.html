Tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy in children – Discharge instructions

🏠 Home / Children's health / Ear, nose, and throat

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Recovery Period: Expect a recovery period of 10-14 days, during which your child will experience discomfort.
  • Pain Management is Key: Consistent pain medication administration is crucial for your child's comfort, hydration, and healing.
  • Hydration Priority: Maintaining adequate fluid intake is the most important aspect of post-operative care to prevent dehydration and aid healing.
  • Activity Restrictions: Strenuous activities and rough play must be avoided to minimize the risk of bleeding.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Sore Throat & Difficulty Swallowing: This is expected and will be most severe for the first few days, gradually improving.
  • Ear Pain: Referred pain to the ears is common, especially when swallowing, and does not necessarily indicate an ear infection.
  • Low-Grade Fever: A temperature up to 101°F (38.3°C) is common for the first 2-3 days post-surgery.
  • Bad Breath: This is normal due to the healing scabs in the throat and usually resolves as healing progresses.
  • Nausea & Vomiting: May occur in the first 24 hours due to anesthesia or swallowed blood; report persistent vomiting.
  • Snoring & Nasal Congestion: May improve over time as swelling subsides, but some snoring can persist temporarily.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Monitor for Bleeding: Watch for any bright red blood from the mouth or nose. Small streaks are common, but persistent or significant bleeding requires immediate medical attention.
  • Prevent Dehydration: Offer fluids frequently, even small amounts, to ensure continuous hydration. Signs of dehydration include decreased urination, dry mouth, and lethargy.
  • Avoid Irritating Foods: Do not give spicy, acidic, crunchy, or very hot foods, as these can irritate the surgical site and potentially cause bleeding.
  • Limit Activity: Restrict strenuous activities, running, jumping, and contact sports for at least two weeks post-surgery.
  • No Straws: Avoid using straws, as the sucking motion can dislodge scabs and increase the risk of bleeding.
  • Avoid Smoking Environments: Ensure your child is in a smoke-free environment to prevent irritation and promote healing.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Clear, Cool Liquids First: Start with sips of water, clear broth, diluted juice, or popsicles immediately post-op.
  • Soft, Bland Foods: Gradually introduce soft, non-acidic foods like mashed potatoes, yogurt, applesauce, scrambled eggs, and soft pasta.
  • Avoid Citrus & Acidic Juices: Orange juice, lemonade, and tomato products can sting the throat.
  • No Crunchy or Hard Foods: Chips, crackers, toast, and hard candies are strictly forbidden as they can scratch the healing tissue.
  • Dairy Products: While not strictly forbidden, dairy can thicken mucus, which some children find uncomfortable. Offer in moderation if tolerated.
  • Frequent Small Offerings: Encourage frequent, small amounts of fluids and soft foods rather than large meals.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Strict Medication Schedule: Administer pain medication around the clock as prescribed, even if the child seems comfortable, to stay ahead of the pain.
  • Observe for Bleeding: Continuously monitor for signs of fresh bleeding. If your child vomits dark brown or coffee-ground material, it may indicate old blood, but bright red blood is an emergency.
  • Encourage Fluid Intake: Make fluids readily available and encourage sips every 15-30 minutes while awake.
  • Maintain a Calm Environment: Keep your child rested and calm to aid healing and reduce the risk of complications.
  • Know When to Call: Contact your surgeon immediately for bright red bleeding, difficulty breathing, persistent high fever (over 102°F/38.9°C), or signs of severe dehydration.
  • Follow-up Appointment: Ensure you attend the scheduled post-operative follow-up appointment.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Pain Management is Paramount: Effective pain control is critical not only for comfort but also to ensure the child can swallow and maintain hydration, which directly impacts healing and prevents complications.
  • Risk of Post-Operative Hemorrhage: The highest risk of bleeding is within the first 24 hours and again around 5-10 days post-op when scabs naturally detach. Vigilant monitoring is essential.
  • Dehydration Complications: Dehydration can lead to increased pain, delayed healing, and potential re-hospitalization. Aggressive fluid intake is a non-negotiable aspect of recovery.
  • Airway Patency: While rare, swelling can sometimes compromise the airway. Any signs of difficulty breathing, stridor, or excessive drooling warrant immediate evaluation.
  • Gradual Return to Normal: Emphasize a slow, progressive return to normal diet and activity to prevent setbacks and ensure complete healing.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Post-Anesthesia Care Unit (PACU) Assessment: Focus on airway patency, respiratory effort, pain level, and initial signs of bleeding (e.g., frequent swallowing, blood-tinged secretions).
  • Discharge Teaching Reinforcement: Systematically review all discharge instructions with parents/guardians, using teach-back methods to ensure comprehension, especially regarding pain management and bleeding precautions.
  • Hydration Status Monitoring: Educate parents on signs of dehydration in children (e.g., dry mucous membranes, decreased urine output, sunken fontanelle in infants, lethargy).
  • Pain Assessment Tools: Utilize age-appropriate pain scales (e.g., FLACC for non-verbal children, Wong-Baker FACES for verbal children) to accurately assess and manage post-operative pain.
  • Emergency Protocol Education: Clearly outline when and how to contact the surgical team or seek emergency care for complications like hemorrhage or respiratory distress.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Post-Tonsillectomy Hemorrhage (PTH): Defined as bleeding requiring intervention after tonsillectomy. Primary PTH occurs within 24 hours; secondary PTH occurs 24 hours to 10-14 days post-op. Incidence ranges from 0.1% to 8%.
  • Pain Management Regimen: Typically involves a combination of acetaminophen and ibuprofen, often staggered. Opioids (e.g., hydrocodone) may be prescribed for severe pain, with careful consideration of respiratory depression risk in children.
  • Dehydration Assessment: Clinical signs include tachycardia, prolonged capillary refill time, decreased skin turgor, and altered mental status. Laboratory markers (e.g., elevated BUN/creatinine ratio) may support diagnosis in severe cases.
  • Airway Obstruction: Can be caused by post-operative edema, hematoma, or aspiration. Signs include stridor, retractions, tachypnea, and cyanosis. Requires immediate medical intervention, potentially re-intubation.
  • Scab Sloughing: The white/yellowish patches in the tonsillar fossae are fibrin and healing tissue, not infection. Scabs typically detach around 5-10 days post-op, which is a period of increased bleeding risk.
