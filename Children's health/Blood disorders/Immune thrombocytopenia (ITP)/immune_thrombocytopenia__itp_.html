Immune thrombocytopenia (ITP)

🏠 Home / Children's health / Blood disorders

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Immune Thrombocytopenia (ITP): An autoimmune disorder where the body's immune system mistakenly attacks and destroys its own platelets, leading to a low platelet count.
  • Childhood Onset: Often presents acutely in children, frequently following a viral infection, and is the most common cause of sudden low platelet count in otherwise healthy children.
  • Platelet Function: Platelets are crucial for blood clotting; a low count increases the risk of bleeding and bruising.
  • Acute vs. Chronic: Most childhood ITP cases are acute and resolve spontaneously within 6-12 months; chronic ITP persists beyond 12 months.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Petechiae: Tiny, pinpoint red or purple spots on the skin, often appearing on the lower legs, not blanching under pressure.
  • Purpura/Ecchymoses: Larger bruises (purple or blue patches) that appear easily or are disproportionate to minor trauma.
  • Mucosal Bleeding: Nosebleeds (epistaxis) that are frequent or difficult to stop, and bleeding from the gums, especially during brushing.
  • Fatigue: May be present, particularly in chronic ITP or if significant bleeding leads to anemia.
  • Internal Bleeding (Rare): Signs such as blood in urine (hematuria), blood in stool (melena), or severe headaches and neurological changes (suggesting intracranial hemorrhage) require immediate medical attention.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Injury Prevention: Avoid contact sports, rough play, and activities with a high risk of falls or head trauma, especially when platelet counts are very low.
  • Medication Avoidance: Do not administer aspirin, ibuprofen (NSAIDs), or other medications that can inhibit platelet function without consulting a physician.
  • Bleeding Management: Apply firm, direct pressure to any cuts or bleeding sites for several minutes. Use a soft-bristle toothbrush to prevent gum irritation.
  • Vaccinations: Discuss with the healthcare provider; subcutaneous injections are generally preferred over intramuscular, and platelet counts may need to be considered.
  • Emergency Signs: Seek immediate medical care for any signs of severe bleeding, significant head injury, persistent severe headache, or changes in consciousness.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Soft Foods: Encourage a diet of soft, non-irritating foods if the child experiences oral petechiae or gum bleeding to prevent further trauma.
  • Hydration: Ensure adequate fluid intake, especially if the child has a fever or is feeling unwell, to support overall health.
  • Avoid Hard/Crunchy Foods: Temporarily restrict foods that are hard, crunchy, or sharp (e.g., hard candies, chips) to minimize the risk of oral injury or gum bleeding.
  • Iron-Rich Foods: If chronic bleeding leads to iron deficiency anemia, encourage iron-rich foods (e.g., lean meats, fortified cereals, leafy greens) as advised by the physician.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Regular Monitoring: Adhere strictly to the schedule for blood tests and follow-up appointments to monitor platelet counts and overall condition.
  • Medication Adherence: Administer all prescribed medications (e.g., corticosteroids, IVIG) exactly as directed by the physician, understanding their purpose and potential side effects.
  • School and Caregiver Notification: Inform school nurses, teachers, and other caregivers about the child's ITP diagnosis, potential risks, and emergency contact information.
  • Medical Alert Identification: Consider a medical alert bracelet or necklace for children with severe ITP or those at higher risk of bleeding complications.
  • Dental Care: Inform the dentist about the ITP diagnosis before any dental procedures; prophylactic measures may be necessary to prevent excessive bleeding.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Diagnosis by Exclusion: ITP is diagnosed based on isolated thrombocytopenia (platelet count <100,000/µL) in the absence of other identifiable causes of low platelets.
  • Treatment Philosophy: The primary goal of treatment is to prevent severe bleeding, particularly intracranial hemorrhage, rather than to normalize the platelet count.
  • First-Line Therapies: For significant bleeding or very low platelet counts, corticosteroids (e.g., prednisone) or intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) are common initial treatments.
  • Watchful Waiting: Many children with mild ITP and no significant bleeding may be managed with observation alone, as spontaneous remission is common.
  • Prognosis: The majority of children with acute ITP experience spontaneous resolution within 6-12 months, often without specific treatment.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Comprehensive Assessment: Regularly assess for signs of bleeding (skin, mucous membranes, neurological changes), vital signs, and the child's activity level.
  • Patient and Family Education: Educate parents/guardians on recognizing bleeding signs, injury prevention strategies, medication administration, and when to seek emergency care.
  • Emotional Support: Provide psychological support to children and families, addressing anxieties related to bleeding risk and the chronic nature of the condition.
  • IVIG Administration Protocol: Understand the proper administration techniques for intravenous immunoglobulin, monitoring for infusion reactions (e.g., headache, fever, chills), and post-infusion care.
  • Splenectomy Considerations: Recognize splenectomy as a rare, last-resort treatment option for chronic, severe, refractory ITP in children, understanding its indications and potential complications.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Pathophysiology: Characterized by autoantibodies (primarily IgG) directed against platelet surface glycoproteins (e.g., GPIIb/IIIa), leading to accelerated platelet destruction by macrophages in the spleen.
  • Diagnostic Workup: Complete blood count (CBC) with differential, peripheral blood smear review (to rule out pseudothrombocytopenia or other morphologic abnormalities), and exclusion of other causes of thrombocytopenia. Bone marrow examination is typically reserved for atypical presentations or refractory cases.
  • Treatment Modalities: Corticosteroids (e.g., prednisolone, dexamethasone), IVIG, anti-D immunoglobulin (RhoGAM for Rh-positive patients), and thrombopoietin receptor agonists (TPO-RAs) such as eltrombopag or romiplostim for chronic refractory ITP.
  • Differential Diagnosis: Includes other causes of thrombocytopenia such as viral infections (e.g., HIV, CMV, EBV), drug-induced thrombocytopenia, systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), aplastic anemia, leukemia, myelodysplastic syndromes, and thrombotic microangiopathies (TTP/HUS).
  • Bleeding Severity Scales: Utilize standardized bleeding assessment tools (e.g., WHO bleeding scale, ITP-BAT) to objectively grade bleeding severity and guide treatment decisions.
