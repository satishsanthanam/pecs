Conjunctivitis (pink eye)

🏠 Home / Children's health / Eye problems

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Definition: Conjunctivitis, commonly known as 'pink eye', is an inflammation of the conjunctiva, the transparent membrane lining the eyelid and covering the white part of the eyeball.
  • Common Causes: It is frequently caused by viral infections (often associated with colds), bacterial infections, or allergic reactions. Irritants like smoke or chemicals can also cause it.
  • Contagiousness: Viral and bacterial forms are highly contagious, spreading easily through direct contact with eye secretions or contaminated surfaces.
  • Children's Vulnerability: Children are particularly susceptible due to close contact in schools and daycares, and less developed hygiene habits.
  • Types: Differentiating between viral (watery discharge), bacterial (thick, purulent discharge), and allergic (itchy, watery, often bilateral) types is crucial for appropriate management.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Redness: Noticeable redness in one or both eyes, often described as 'pink' or 'red eye'.
  • Discharge: Watery discharge (viral, allergic), thick yellow or green discharge (bacterial), or crusting of eyelids, especially upon waking.
  • Itching/Burning: Sensation of itching, burning, or grittiness in the eye.
  • Swelling: Mild to moderate swelling of the eyelids.
  • Photophobia: Increased sensitivity to light.
  • Foreign Body Sensation: Feeling as if something is in the eye.
  • Tearing: Excessive tearing, particularly with viral or allergic forms.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Strict Hand Hygiene: Emphasize frequent and thorough hand washing with soap and water, especially after touching the eyes or administering eye drops.
  • Avoid Eye Touching: Instruct children to avoid touching or rubbing their eyes to prevent self-contamination and spread.
  • Separate Personal Items: Use separate towels, washcloths, and pillowcases for the affected child and avoid sharing any personal items.
  • School/Daycare Exclusion: Keep children home from school or daycare until symptoms resolve or for at least 24 hours after starting antibiotic treatment for bacterial conjunctivitis.
  • Contact Lens Discontinuation: If applicable for older children, discontinue contact lens wear immediately and discard current lenses and solution.
  • Allergen Avoidance: For allergic conjunctivitis, identify and minimize exposure to known allergens (e.g., pollen, pet dander, dust mites).

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Hydration Support: Encourage consistent fluid intake, particularly if the child has a fever or other systemic symptoms alongside the eye infection, to support overall well-being.
  • Soft Food Options: Offer easily digestible, non-irritating foods if the child's appetite is reduced due to concurrent illness or discomfort.
  • Prevent Cross-Contamination: Ensure meticulous hand hygiene before and after meals to prevent accidental transfer of pathogens from hands to eyes or to shared food items.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Prompt Medical Evaluation: Seek immediate medical attention for any suspected conjunctivitis, especially in newborns, or if symptoms are severe, vision changes occur, or pain is present.
  • Avoid Self-Medication: Do not use leftover eye drops, over-the-counter remedies, or home remedies without professional medical advice, as this can worsen the condition or delay appropriate treatment.
  • Monitor for Worsening: Watch for signs of worsening symptoms, such as increased pain, significant vision changes, or spread of redness/swelling, and report them to a healthcare provider.
  • Newborn Conjunctivitis: Conjunctivitis in newborns (ophthalmia neonatorum) is a medical emergency that requires urgent diagnosis and treatment to prevent serious complications.
  • Complete Treatment Course: If antibiotics are prescribed, ensure the full course is completed, even if symptoms improve, to prevent recurrence and antibiotic resistance.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Accurate Diagnosis is Key: Differentiating between viral, bacterial, and allergic conjunctivitis is paramount for guiding appropriate treatment and preventing unnecessary antibiotic use.
  • Patient Education: Educate parents thoroughly on the contagious nature of infectious conjunctivitis and the importance of strict hygiene measures to prevent spread within the family and community.
  • Symptomatic Relief: Advise on comfort measures such as cool compresses for viral or allergic types, and warm compresses for bacterial crusting, alongside prescribed medications.
  • Vision Monitoring: Counsel parents to monitor for any changes in vision, severe pain, or corneal involvement, which warrant immediate re-evaluation.
  • Return to School/Daycare: Provide clear guidance on when a child can safely return to group settings to minimize outbreaks.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Comprehensive Assessment: Perform a thorough eye assessment, noting discharge characteristics (serous, mucoid, purulent), conjunctival injection pattern, eyelid edema, and presence of photophobia.
  • Parental Education: Provide detailed instructions to parents on proper eye drop/ointment administration technique, emphasizing hand hygiene before and after, and avoiding contamination of the applicator tip.
  • Infection Control: Implement and reinforce contact precautions in clinical settings for suspected infectious conjunctivitis to prevent nosocomial transmission.
  • Differential Diagnosis Skills: Develop the ability to distinguish between the various etiologies of conjunctivitis based on clinical presentation and patient history.
  • Comfort Interventions: Teach and demonstrate effective comfort measures, such as applying appropriate temperature compresses, to alleviate symptoms and promote healing.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Etiology: Viral (e.g., Adenovirus, Herpes Simplex Virus), Bacterial (e.g., *Staphylococcus aureus*, *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, *Haemophilus influenzae*), Allergic (e.g., pollen, dust mites, animal dander).
  • Pathophysiology: Inflammation of the conjunctiva, leading to vasodilation, edema, and cellular infiltration, resulting in characteristic redness, discharge, and discomfort.
  • Pharmacology: Topical antibiotics (e.g., erythromycin, polymyxin B-trimethoprim, fluoroquinolones) for bacterial; topical antihistamines/mast cell stabilizers (e.g., olopatadine, ketotifen) for allergic; supportive care for viral.
  • Complications: Rarely, corneal involvement (keratitis), corneal ulceration, vision impairment, or spread to other ocular structures if severe or untreated, particularly with bacterial or herpetic forms.
  • ICD-10 Codes: H10.0 (Mucopurulent conjunctivitis), H10.1 (Acute atopic conjunctivitis), H10.3 (Acute unspecified conjunctivitis), H10.4 (Chronic conjunctivitis), H10.5 (Blepharoconjunctivitis), H10.8 (Other conjunctivitis), H10.9 (Unspecified conjunctivitis).
