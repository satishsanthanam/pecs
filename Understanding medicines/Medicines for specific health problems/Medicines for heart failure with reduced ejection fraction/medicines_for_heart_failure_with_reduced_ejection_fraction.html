Medicines for heart failure with reduced ejection fraction

🏠 Home / Understanding medicines / Medicines for specific health problems

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Understanding HFrEF: Heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) means the heart's main pumping chamber (left ventricle) is weakened and cannot pump enough blood to meet the body's needs.
  • Goal of Medications: Medicines for HFrEF aim to improve symptoms, reduce hospitalizations, slow disease progression, and prolong life by optimizing heart function and managing fluid balance.
  • Pillars of Treatment: Treatment typically involves a combination of several drug classes, often referred to as Guideline-Directed Medical Therapy (GDMT), which are titrated to optimal doses.
  • Lifelong Management: HFrEF is a chronic condition requiring consistent medication adherence and regular medical follow-up for effective management.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Shortness of Breath (Dyspnea): Especially with exertion, lying flat (orthopnea), or waking up at night gasping for air (paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea).
  • Fatigue and Weakness: Persistent tiredness and lack of energy, even with minimal activity, due to reduced blood flow to muscles.
  • Swelling (Edema): Accumulation of fluid, commonly in the legs, ankles, feet, and sometimes the abdomen (ascites) or lungs.
  • Rapid or Irregular Heartbeat: Sensations of a racing heart or palpitations, which can be a symptom of worsening heart function or arrhythmias.
  • Persistent Cough or Wheezing: Often accompanied by white or pink blood-tinged phlegm, indicating fluid buildup in the lungs.
  • Weight Gain: Sudden, unexplained weight gain over a few days can signal fluid retention.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Regular Monitoring: Frequent blood tests are essential to check kidney function (creatinine, eGFR) and electrolyte levels (potassium, sodium), especially when starting or adjusting ACE inhibitors, ARBs, ARNIs, MRAs, and diuretics.
  • Blood Pressure and Heart Rate: Monitor blood pressure and heart rate regularly, as many HFrEF medications can cause hypotension (low blood pressure) or bradycardia (slow heart rate).
  • Avoid NSAIDs: Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) like ibuprofen or naproxen can worsen heart failure, increase blood pressure, and impair kidney function, especially when taken with diuretics or ACE inhibitors.
  • Potassium Levels: Be cautious with potassium supplements or high-potassium foods if taking ACE inhibitors, ARBs, ARNIs, or mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs), as these can increase potassium levels.
  • Dizziness/Lightheadedness: Report any significant dizziness or lightheadedness, particularly when standing up, as this may indicate low blood pressure or over-diuresis.
  • Pregnancy and Breastfeeding: Discuss all medications with your doctor if you are pregnant, planning to become pregnant, or breastfeeding, as some HFrEF drugs are contraindicated.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Sodium Restriction: Strictly limit dietary sodium intake to typically less than 2000 mg per day to help manage fluid retention and reduce the workload on the heart. Avoid processed foods, canned goods, and excessive table salt.
  • Fluid Management: Your doctor may recommend a fluid restriction (e.g., 1.5-2 liters per day) if you have significant fluid retention or advanced heart failure. Monitor daily fluid intake carefully.
  • Potassium Awareness: Depending on your medications (e.g., ACE inhibitors, ARBs, MRAs, diuretics), your doctor may advise specific guidance on potassium-rich foods. Some medications can increase potassium, while others can deplete it.
  • Alcohol Moderation: Limit or avoid alcohol consumption, as it can weaken the heart muscle and interact negatively with some medications.
  • Caffeine Intake: Moderate caffeine intake, as excessive amounts can sometimes trigger palpitations or arrhythmias in sensitive individuals.
  • Grapefruit Products: Be aware that grapefruit and grapefruit juice can interact with certain medications, potentially increasing their levels in the blood. Consult your pharmacist or doctor about specific drug interactions.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Medication Adherence: Take all prescribed medications exactly as directed by your healthcare provider. Do not stop or adjust doses without medical consultation, even if you feel better.
  • Daily Weight Monitoring: Weigh yourself daily, preferably at the same time each morning, and report any sudden weight gain (e.g., 2-3 pounds in a day or 5 pounds in a week) to your doctor, as this may indicate fluid retention.
  • Symptom Vigilance: Be alert for worsening symptoms such as increased shortness of breath, swelling, or fatigue. Promptly report any new or worsening symptoms to your healthcare team.
  • Emergency Preparedness: Know when to seek urgent medical attention (e.g., severe chest pain, sudden severe shortness of breath, fainting).
  • Vaccinations: Stay up-to-date with recommended vaccinations, including influenza and pneumococcal vaccines, to prevent infections that can exacerbate heart failure.
  • Lifestyle Integration: Combine medication therapy with recommended lifestyle changes, including regular physical activity (as tolerated), smoking cessation, and stress management.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Personalized Treatment: HFrEF management is highly individualized. Your medication regimen will be tailored based on your specific symptoms, comorbidities, kidney function, and blood pressure.
  • Titration is Key: Many HFrEF medications require careful, gradual dose increases (titration) to reach optimal therapeutic levels while minimizing side effects. Patience and close monitoring are crucial during this process.
  • Guideline-Directed Therapy: We aim to implement Guideline-Directed Medical Therapy (GDMT), which involves specific classes of medications proven to improve outcomes in HFrEF.
  • Shared Decision-Making: It's vital to have an open dialogue about your treatment plan, potential side effects, and any concerns you may have. Your active participation is essential for successful management.
  • Holistic Approach: Medications are a cornerstone, but a comprehensive approach including lifestyle modifications, cardiac rehabilitation, and addressing underlying causes is paramount for long-term success.
  • Regular Follow-ups: Consistent follow-up appointments are critical to monitor your condition, adjust medications as needed, and address any emerging issues.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Drug Classifications: Understand the primary drug classes used in HFrEF: ACE inhibitors/ARBs/ARNIs, Beta-blockers, Mineralocorticoid Receptor Antagonists (MRAs), SGLT2 inhibitors, and Diuretics. Know their mechanisms of action and common side effects.
  • Patient Education: Educate patients on medication names, dosages, administration times, potential side effects, and the importance of adherence. Emphasize daily weight monitoring and symptom recognition.
  • Vital Sign Assessment: Consistently assess blood pressure, heart rate, respiratory rate, and oxygen saturation. Recognize parameters that warrant holding a medication or notifying a physician (e.g., hypotension, bradycardia).
  • Fluid Status Assessment: Perform thorough assessments for signs of fluid overload (e.g., crackles, JVD, peripheral edema, S3 heart sound) and dehydration.
  • Electrolyte Imbalance Recognition: Be vigilant for signs and symptoms of hyperkalemia (with ACEIs, ARBs, MRAs) or hypokalemia (with loop diuretics), and hyponatremia.
  • Medication Reconciliation: Ensure accurate medication reconciliation at every transition of care to prevent errors and optimize therapy.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • GDMT (Guideline-Directed Medical Therapy): The standard of care for HFrEF, typically including an ARNI (or ACEI/ARB), a beta-blocker, an MRA, and an SGLT2 inhibitor, unless contraindicated.
  • Ejection Fraction (EF): A measurement of the percentage of blood leaving the heart each time it contracts. HFrEF is defined as an EF of 40% or less.
  • RAAS Inhibition: Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone System inhibition is a primary target for HFrEF medications (ACEIs, ARBs, ARNIs, MRAs) to reduce vasoconstriction, sodium/water retention, and cardiac remodeling.
  • Beta-Blockers: Specifically carvedilol, metoprolol succinate, and bisoprolol, which reduce sympathetic nervous system activity, heart rate, and improve left ventricular function over time.
  • SGLT2 Inhibitors: (e.g., dapagliflozin, empagliflozin) Originally for diabetes, now proven to reduce cardiovascular death and heart failure hospitalizations in HFrEF patients, regardless of diabetes status.
  • Diuretics: (e.g., furosemide, torsemide) Used for symptomatic relief of congestion by promoting fluid excretion, but do not directly improve long-term mortality in HFrEF.
