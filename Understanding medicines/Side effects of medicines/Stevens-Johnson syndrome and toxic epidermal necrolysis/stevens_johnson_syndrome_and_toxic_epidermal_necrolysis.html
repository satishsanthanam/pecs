Stevens-Johnson syndrome and toxic epidermal necrolysis

🏠 Home / Understanding medicines / Side effects of medicines

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Definition: Stevens-Johnson syndrome (SJS) and toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN) are severe, life-threatening mucocutaneous reactions, most often triggered by medications.
  • Spectrum: SJS involves less than 10% body surface area (BSA) skin detachment, SJS/TEN overlap involves 10-30% BSA, and TEN involves greater than 30% BSA.
  • Pathophysiology: Characterized by widespread apoptosis of keratinocytes, leading to epidermal necrosis and detachment from the dermis.
  • Common Triggers: Frequently caused by certain medications including antibiotics (e.g., sulfonamides), anticonvulsants (e.g., lamotrigine, carbamazepine), NSAIDs (e.g., oxicams), and allopurinol.
  • Prognosis: Both conditions carry significant morbidity and mortality, with TEN having a higher mortality rate due to complications like sepsis and multi-organ failure.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Prodromal Phase: Often begins with non-specific flu-like symptoms such as fever, malaise, myalgia, arthralgia, sore throat, and conjunctivitis, typically 1-3 weeks after drug exposure.
  • Skin Manifestations: Rapid onset of painful, erythematous macules that progress to atypical target lesions, flaccid blisters, and widespread epidermal detachment. Nikolsky's sign (skin sloughing with gentle pressure) is positive.
  • Mucosal Involvement: Severe erosions and ulcerations affecting at least two mucosal sites (oral, ocular, anogenital) are hallmark features and often precede skin lesions.
  • Systemic Symptoms: Patients may develop tachycardia, hypotension, severe pain, dehydration, electrolyte imbalances, acute kidney injury, and respiratory distress.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Early Recognition: Prompt identification of prodromal symptoms and initial skin/mucosal changes is paramount for improving outcomes.
  • Immediate Drug Discontinuation: The single most critical intervention is the immediate and complete withdrawal of the suspected causative drug.
  • Avoid Re-exposure: Patients must be educated to strictly avoid the identified causative drug and chemically related compounds for life.
  • Genetic Screening: Consider HLA-B*1502 screening in patients of Asian descent before initiating carbamazepine to reduce SJS/TEN risk.
  • Allergy Documentation: Meticulously document all drug allergies and adverse reactions in the patient's medical record to prevent future exposure.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Aggressive Hydration: Crucial due to extensive fluid loss from denuded skin and mucosal surfaces; intravenous fluids are often required.
  • Nutritional Support: Many patients require enteral (nasogastric or gastrostomy tube) or parenteral nutrition due to severe oral and esophageal erosions preventing oral intake.
  • Soft, Bland Diet: If oral intake is tolerated, a soft, bland, non-acidic, and non-spicy diet is recommended to minimize irritation to the compromised oral mucosa.
  • Avoid Irritants: Restrict foods and beverages that are hot, acidic, spicy, or have a rough texture, as these can exacerbate oral and esophageal lesions.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Sterile Environment: Manage patients in a specialized burn unit or intensive care unit with strict aseptic techniques to minimize the risk of secondary infections.
  • Wound Care: Apply gentle, non-adhesive dressings to denuded skin areas, similar to burn wound management, to protect the fragile epidermis.
  • Ocular Care: Frequent ophthalmologic consultation and aggressive topical lubrication, corticosteroids, or cyclosporine drops are essential to prevent corneal damage and long-term visual impairment.
  • Pain Management: Implement aggressive pain control strategies, including systemic analgesics and topical anesthetics, due to the severe pain associated with skin and mucosal lesions.
  • Temperature Regulation: Maintain a warm ambient temperature to prevent hypothermia, as the extensive loss of skin barrier impairs thermoregulation.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Diagnosis: Primarily a clinical diagnosis based on characteristic skin lesions, mucosal involvement, and rapid progression. A skin biopsy can confirm full-thickness epidermal necrosis.
  • SCORTEN Score: Utilize the SCORTEN prognostic score to assess disease severity and predict mortality risk based on clinical parameters.
  • Immunomodulation: Systemic corticosteroids, intravenous immunoglobulins (IVIG), or cyclosporine may be considered, but their efficacy remains controversial and timing is critical.
  • Multidisciplinary Care: A collaborative approach involving dermatology, critical care, ophthalmology, nutrition, and pain management specialists is vital for comprehensive care.
  • Long-term Follow-up: Essential to monitor for chronic sequelae, including ocular complications (e.g., dry eyes, symblepharon), skin scarring, dyspigmentation, and nail dystrophies.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Comprehensive Assessment: Perform meticulous skin and mucosal assessments, monitor vital signs, and accurately chart fluid balance.
  • Aseptic Wound Management: Adhere strictly to aseptic techniques during dressing changes and handle fragile skin with extreme gentleness to prevent further damage and infection.
  • Oral Hygiene: Provide frequent, gentle oral care using non-irritating solutions to maintain hygiene and comfort for patients with severe oral erosions.
  • Patient and Family Education: Educate patients and their families thoroughly about the condition, identified causative agents, and the critical importance of lifelong avoidance.
  • Psychological Support: Offer emotional and psychological support to patients and families, addressing the significant distress caused by the disfiguring nature and severe pain of SJS/TEN.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • ICD-10 Codes: L51.1 (Stevens-Johnson syndrome), L51.2 (Toxic epidermal necrolysis), L51.3 (SJS/TEN overlap syndrome).
  • Key Causative Drugs: Sulfonamide antibiotics, anticonvulsants (phenytoin, carbamazepine, lamotrigine), allopurinol, NSAIDs (oxicams), nevirapine.
  • Histopathology: Characterized by full-thickness epidermal necrosis with sparse dermal inflammatory infiltrate and subepidermal blistering.
  • Differential Diagnosis: Includes Staphylococcal scalded skin syndrome (SSSS), erythema multiforme major, generalized bullous fixed drug eruption, and acute generalized exanthematous pustulosis (AGEP).
  • SCORTEN Criteria: Age >40 years, heart rate >120 bpm, presence of malignancy, BSA involved >10%, serum urea >10 mmol/L, serum glucose >14 mmol/L, serum bicarbonate <20 mmol/L.
