How to give a subcutaneous injection

🏠 Home / Understanding medicines / How to take medicines

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Definition: Subcutaneous (SC) injection delivers medication into the fatty tissue layer just beneath the skin.
  • Common Applications: Frequently used for medications requiring slower absorption, such as insulin, heparin, certain vaccines, and epinephrine auto-injectors.
  • Self-Administration: Many SC medications are designed for patient self-administration, requiring proper technique education.
  • Absorption Rate: Offers a slower, more sustained absorption rate compared to intramuscular (IM) or intravenous (IV) routes.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Diabetes Mellitus: Patients with diabetes require daily insulin injections to manage blood glucose levels.
  • Risk of Thrombosis: Individuals at risk for deep vein thrombosis (DVT) may receive prophylactic anticoagulant injections (e.g., heparin, enoxaparin).
  • Severe Allergic Reactions: Patients with severe allergies are prescribed epinephrine auto-injectors for emergency treatment of anaphylaxis.
  • Autoimmune Conditions: Certain chronic autoimmune diseases may require SC administration of biologic medications.
  • Infectious Disease Prevention: Specific vaccines are administered subcutaneously to elicit an immune response.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Site Rotation: Always rotate injection sites to prevent lipodystrophy (fat tissue changes) and ensure consistent medication absorption.
  • Aseptic Technique: Maintain strict hand hygiene and use an alcohol swab to clean the injection site to prevent infection.
  • Needle Safety: Never recap a used needle; immediately dispose of it in a designated sharps container to prevent needlestick injuries.
  • Medication Verification: Double-check the medication, dose, route, time, and patient identity before every injection.
  • Avoid Compromised Areas: Do not inject into areas that are bruised, scarred, inflamed, infected, or have moles or birthmarks.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Pre-injection Fasting: Generally not required for subcutaneous injections unless specifically indicated by the medication or a concurrent diagnostic procedure.
  • Hydration Status: Maintaining adequate hydration can contribute to healthy skin turgor, potentially making injections more comfortable.
  • Insulin & Meal Timing: For insulin injections, strictly adhere to prescribed meal timings and carbohydrate intake to optimize blood glucose control.
  • Post-injection Fluid Intake: Encourage normal fluid intake unless contraindicated by the specific medication or the patient's underlying medical condition.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Emergency Response: Be aware of potential allergic reactions (e.g., rash, swelling, difficulty breathing) and know how to respond immediately.
  • Proper Sharps Disposal: Always use an FDA-cleared sharps disposal container for used needles and syringes; never dispose of them in household trash.
  • Medication Storage: Store all medications according to manufacturer guidelines, paying close attention to temperature requirements (e.g., refrigeration for insulin).
  • Monitor Injection Site: Observe the injection site for any signs of adverse reactions such as excessive redness, swelling, pain, warmth, or pus, and report them promptly.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Patient Education is Key: Thoroughly educate patients on proper injection technique, site rotation, and sharps disposal for safe self-administration.
  • Adherence to Regimen: Emphasize the critical importance of consistent medication adherence and correct dosing for optimal therapeutic outcomes.
  • Recognize Complications: Instruct patients on how to identify and report potential complications such as infection, lipodystrophy, or systemic adverse effects.
  • Regular Follow-up: Schedule regular follow-up appointments to assess injection sites, review technique, and make necessary dose adjustments.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Anatomy Review: Understand the layers of the skin and the location of subcutaneous tissue for accurate needle placement.
  • Injection Angle: Typically, a 45-degree angle is used for thinner patients or longer needles, while a 90-degree angle is suitable for patients with more subcutaneous tissue or shorter needles.
  • Aspiration: Current guidelines generally do not recommend aspirating (pulling back on the plunger) for most subcutaneous injections, as large blood vessels are not typically present in this layer.
  • Documentation: Meticulously document the medication, dose, date, time, specific injection site, and any patient reactions or education provided.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Needle Specifications: Common needle gauges range from 25-30 gauge, with lengths typically between 4mm (5/32 inch) and 8mm (5/16 inch).
  • Preferred Sites: The abdomen (at least 2 inches from the navel), outer aspects of the upper arms, and anterior/lateral thighs are common sites due to ample subcutaneous tissue.
  • Volume Limit: The maximum volume for a single subcutaneous injection is generally 0.5 mL to 1.5 mL to ensure proper absorption and minimize discomfort.
  • Lipohypertrophy/Lipoatrophy: These are localized fat tissue changes (lumps or indentations) that can occur with repeated injections in the same site, affecting absorption.
  • Bioavailability: Subcutaneous administration offers good bioavailability, meaning a high percentage of the drug reaches systemic circulation, albeit at a slower rate than IV.
