Bacterial vaginosis

🏠 Home / Sexual and reproductive health / Vaginal infections and vaginal discharge

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Common Vaginal Infection: Bacterial vaginosis (BV) is a common condition resulting from an overgrowth of certain bacteria naturally found in the vagina, disrupting the normal balance of vaginal flora.
  • Not an STI, but Linked: While not considered a sexually transmitted infection (STI), BV is more common in sexually active individuals and can increase the risk of acquiring STIs.
  • Impact on Reproductive Health: Untreated BV can lead to serious complications, including an increased risk of pelvic inflammatory disease (PID), preterm birth in pregnant individuals, and heightened susceptibility to other infections.
  • Recurrence is Common: BV frequently recurs, even after successful treatment, necessitating ongoing management and awareness of risk factors.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Unusual Vaginal Discharge: Thin, white or gray discharge, often described as milky or watery.
  • Fishy Odor: A strong, foul-smelling, fishy vaginal odor, which may become more noticeable after sexual intercourse or during menstruation.
  • Vaginal Itching or Irritation: Mild to moderate itching around the outside of the vagina.
  • Burning During Urination: Discomfort or a burning sensation when urinating.
  • Asymptomatic Cases: Many individuals with BV experience no noticeable symptoms, making regular check-ups important.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Avoid Douching: Douching can disrupt the natural balance of vaginal bacteria and increase the risk of BV.
  • Practice Safe Sex: Consistent and correct use of condoms can help reduce the risk of BV, especially with new or multiple partners.
  • Limit Sexual Partners: Reducing the number of sexual partners may decrease the risk of BV.
  • Choose Breathable Underwear: Wearing cotton underwear and avoiding tight-fitting clothing can help maintain a healthy vaginal environment.
  • Avoid Scented Products: Refrain from using scented tampons, pads, soaps, or other feminine hygiene products that can irritate the vagina.
  • Complete Treatment Course: If diagnosed and prescribed antibiotics, it is crucial to complete the entire course of medication, even if symptoms improve.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Probiotic-Rich Foods: Incorporating foods rich in probiotics, such as yogurt with live and active cultures, kefir, and fermented vegetables, may help support a healthy gut and vaginal microbiome.
  • Hydration: Maintain adequate fluid intake to support overall bodily functions and mucosal health.
  • Limit Sugary and Processed Foods: Reducing intake of excessive sugars and highly processed foods may contribute to a more balanced internal environment, indirectly supporting microbial health.
  • Balanced Nutrient Intake: A diet rich in fruits, vegetables, and whole grains provides essential nutrients that support immune function and overall health.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Prompt Medical Consultation: Seek medical attention immediately if new vaginal symptoms appear, especially if accompanied by fever, pelvic pain, or unusual discharge.
  • Avoid Self-Treatment: Do not attempt to self-diagnose or self-treat BV, as symptoms can mimic other vaginal infections requiring different treatments.
  • Inform Healthcare Provider of Pregnancy: Pregnant individuals with BV symptoms must inform their healthcare provider promptly due to the increased risk of preterm labor and other pregnancy complications.
  • Alcohol Restriction with Metronidazole: If prescribed metronidazole, strictly avoid alcohol consumption during treatment and for at least 24-72 hours after the last dose to prevent severe adverse reactions (disulfiram-like effect).
  • Partner Notification (Recurrent BV): While BV is not an STI, if you experience recurrent BV, discussing it with your sexual partners and healthcare provider may be beneficial for identifying potential contributing factors.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Accurate Diagnosis is Key: Diagnosis of BV relies on clinical assessment, including Amsel criteria (homogeneous discharge, vaginal pH >4.5, positive whiff test, clue cells on microscopy) or Gram stain (Nugent score).
  • Antibiotic Treatment: First-line treatment typically involves antibiotics such as metronidazole (oral or vaginal gel) or clindamycin (oral or vaginal cream/suppository).
  • Adherence and Follow-up: Emphasize the importance of completing the full course of antibiotics and scheduling follow-up if symptoms persist or recur.
  • Counseling on Risk Reduction: Provide comprehensive counseling on modifiable risk factors, including douching, unprotected sex, and use of irritating feminine products.
  • Screen for Co-infections: Consider screening for other STIs, especially in individuals with recurrent BV or multiple risk factors.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Pathophysiology: BV is characterized by a shift from a lactobacilli-dominant vaginal flora to an overgrowth of anaerobic bacteria (e.g., Gardnerella vaginalis, Mycoplasma hominis).
  • Nursing Assessment: Obtain a thorough history including sexual activity, douching practices, and detailed description of vaginal discharge, odor, and associated symptoms.
  • Patient Education: Educate patients on medication administration, potential side effects (e.g., metallic taste with metronidazole), importance of adherence, and strategies for preventing recurrence.
  • Complication Awareness: Understand and educate patients about the increased risk of HIV acquisition, pelvic inflammatory disease (PID), and adverse pregnancy outcomes associated with untreated BV.
  • Vaginal pH Monitoring: Nurses may assist in measuring vaginal pH, a key diagnostic indicator for BV (typically >4.5).

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Amsel Criteria: Clinical diagnostic criteria for BV, requiring at least three of four signs: (1) homogeneous, thin, white discharge; (2) vaginal pH >4.5; (3) positive whiff test (amine odor with KOH); (4) presence of clue cells on microscopy.
  • Nugent Score: A Gram stain-based scoring system used to diagnose BV by quantifying different bacterial morphotypes in vaginal smears.
  • First-line Pharmacotherapy: Metronidazole (500 mg orally twice daily for 7 days or 0.75% gel intravaginally once daily for 5 days) or Clindamycin (300 mg orally twice daily for 7 days or 2% cream intravaginally at bedtime for 7 days).
  • Recurrence Management: For recurrent BV, extended or suppressive regimens may be considered, often involving longer courses of metronidazole gel or oral metronidazole.
  • Differential Diagnoses: Candidiasis, Trichomoniasis, desquamative inflammatory vaginitis, atrophic vaginitis.
  • ICD-10 Code: N76.0 (Acute vaginitis) or N76.1 (Subacute and chronic vaginitis) are often used, with specific codes for other specified inflammations of the vagina and vulva if more detail is available.
