Acute liver failure in adults

🏠 Home / Liver disease / Cirrhosis

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Definition: Acute liver failure (ALF) is the rapid development of severe liver dysfunction in an individual without pre-existing liver disease.
  • Key Characteristics: It is defined by coagulopathy (INR ≥ 1.5) and any degree of hepatic encephalopathy occurring within 8 weeks of the onset of liver injury.
  • Urgency: ALF is a medical emergency requiring immediate hospitalization, often in an intensive care unit, due to its rapid progression and high mortality rate.
  • Etiology: Common causes include acetaminophen overdose, viral hepatitis (A, B, E), drug-induced liver injury, and autoimmune hepatitis.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Early Non-specific Symptoms: Fatigue, malaise, nausea, vomiting, and right upper quadrant abdominal pain.
  • Jaundice: Yellowing of the skin and sclera (whites of the eyes) due to elevated bilirubin levels.
  • Coagulopathy Signs: Easy bruising, petechiae, epistaxis (nosebleeds), and gastrointestinal bleeding.
  • Hepatic Encephalopathy: Altered mental status ranging from subtle confusion and disorientation to asterixis (flapping tremor), somnolence, and coma.
  • Fluid Retention: Development of ascites (fluid accumulation in the abdomen) and peripheral edema.
  • Other: Dark urine and pale stools.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Hepatotoxic Drug Avoidance: Exercise extreme caution with medications known to cause liver damage, especially acetaminophen, and avoid excessive dosing.
  • Alcohol Abstinence: Strict avoidance of alcohol, as it can exacerbate liver injury and complicate recovery.
  • Infection Prevention: Implement rigorous infection control measures, as infections can precipitate or worsen ALF.
  • Bleeding Risk Management: Monitor closely for signs of bleeding and take precautions to prevent trauma due to impaired clotting.
  • Neurological Monitoring: Frequent and meticulous assessment of neurological status is vital to detect and manage worsening hepatic encephalopathy.
  • Early Referral: Prompt referral to a specialized liver center or transplant unit is critical for optimal management.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Nutritional Support: Patients often require aggressive nutritional support, including enteral or parenteral feeding, to meet high metabolic demands.
  • Protein Intake: While historically restricted, current guidelines emphasize adequate protein intake unless encephalopathy is severe and refractory to other treatments.
  • Sodium Restriction: May be necessary to manage ascites and peripheral edema.
  • Fluid Management: Careful monitoring and restriction of fluid intake may be required, especially in the presence of cerebral edema or renal impairment.
  • Vitamin K Supplementation: Administered to correct coagulopathy if vitamin K deficiency is identified.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Emergency Response: Recognize ALF as a life-threatening emergency and ensure rapid transfer to an appropriate critical care setting.
  • Neurological Assessment: Perform frequent neurological assessments (e.g., Glasgow Coma Scale, assessment for asterixis) to track encephalopathy progression.
  • Bleeding Precautions: Implement measures such as avoiding intramuscular injections, using soft toothbrushes, and monitoring for signs of hemorrhage.
  • Infection Surveillance: Vigilantly monitor for signs of infection (fever, chills, elevated white blood cell count) and initiate prompt diagnostic workup and treatment.
  • Fluid & Electrolyte Balance: Maintain strict intake and output records and monitor electrolyte levels, renal function, and acid-base status.
  • Medication Management: Administer medications cautiously, adjusting dosages as needed due to impaired hepatic metabolism and potential for drug accumulation.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Rapid Etiology Identification: Prioritize swift diagnosis of the underlying cause, as specific antidotes (e.g., N-acetylcysteine for acetaminophen toxicity) can be life-saving.
  • Transplant Evaluation: Early consultation with a liver transplant center is paramount, as liver transplantation is often the definitive treatment for non-recovering patients.
  • Multidisciplinary Care: Emphasize a collaborative approach involving hepatologists, intensivists, neurologists, and transplant surgeons.
  • Complication Management: Focus on aggressive management of critical complications such as cerebral edema, intracranial hypertension, sepsis, acute kidney injury, and hypoglycemia.
  • Prognostic Assessment: Utilize established criteria (e.g., King's College Criteria) to assess prognosis and guide management decisions, including transplant candidacy.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Pathophysiology Understanding: Grasp the mechanisms of massive hepatocyte necrosis, impaired synthetic function, and the resulting systemic complications.
  • Comprehensive Assessment: Develop skills in assessing for jaundice, ascites, neurological changes, and signs of bleeding or infection.
  • Pharmacology Knowledge: Understand the indications, dosages, and side effects of medications used in ALF, including specific antidotes and supportive therapies.
  • Monitoring Expertise: Become proficient in interpreting laboratory values (INR, bilirubin, transaminases, ammonia, electrolytes) and their clinical implications.
  • Patient & Family Education: Provide clear, empathetic communication and education to patients and their families regarding the critical nature of ALF and treatment plans.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Common Etiologies: Acetaminophen overdose, viral hepatitis (A, B, E), drug-induced liver injury (DILI), autoimmune hepatitis, Wilson's disease, Budd-Chiari syndrome, acute fatty liver of pregnancy.
  • Diagnostic Criteria: INR ≥ 1.5 and any grade of hepatic encephalopathy in a patient without pre-existing liver disease, with illness duration typically less than 26 weeks.
  • Key Complications: Cerebral edema, intracranial hypertension, sepsis, acute kidney injury, metabolic acidosis, hypoglycemia, coagulopathy.
  • Management Strategies: N-acetylcysteine (for acetaminophen toxicity), antiviral therapy, corticosteroids (for autoimmune), plasmapheresis, continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT), liver transplantation.
  • Prognostic Scores: King's College Criteria and MELD (Model for End-Stage Liver Disease) score are used to assess severity and predict outcomes.
