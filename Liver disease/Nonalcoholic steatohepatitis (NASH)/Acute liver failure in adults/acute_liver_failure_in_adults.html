Acute liver failure in adults

🏠 Home / Liver disease / Nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH)

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Rapid Onset & Severity: Acute liver failure (ALF) is a rare but life-threatening condition characterized by the rapid development of severe liver dysfunction, encephalopathy, and coagulopathy in individuals with no prior history of liver disease or with previously undiagnosed, compensated liver disease.
  • NASH as a Risk Factor: While often triggered by acute insults, underlying conditions like Nonalcoholic Steatohepatitis (NASH) can increase liver vulnerability, potentially contributing to the severity or progression of acute liver injury, or leading to acute decompensation in advanced NASH-related cirrhosis.
  • Urgent Medical Emergency: ALF requires immediate hospitalization and specialized care, often in an intensive care unit, due to its rapid progression and high mortality rate without timely intervention, including potential liver transplantation.
  • Diverse Etiologies: Causes range from drug-induced liver injury (e.g., acetaminophen overdose) and viral hepatitis to autoimmune conditions and ischemic events, necessitating prompt identification of the underlying cause.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Jaundice: Yellowing of the skin and eyes due to bilirubin accumulation, often one of the earliest and most noticeable signs.
  • Hepatic Encephalopathy: A spectrum of neuropsychiatric abnormalities, including confusion, disorientation, lethargy, asterixis (flapping tremor), and in severe cases, coma, resulting from the liver's inability to detoxify ammonia.
  • Coagulopathy: Impaired blood clotting leading to easy bruising, petechiae, epistaxis (nosebleeds), or gastrointestinal bleeding due to reduced synthesis of clotting factors.
  • Nausea & Vomiting: Common gastrointestinal symptoms that can precede or accompany other signs of liver failure.
  • Fatigue & Malaise: Profound tiredness and a general feeling of discomfort or illness.
  • Abdominal Pain & Distension: May indicate liver enlargement, ascites (fluid accumulation in the abdomen), or other intra-abdominal complications.
  • Dark Urine & Pale Stools: Resulting from bilirubin excretion in urine and lack of bilirubin in stools, respectively.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Avoid Hepatotoxic Substances: Exercise extreme caution with medications, herbal supplements, and recreational drugs known to cause liver damage. Always adhere to prescribed dosages, especially for acetaminophen.
  • Vaccination & Hygiene: Ensure vaccination against hepatitis A and B, and practice good hygiene to prevent viral hepatitis infections.
  • Manage Underlying Metabolic Conditions: For individuals with NASH, rigorous management of associated metabolic syndrome components (diabetes, obesity, dyslipidemia) is vital to prevent progression to advanced liver disease and reduce vulnerability to acute insults.
  • Early Recognition of Symptoms: Promptly seek medical attention for new-onset jaundice, unexplained confusion, severe fatigue, or easy bleeding, as early diagnosis is critical for improving outcomes.
  • Alcohol Abstinence: Strict avoidance of alcohol, particularly for individuals with any pre-existing liver condition, as it can exacerbate liver injury and precipitate acute decompensation.
  • Infection Prevention: Maintain vigilance against infections, as they can trigger or worsen acute liver failure.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Protein Restriction (if encephalopathy present): Protein intake may be carefully restricted to reduce ammonia production, especially in patients with significant hepatic encephalopathy. This must be balanced with nutritional needs.
  • Sodium Restriction: Strict limitation of sodium intake is crucial to manage and prevent ascites and peripheral edema.
  • Fluid Restriction: Fluid intake may be restricted in cases of severe hyponatremia or significant fluid overload.
  • Caloric Support: Adequate non-protein calories (from carbohydrates and fats) are essential to prevent catabolism and provide energy, often administered via enteral or parenteral routes in critical settings.
  • Vitamin & Mineral Supplementation: Close monitoring and supplementation of vitamins (especially fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E, K) and minerals may be necessary due to impaired absorption and synthesis.
  • Avoidance of Certain Foods: Foods high in ammonia-producing compounds or those that are difficult to digest may be restricted based on individual patient status and tolerance.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Continuous Monitoring: Meticulous monitoring of vital signs, neurological status (Glasgow Coma Scale, pupillary reflexes), fluid balance (strict intake/output), and laboratory parameters (INR, bilirubin, ammonia, electrolytes, renal function) is paramount.
  • Cerebral Edema Management: Vigilance for signs of increasing intracranial pressure and prompt intervention with measures like mannitol, hypertonic saline, and head elevation.
  • Coagulopathy Correction: Administration of vitamin K, fresh frozen plasma (FFP), or cryoprecipitate as indicated to manage bleeding risk, while being mindful of volume overload.
  • Infection Surveillance & Treatment: Proactive screening for infections (bacterial, fungal) and prompt initiation of broad-spectrum antibiotics or antifungals, as infections are common and can worsen prognosis.
  • Renal Support: Management of acute kidney injury, which often complicates ALF, potentially requiring renal replacement therapy (dialysis).
  • Nutritional Support: Ensuring adequate caloric and micronutrient intake, often through enteral or parenteral nutrition, to prevent malnutrition and support recovery.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Etiology-Specific Treatment: Identifying the precise cause of ALF is critical, as specific antidotes (e.g., N-acetylcysteine for acetaminophen toxicity) or targeted therapies (e.g., antivirals for hepatitis B, corticosteroids for autoimmune hepatitis) can significantly alter outcomes.
  • Early Transplant Referral: For patients meeting specific criteria (e.g., King's College Criteria, MELD score), early referral to a liver transplant center is crucial, as liver transplantation remains the definitive treatment for many cases of ALF.
  • Multidisciplinary Team Approach: Optimal management requires a coordinated effort involving hepatologists, intensivists, neurologists, nephrologists, infectious disease specialists, and transplant surgeons.
  • Prognostic Assessment: Regular reassessment of prognostic indicators and clinical status guides treatment escalation, withdrawal of care discussions, and transplant candidacy.
  • Complication Prevention: Proactive strategies to prevent and manage common complications such as cerebral edema, sepsis, acute kidney injury, and gastrointestinal bleeding are central to care.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Neurological Assessment Proficiency: Develop expertise in performing frequent and accurate neurological assessments, including GCS, pupillary responses, and assessment for asterixis, to detect subtle changes indicative of worsening encephalopathy or cerebral edema.
  • Fluid & Electrolyte Management: Master the principles of fluid balance, electrolyte replacement, and recognizing signs of hyponatremia or hyperkalemia, which are common in ALF.
  • Medication Administration & Monitoring: Understand the indications, dosages, and potential side effects of medications used in ALF, including lactulose, rifaximin, and vasoactive agents.
  • Aseptic Technique & Infection Control: Implement stringent infection control practices, including meticulous central line care and Foley catheter management, to minimize the risk of nosocomial infections.
  • Patient & Family Education/Support: Provide clear, empathetic communication to patients and their families regarding the critical nature of the illness, treatment plan, and potential outcomes, offering emotional support during a highly stressful period.
  • Preparation for Procedures: Assist in preparing patients for various procedures, including paracentesis, central line insertion, and potential transfer for liver transplantation.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Definition: Acute liver failure is defined by the rapid onset of severe liver dysfunction (coagulopathy with INR ≥ 1.5) and encephalopathy in a patient without pre-existing cirrhosis, with an illness duration of less than 26 weeks.
  • Etiologies (Common): Acetaminophen overdose (most common in Western countries), viral hepatitis (A, B, E), idiosyncratic drug reactions, autoimmune hepatitis, ischemic hepatitis, Wilson's disease, Budd-Chiari syndrome, acute fatty liver of pregnancy, mushroom poisoning. Severe acute decompensation of underlying NASH-related cirrhosis can present similarly.
  • Diagnostic Criteria: King's College Criteria and MELD (Model for End-Stage Liver Disease) score are commonly used for prognostication and liver transplant candidacy assessment.
  • Pathophysiology: Characterized by massive hepatocellular necrosis or dysfunction, leading to impaired synthetic function (coagulopathy, hypoglycemia), impaired detoxification (encephalopathy), and systemic inflammatory response.
  • Complications: Cerebral edema, sepsis, acute kidney injury (hepatorenal syndrome), metabolic disturbances (hypoglycemia, lactic acidosis), cardiovascular instability, and gastrointestinal bleeding.
  • Treatment Modalities: Supportive care, specific antidotes (if applicable), management of complications, and orthotopic liver transplantation.
