Whooping cough in teens and adults

🏠 Home / Lung disease / Respiratory tract infections

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Contagious Bacterial Infection: Whooping cough (pertussis) is a highly contagious respiratory infection caused by the bacterium Bordetella pertussis.
  • Atypical Presentation in Adults: In teens and adults, symptoms are often milder and may not include the classic 'whooping' sound, making diagnosis challenging.
  • Source of Infection: Infected teens and adults are a significant source of transmission to vulnerable infants who are not yet fully vaccinated or are too young to be vaccinated.
  • Vaccination is Key: The Tdap vaccine (tetanus, diphtheria, acellular pertussis) is crucial for protection and preventing spread, especially for those in contact with infants.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Prolonged Cough: A severe, persistent cough lasting for weeks or even months, often worsening at night.
  • Paroxysmal Coughing Fits: Episodes of rapid, violent coughing that can make breathing difficult and may lead to exhaustion.
  • Post-Tussive Vomiting: Vomiting that occurs immediately after a coughing spell.
  • Fatigue and Sleep Disturbance: Due to the intensity and frequency of coughing episodes.
  • Mild Initial Symptoms: Early signs can resemble a common cold, including runny nose, sneezing, low-grade fever, and a mild cough.
  • Less Common 'Whoop': The characteristic inspiratory 'whoop' sound is less frequently observed in older children, teens, and adults compared to infants.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Maintain Vaccination Status: Ensure all adolescents and adults receive the Tdap vaccine as recommended, especially if they are pregnant or in close contact with infants.
  • Practice Respiratory Hygiene: Cover coughs and sneezes with a tissue or into the elbow, and dispose of tissues properly.
  • Frequent Hand Washing: Wash hands thoroughly and often with soap and water, or use an alcohol-based hand sanitizer.
  • Limit Contact When Symptomatic: If experiencing a persistent cough, avoid close contact with infants, pregnant individuals, and immunocompromised persons until diagnosis is confirmed or treatment is completed.
  • Seek Early Medical Evaluation: Consult a healthcare provider promptly for any prolonged or worsening cough to facilitate early diagnosis and treatment.
  • Consider Post-Exposure Prophylaxis: Discuss antibiotic prophylaxis with a healthcare provider if you have been in close contact with a confirmed pertussis case, especially if you are at high risk or live with vulnerable individuals.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Ensure Adequate Hydration: Drink plenty of fluids (water, clear broths, herbal teas) to help thin mucus and prevent dehydration, particularly if experiencing post-tussive vomiting.
  • Consume Small, Frequent Meals: Opt for smaller, more frequent meals rather than large ones to reduce the risk of vomiting triggered by coughing episodes.
  • Choose Easy-to-Digest Foods: Focus on bland, soft foods that are gentle on the stomach and less likely to provoke coughing or nausea.
  • Avoid Irritating Substances: Limit exposure to smoke, strong fumes, and very cold or very hot beverages that might irritate the airways and trigger coughing.
  • Nutrient-Rich Diet: Maintain a diet rich in vitamins and minerals to support the immune system during recovery.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Complete Antibiotic Course: If prescribed antibiotics, it is critical to complete the entire course, even if symptoms improve, to effectively clear the infection and prevent further transmission.
  • Monitor for Respiratory Distress: Watch for signs of severe breathing difficulty, such as rapid breathing, gasping, or bluish discoloration of the lips or face, and seek immediate medical attention.
  • Avoid Unnecessary Cough Suppressants: Over-the-counter cough suppressants are generally not effective for pertussis and may mask symptoms; consult a doctor before using.
  • Protect Vulnerable Contacts: Inform close contacts, especially those with infants or who are pregnant, about your diagnosis so they can seek medical advice regarding prophylaxis.
  • Rest and Recovery: Prioritize rest to aid recovery and manage the fatigue associated with persistent coughing.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • High Index of Suspicion: Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for pertussis in adolescents and adults presenting with a prolonged cough, even without the classic 'whoop.'
  • Timely Diagnostic Testing: Nasopharyngeal swab for PCR (polymerase chain reaction) is the preferred diagnostic method, especially in the early stages of illness.
  • Early Antibiotic Treatment: Macrolide antibiotics (e.g., azithromycin, clarithromycin, erythromycin) are most effective when administered during the catarrhal stage or early paroxysmal stage to reduce symptom severity and duration, and to prevent transmission.
  • Public Health Reporting: Pertussis is a reportable disease, and healthcare providers must notify local public health authorities of confirmed cases.
  • Vaccination Recommendations: Advocate for Tdap vaccination for all adolescents and adults, particularly pregnant women (during each pregnancy, preferably in the third trimester) and caregivers of infants.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Patient Education: Educate patients and their families on the contagious nature of pertussis, the importance of vaccination, and proper infection control measures.
  • Assessment and Monitoring: Assess respiratory status, hydration, and nutritional intake, especially in patients with severe coughing or post-tussive vomiting.
  • Infection Control Protocols: Implement droplet precautions for hospitalized patients with suspected or confirmed pertussis to prevent nosocomial spread.
  • Vaccine Administration: Understand the indications, contraindications, and proper administration techniques for the Tdap vaccine in adolescents and adults.
  • Symptom Management: Provide comfort measures, such as humidified air, and assist patients in managing coughing paroxysms and associated distress.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Etiological Agent: Bordetella pertussis, a Gram-negative coccobacillus.
  • Pathophysiology: Bacteria adhere to ciliated epithelial cells in the respiratory tract, producing toxins (e.g., pertussis toxin, tracheal cytotoxin) that impair ciliary function, cause local inflammation, and systemic effects.
  • Clinical Stages: Typically progresses through catarrhal (1-2 weeks), paroxysmal (1-6 weeks), and convalescent (weeks to months) stages.
  • Complications in Adults: Can include rib fractures, pneumonia, weight loss, urinary incontinence, and subconjunctival hemorrhage due to severe coughing.
  • Vaccine Efficacy and Waning Immunity: Tdap vaccine provides good protection, but immunity wanes over time, necessitating booster doses and targeted vaccination strategies.
  • Diagnostic Methods: PCR from nasopharyngeal swabs is the most sensitive and specific test, especially in the first 3-4 weeks of cough. Culture is also possible but less sensitive.
