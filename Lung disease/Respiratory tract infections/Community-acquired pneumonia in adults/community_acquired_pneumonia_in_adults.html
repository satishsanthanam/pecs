Community-acquired pneumonia in adults

🏠 Home / Lung disease / Respiratory tract infections

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Definition: Community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) is an acute infection of the lung parenchyma acquired outside of hospitals or long-term care facilities.
  • Common Pathogens: Frequently caused by bacteria such as Streptococcus pneumoniae, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae, and various viruses.
  • Risk Factors: Increased risk in individuals over 65 years, those with chronic lung diseases (e.g., COPD, asthma), smokers, and immunocompromised patients.
  • Severity Assessment: Tools like CURB-65 or the Pneumonia Severity Index (PSI/PORT score) are used to determine the appropriate treatment setting (outpatient vs. inpatient).

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Acute Onset: Sudden development of fever, chills, and a cough that may produce phlegm (sputum) or be dry.
  • Respiratory Distress: Shortness of breath (dyspnea) and pleuritic chest pain, which worsens with deep breaths or coughing.
  • Systemic Manifestations: General fatigue, muscle aches (myalgia), headache, and in elderly patients, new-onset confusion or altered mental status.
  • Physical Findings: Tachypnea (rapid breathing), crackles (rales) on lung auscultation, egophony, and dullness to percussion over affected areas.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Vaccination: Ensure up-to-date pneumococcal and influenza vaccinations, especially for at-risk populations, to significantly reduce incidence and severity.
  • Hygiene Practices: Regular hand washing with soap and water or alcohol-based sanitizer, and practicing respiratory etiquette (covering coughs/sneezes).
  • Smoking Cessation: Advise and support patients in quitting smoking, as it is a major risk factor for developing and worsening CAP.
  • Early Medical Consultation: Encourage prompt medical evaluation for persistent or worsening respiratory symptoms to facilitate early diagnosis and treatment.
  • Treatment Adherence: Emphasize the importance of completing the full course of prescribed antibiotics, even if symptoms improve, to prevent recurrence and resistance.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Hydration: Maintain adequate fluid intake (water, clear broths, electrolyte solutions) to help thin respiratory secretions and prevent dehydration, particularly with fever.
  • Nutritional Support: Consume small, frequent, nutrient-dense meals to support the immune system and provide energy for recovery, especially if appetite is poor.
  • Avoid Irritants: Limit intake of alcohol and caffeine, which can contribute to dehydration and potentially interfere with rest.
  • Soft Foods: Opt for easily digestible, soft foods if coughing or a sore throat makes chewing and swallowing difficult.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Monitor for Worsening: Immediately seek medical attention for increasing shortness of breath, high-grade fever, new confusion, or severe chest pain.
  • Medication Compliance: Strictly adhere to the prescribed antibiotic regimen; do not discontinue medication prematurely, even if symptoms improve.
  • Infection Control: Practice good respiratory hygiene and consider temporary isolation from vulnerable individuals to prevent transmission.
  • Follow-up Care: Attend all scheduled follow-up appointments to ensure complete resolution of the infection and monitor for potential complications.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Diagnostic Confirmation: A chest X-ray is crucial for confirming the diagnosis of pneumonia by identifying new infiltrates; sputum cultures and blood tests may guide specific antimicrobial therapy.
  • Empiric Treatment: Initial antibiotic selection is based on local resistance patterns, patient comorbidities, severity of illness, and risk factors for specific pathogens.
  • Hospitalization Criteria: Decisions for inpatient admission are guided by severity scores (e.g., CURB-65) and clinical judgment, considering factors like hypoxemia, hemodynamic instability, and inability to maintain oral intake.
  • Potential Complications: Vigilance is required for complications such as pleural effusion, empyema, sepsis, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), and lung abscess.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Comprehensive Assessment: Perform thorough respiratory assessments, including monitoring vital signs, oxygen saturation, respiratory rate, and lung sounds.
  • Therapeutic Interventions: Administer prescribed medications (antibiotics, bronchodilators, antipyretics), provide oxygen therapy as indicated, and assist with airway clearance techniques.
  • Patient Education: Educate patients on medication adherence, symptom monitoring, importance of hydration, energy conservation, and activity limitations during recovery.
  • Infection Prevention: Implement appropriate infection control measures, including droplet precautions if indicated, and reinforce hand hygiene for patients and visitors.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • ICD-10 Codes: J18.9 (Pneumonia, unspecified organism), J13 (Pneumonia due to Streptococcus pneumoniae), J15.7 (Pneumonia due to Mycoplasma pneumoniae).
  • Common Antibiotics (Outpatient): Amoxicillin, Doxycycline, Azithromycin.
  • Common Antibiotics (Inpatient): Ceftriaxone + Azithromycin/Doxycycline, or a fluoroquinolone (e.g., Levofloxacin).
  • Diagnostic Criteria: Clinical symptoms (cough, fever, dyspnea) combined with a new infiltrate on chest X-ray.
  • Risk Stratification Tools: CURB-65 score (Confusion, Urea >7 mmol/L, Respiratory rate >30 breaths/min, Blood pressure <90 mmHg systolic or <60 mmHg diastolic, Age >65 years), Pneumonia Severity Index (PSI)/PORT score.
