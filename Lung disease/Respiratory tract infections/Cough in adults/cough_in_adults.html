Cough in adults

🏠 Home / Lung disease / Respiratory tract infections

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Definition: A cough is a sudden, forceful expulsion of air from the lungs, serving as a protective reflex to clear the airways of irritants, mucus, or foreign particles.
  • Common Causes: In adults, coughs are frequently caused by respiratory tract infections (e.g., common cold, flu, bronchitis), allergies, asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), and post-nasal drip.
  • Acute vs. Chronic: An acute cough typically lasts less than three weeks, often due to viral infections. A chronic cough persists for eight weeks or longer, warranting thorough medical investigation.
  • When to Seek Care: Persistent cough, cough accompanied by fever, shortness of breath, chest pain, or coughing up blood (hemoptysis) requires prompt medical evaluation.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Fever and Chills: Often indicate an underlying infection such as influenza, bronchitis, or pneumonia.
  • Sore Throat and Runny Nose: Commonly associated with upper respiratory tract infections (URTIs).
  • Chest Pain or Discomfort: Can be a symptom of pleurisy, bronchitis, or pneumonia, especially if sharp or worsened by deep breaths.
  • Shortness of Breath (Dyspnea): A concerning symptom that may suggest asthma exacerbation, pneumonia, or other serious lung conditions.
  • Wheezing: A high-pitched whistling sound, often heard during exhalation, indicative of narrowed airways, common in asthma or COPD.
  • Hoarseness: May accompany a cough, particularly if the larynx is inflamed due to infection or irritation.
  • Sputum Production: The color, consistency, and volume of mucus can provide clues; clear/white for viral, yellow/green for bacterial infections.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Infection Control: Always cover your mouth and nose with a tissue or your elbow when coughing or sneezing to prevent the spread of respiratory droplets.
  • Hand Hygiene: Wash hands frequently with soap and water for at least 20 seconds, especially after coughing, sneezing, or blowing your nose.
  • Avoid Irritants: Minimize exposure to environmental irritants such as tobacco smoke (firsthand and secondhand), strong chemical fumes, and known allergens.
  • Hydration: Maintain adequate fluid intake to help thin mucus, making it easier to clear from the airways.
  • Rest: Ensure sufficient rest to support the body's immune response and recovery process.
  • Monitor for Worsening: Be vigilant for any signs of worsening symptoms, such as increasing difficulty breathing, high fever, or changes in sputum color/volume.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Warm Fluids: Consume warm liquids like herbal teas, broth, or warm water with honey and lemon to soothe the throat and help thin mucus.
  • Hydration: Increase overall fluid intake (water, clear juices) to prevent dehydration and aid in mucus clearance.
  • Avoid Irritating Foods: Limit consumption of very spicy, acidic, or extremely cold foods and beverages that might irritate a sensitive throat or trigger reflux.
  • Honey: For adults, a teaspoon of honey can help soothe a cough, particularly before bedtime. Avoid in infants under one year old.
  • Nutrient-Rich Foods: Focus on easily digestible, nutrient-dense foods to support immune function without causing gastrointestinal distress.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Medical Consultation: Seek medical advice for any cough that is severe, persistent, accompanied by high fever, shortness of breath, chest pain, or hemoptysis.
  • Medication Use: Use over-the-counter cough suppressants or expectorants judiciously and according to package directions; they are not suitable for all types of coughs.
  • Antibiotic Misuse: Do not demand antibiotics for viral coughs, as they are ineffective against viruses and contribute to antibiotic resistance.
  • Allergy Management: If allergies are suspected, consider antihistamines or nasal corticosteroids as directed by a healthcare provider.
  • Reflux Management: For coughs related to GERD, adhere to prescribed acid-reducing medications and dietary modifications.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Thorough Assessment: A comprehensive history, physical examination, and potentially diagnostic tests are crucial to identify the underlying cause of a cough.
  • Differential Diagnosis: Consider a broad range of etiologies, from common viral infections to more serious conditions like pneumonia, asthma, COPD, heart failure, or even certain medications.
  • Tailored Treatment: Treatment must be individualized, targeting the specific cause of the cough rather than just symptomatic relief.
  • Patient Education: Educate patients on cough etiquette, hydration, and the appropriate use of medications, emphasizing when to return for follow-up.
  • Red Flag Recognition: Always be alert for 'red flag' symptoms such as hemoptysis, unexplained weight loss, or persistent dyspnea, which necessitate urgent investigation.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Cough Assessment: Systematically assess cough characteristics including onset, duration, frequency, quality (e.g., dry, productive, barking), precipitating factors, and associated symptoms.
  • Patient Education: Provide clear instructions on proper cough etiquette, hand hygiene, fluid intake, and the importance of completing prescribed medication courses.
  • Respiratory Monitoring: Monitor respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, breath sounds, and work of breathing to detect signs of respiratory distress or worsening lung function.
  • Comfort Measures: Implement non-pharmacological interventions such as humidified air, elevation of the head of the bed, and throat lozenges to enhance patient comfort.
  • Medication Administration: Administer prescribed antitussives, expectorants, bronchodilators, or antibiotics, ensuring correct dosage, route, and monitoring for side effects.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Etiologies: Acute (viral URI, acute bronchitis, pneumonia, pertussis); Chronic (post-nasal drip syndrome, asthma, GERD, COPD, ACE inhibitor use, bronchiectasis, lung cancer).
  • Diagnostic Tools: Chest X-ray, spirometry, sputum culture, allergy testing, pH monitoring (for GERD), CT scan of the chest, bronchoscopy.
  • Pharmacological Interventions: Antitussives (dextromethorphan, codeine), expectorants (guaifenesin), bronchodilators (albuterol), inhaled corticosteroids, antibiotics (for bacterial infections), proton pump inhibitors (for GERD).
  • Red Flag Symptoms: Hemoptysis, unexplained weight loss, persistent fever, night sweats, dyspnea at rest or with minimal exertion, unilateral wheezing, dysphagia.
  • Complications: Rib fractures (from severe coughing), syncope, urinary incontinence, sleep disturbance, spread of infection.
