Tracheostomy

🏠 Home / Lung disease / Tracheostomy

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Definition: A tracheostomy is a surgical procedure to create an opening (stoma) in the neck into the trachea (windpipe) to insert a tube.
  • Purpose: It provides an alternative airway for breathing, bypassing upper airway obstructions, facilitating long-term mechanical ventilation, or aiding in the removal of respiratory secretions.
  • Indications: Commonly performed for prolonged respiratory support, severe airway obstruction (e.g., tumors, trauma, swelling), inability to clear secretions, or to protect the airway from aspiration.
  • Types: Can be temporary (e.g., for acute respiratory failure recovery) or permanent (e.g., for chronic conditions requiring lifelong airway support).

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Severe Respiratory Distress: Marked by labored breathing, gasping, accessory muscle use, and cyanosis, often necessitating emergency airway intervention.
  • Upper Airway Obstruction: Symptoms include stridor (high-pitched breathing sound), hoarseness, difficulty swallowing, and progressive shortness of breath.
  • Chronic Aspiration: Recurrent pneumonia, coughing during or after eating, and unexplained weight loss due to food or fluid entering the lungs.
  • Neurological Impairment: Conditions leading to impaired cough reflex, inability to protect the airway, or prolonged unconsciousness requiring ventilatory support.
  • Failed Extubation: Inability to maintain adequate ventilation after removal of an endotracheal tube, indicating a need for a more stable airway.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Stoma Site Care: Meticulous daily cleaning of the stoma site to prevent infection and skin breakdown, using sterile technique for dressing changes.
  • Suctioning Protocol: Perform suctioning only when indicated (e.g., audible secretions, increased work of breathing), using appropriate catheter size and sterile technique to prevent trauma and infection.
  • Cuff Management: If a cuffed tube is used, monitor cuff pressure regularly (typically 20-25 cmH2O) to prevent tracheal ischemia or aspiration.
  • Emergency Preparedness: Always have a spare tracheostomy tube (same size and one size smaller), obturator, suction equipment, and resuscitation bag readily available.
  • Humidification: Ensure adequate humidification of inspired air to prevent drying of secretions and mucous plugging, which can lead to airway obstruction.
  • Communication Strategies: Establish alternative communication methods (e.g., writing, picture boards, speaking valves) as the patient may be unable to speak initially.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Swallowing Assessment: A thorough swallowing evaluation by a speech-language pathologist is crucial before initiating oral intake to assess aspiration risk.
  • Modified Diets: Patients may require thickened liquids or pureed foods initially, progressing as tolerated based on swallowing function.
  • Aspiration Precautions: Ensure the patient is upright during meals, encourage slow eating, and monitor for signs of aspiration (e.g., coughing, choking, wet voice).
  • Cuff Deflation for Swallowing: If medically appropriate and safe, cuff deflation during meals may improve swallowing mechanics and sensation, but only under clinical guidance.
  • Oral Hygiene: Meticulous oral care is essential to reduce bacterial load and prevent aspiration pneumonia, even if not eating orally.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Monitor Respiratory Status: Continuously assess for signs of respiratory distress, changes in breathing patterns, or increased work of breathing.
  • Tracheostomy Tube Patency: Regularly check for signs of tube obstruction (e.g., difficulty passing suction catheter, increased peak inspiratory pressures).
  • Bleeding and Infection: Observe the stoma site for excessive bleeding, redness, swelling, purulent discharge, or foul odor.
  • Accidental Decannulation: Know the emergency protocol for re-insertion of the tracheostomy tube, especially in newly formed stomas.
  • Patient Positioning: Maintain appropriate head and neck positioning to ensure tube security and patient comfort, especially during sleep.
  • Caregiver Education: Provide comprehensive training to caregivers on tracheostomy care, suctioning, emergency procedures, and signs of complications.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Indications for Placement: Tracheostomy is considered when prolonged intubation is anticipated, for upper airway obstruction, or for chronic secretion management.
  • Surgical Technique: Can be performed surgically (open) or percutaneously, with choice depending on patient factors and urgency.
  • Post-Operative Management: Focuses on airway patency, stoma care, infection prevention, and early mobilization.
  • Weaning and Decannulation: A structured weaning protocol, often involving cuff deflation, speaking valves, and progressive tube downsizing, is crucial for successful decannulation.
  • Potential Complications: Awareness of immediate (e.g., bleeding, pneumothorax) and long-term complications (e.g., granulation tissue, tracheomalacia, tracheoesophageal fistula) is vital for timely intervention.
  • Multidisciplinary Approach: Optimal care involves collaboration with respiratory therapists, speech-language pathologists, nurses, and dietitians.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Assessment Priorities: Focus on respiratory assessment (rate, effort, breath sounds), stoma site integrity, secretion characteristics, and pain level.
  • Skill Competencies: Master sterile suctioning technique, tracheostomy dressing changes, inner cannula care, and emergency tube changes.
  • Patient Education: Educate patients and families on daily care, signs of complications, emergency procedures, and communication strategies.
  • Psychosocial Support: Address patient and family anxiety, body image concerns, and communication challenges associated with a tracheostomy.
  • Preventing Complications: Implement strategies to prevent infection, skin breakdown, and accidental decannulation through meticulous care and monitoring.
  • Documentation: Accurately document all assessments, interventions, patient responses, and teaching provided.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Tracheostomy Tube Types: Includes cuffed (for ventilation/aspiration prevention), uncuffed (for stable airways), fenestrated (for speaking/weaning), and specialized tubes (e.g., Bivona Fome-Cuff).
  • Tube Sizing: Tubes are sized by inner diameter (ID), outer diameter (OD), and length, selected based on patient anatomy and clinical need.
  • Weaning Protocols: Involves progressive cuff deflation, use of speaking valves, capping trials, and downsizing of the tracheostomy tube.
  • Common Complications: Granulation tissue formation, tracheomalacia, tracheoesophageal fistula, stomal infection, hemorrhage, and accidental decannulation.
  • Speaking Valves: One-way valves that allow inspiration through the tracheostomy and expiration through the upper airway, enabling speech.
  • ICD-10 Codes: Relevant codes include J95.0 (Tracheostomy malfunction), Z43.0 (Encounter for attention to tracheostomy), and specific codes for underlying conditions necessitating tracheostomy.
