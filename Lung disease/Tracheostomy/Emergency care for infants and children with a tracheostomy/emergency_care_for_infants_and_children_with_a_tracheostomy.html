Emergency care for infants and children with a tracheostomy

🏠 Home / Lung disease / Tracheostomy

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Tracheostomy Dependence: Infants and children with a tracheostomy rely on it for breathing, making emergency preparedness critical.
  • Common Emergencies: Be vigilant for tube dislodgement (decannulation), obstruction (mucus plug), or infection, which can rapidly lead to respiratory distress.
  • Rapid Response: Immediate recognition and intervention are paramount to prevent severe hypoxia and potential brain injury.
  • Emergency Kit: Always have a readily accessible, well-stocked emergency kit with spare tracheostomy tubes, suction catheters, and a bag-valve mask.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Increased Work of Breathing: Flaring nostrils, retractions (suprasternal, intercostal, subcostal), grunting, or gasping.
  • Cyanosis: Bluish discoloration of lips, nail beds, or skin, indicating low oxygen levels.
  • Stridor or Wheezing: High-pitched sounds during breathing, especially if new or worsening, suggesting airway compromise.
  • Absent Breath Sounds: Lack of air movement heard with a stethoscope, particularly after a suspected obstruction or dislodgement.
  • Coughing/Gagging: Sudden, forceful coughing or gagging, often indicating aspiration or tube irritation.
  • Changes in Mental Status: Lethargy, irritability, unresponsiveness, or confusion, signaling hypoxia.
  • Tracheostomy Site Changes: Redness, swelling, pus, or bleeding around the stoma, indicating infection or trauma.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Securement of Tracheostomy Tube: Ensure ties or fasteners are snug but not overly tight, preventing accidental decannulation.
  • Regular Suctioning: Perform routine suctioning as prescribed to clear secretions and prevent mucus plugs.
  • Humidification: Maintain adequate humidification of inspired air to prevent drying of secretions and crusting within the tube.
  • Emergency Equipment Availability: Keep a complete emergency kit, including appropriately sized spare tubes, suction, and resuscitation bag, within immediate reach at all times.
  • Proper Stoma Care: Clean the stoma site daily with sterile technique to prevent infection and skin breakdown.
  • Infection Prevention: Practice meticulous hand hygiene and sterile technique during all tracheostomy care procedures.
  • Supervision: Never leave an infant or child with a tracheostomy unsupervised, especially during feeding or bathing.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Aspiration Risk: Be acutely aware of the increased risk of aspiration during oral feedings; signs include coughing, choking, or wet voice.
  • Thickened Liquids: Administer thickened liquids only if specifically recommended by a speech-language pathologist to reduce aspiration risk.
  • Proper Positioning: Ensure the child is in an upright position during and for at least 30 minutes after feedings to aid digestion and prevent reflux.
  • Feeding Tube Management: If a feeding tube (e.g., G-tube) is in place, adhere strictly to feeding schedules, flush protocols, and site care to prevent complications.
  • Monitoring for Aspiration: Observe for signs of aspiration such as increased respiratory effort, coughing, or desaturation during or after oral intake.
  • Oral Hygiene: Maintain excellent oral hygiene to reduce bacterial load, even if the child is not feeding orally.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Stay Calm and Act Quickly: In an emergency, maintain composure to effectively implement the emergency plan.
  • Call Emergency Services: Immediately call 911 or your local emergency number if the child is in severe respiratory distress or the tracheostomy tube is dislodged and cannot be reinserted.
  • Follow Emergency Plan: Adhere strictly to the individualized tracheostomy emergency plan provided by the medical team.
  • Do Not Leave Unattended: Never leave a child experiencing a tracheostomy emergency alone, even for a moment.
  • Proper Suctioning Technique: Use sterile technique and appropriate catheter size for suctioning, avoiding deep or prolonged suctioning that can cause hypoxia.
  • Emergency Tube Change: Be prepared to perform an emergency tracheostomy tube change if trained and indicated, using the spare tube and obturator.
  • CPR for Tracheostomy Patients: If CPR is needed, provide breaths directly through the tracheostomy tube, ensuring a good seal.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Individualized Emergency Plan: Every child with a tracheostomy requires a detailed, written emergency plan tailored to their specific needs and tube type.
  • Regular Follow-up: Consistent follow-up with a multidisciplinary team (pulmonology, ENT, speech therapy) is crucial for optimal management and prevention of emergencies.
  • Caregiver Training: Comprehensive training for all caregivers on tracheostomy care, suctioning, and emergency procedures is non-negotiable.
  • Recognizing Subtle Signs: Educate caregivers to recognize subtle changes in the child's condition that may precede a full-blown emergency.
  • When to Seek Immediate Help: Advise immediate emergency department evaluation for any unexplained respiratory distress, persistent fever, or significant changes at the stoma site.
  • Emergency Tube Change Protocol: Provide clear guidelines on when and how to perform an emergency tube change, emphasizing the use of the obturator and proper insertion technique.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Tracheostomy Care Protocols: Adhere to established hospital protocols for routine tracheostomy care, including cleaning, dressing changes, and tie changes.
  • Suctioning Techniques: Master sterile suctioning techniques, understanding appropriate catheter sizes, suction pressures, and duration to prevent complications.
  • Stoma Assessment: Regularly assess the tracheostomy stoma for signs of infection, skin breakdown, granulation tissue, or bleeding.
  • Emergency Equipment Checks: Ensure all emergency tracheostomy equipment is present, functional, and correctly sized at the bedside and in the emergency kit.
  • Caregiver Education: Provide thorough, hands-on education to parents and caregivers on all aspects of tracheostomy care and emergency management.
  • Recognizing Respiratory Compromise: Develop keen assessment skills to identify early signs of respiratory distress or tracheostomy complications in pediatric patients.
  • Communication: Facilitate clear communication between the medical team, child, and family regarding the tracheostomy care plan and any changes.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Tracheostomy Tube Types: Understanding differences between cuffed, uncuffed, fenestrated, and non-fenestrated tubes, and their indications.
  • Obturator: A guide used to facilitate insertion of a new tracheostomy tube, removed immediately after placement.
  • Suction Catheter Sizes: Selection of appropriate catheter size (e.g., 2/3 the internal diameter of the tracheostomy tube) to prevent airway trauma.
  • Bag-Valve Mask (BVM): Manual resuscitation device used to deliver positive pressure ventilation, essential for airway emergencies.
  • Stoma: The surgically created opening in the neck leading to the trachea.
  • Tracheomalacia: Weakness of the tracheal walls, potentially leading to airway collapse, especially in tracheostomy patients.
  • Granulation Tissue: Excessive tissue growth around the stoma, which can cause bleeding or obstruct the airway.
  • Decannulation: Accidental or planned removal of the tracheostomy tube.
  • Humidification: The process of adding moisture to inspired air to prevent drying of respiratory secretions.
