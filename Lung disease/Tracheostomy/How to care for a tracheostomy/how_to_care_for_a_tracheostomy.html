How to care for a tracheostomy

🏠 Home / Lung disease / Tracheostomy

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Purpose of Tracheostomy: A surgical opening in the neck into the trachea to create an alternative airway, often for long-term ventilation, managing secretions, or bypassing upper airway obstruction.
  • Importance of Care: Meticulous daily care is crucial to prevent infection, maintain airway patency, and ensure patient comfort and safety.
  • Team Approach: Care often involves the patient, family, nurses, respiratory therapists, and physicians working collaboratively.
  • Temporary vs. Permanent: Tracheostomies can be temporary, for acute conditions, or permanent, for chronic respiratory needs.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Difficulty Breathing: Persistent respiratory distress, stridor, or signs of airway obstruction that necessitate tracheostomy placement.
  • Excessive Secretions: Inability to effectively clear respiratory secretions, leading to aspiration risk or airway blockage.
  • Prolonged Ventilation Needs: Requirement for long-term mechanical ventilation due to respiratory failure or neurological conditions.
  • Signs of Infection: Redness, swelling, warmth, pain, or purulent discharge around the stoma site, indicating a potential infection requiring immediate attention.
  • Tube Obstruction: Increased work of breathing, noisy breathing, or coughing associated with a blocked or partially blocked tracheostomy tube.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Strict Hand Hygiene: Always perform thorough handwashing or use an alcohol-based hand sanitizer before and after any tracheostomy care.
  • Maintain Sterile Technique: Use sterile gloves and equipment for suctioning and dressing changes to minimize infection risk.
  • Secure the Tracheostomy Tube: Ensure the tracheostomy ties or holder are snug but not overly tight, allowing for two fingers to fit underneath, to prevent accidental dislodgement.
  • Emergency Equipment at Bedside: Always have a spare tracheostomy tube (same size and one size smaller), obturator, suction machine, and oxygen readily available.
  • Monitor for Respiratory Distress: Continuously observe for signs of difficulty breathing, cyanosis, or changes in respiratory rate and effort.
  • Prevent Aspiration: Elevate the head of the bed during and after meals, and ensure proper cuff inflation if applicable, to reduce the risk of food or fluid entering the airway.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Swallowing Assessment: A speech-language pathologist should assess swallowing function due to potential dysphagia associated with the tracheostomy tube.
  • Modified Diet Consistency: Follow recommendations for thickened liquids or pureed/soft foods if dysphagia is present to prevent aspiration.
  • Upright Positioning: Maintain an upright position (at least 45-60 degrees) during all oral intake and for at least 30-60 minutes afterward.
  • Small, Frequent Meals: Offer smaller portions more frequently to reduce fatigue and the risk of aspiration.
  • Oral Hygiene: Perform meticulous oral care regularly to reduce bacterial load and prevent aspiration pneumonia, even if the patient is NPO.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Caregiver Training: All primary caregivers must receive comprehensive training on tracheostomy care, suctioning, and emergency procedures from a qualified healthcare professional.
  • Emergency Protocol: Understand and practice the emergency protocol for tracheostomy tube dislodgement or obstruction, including how to reinsert the tube or call for immediate medical help.
  • Maintain a Clean Environment: Keep the patient's living space clean and free of dust and irritants to minimize respiratory complications.
  • Recognize Warning Signs: Be vigilant for signs of infection, respiratory distress, or changes in the stoma site and report them promptly to the healthcare team.
  • Communication Strategies: Learn and utilize effective communication methods with the patient, such as writing boards, speaking valves (if appropriate), or gestures.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Adherence to Care Plan: Strict adherence to the prescribed tracheostomy care regimen is paramount for preventing complications and ensuring optimal patient outcomes.
  • Regular Follow-up: Schedule and attend all recommended follow-up appointments to monitor the stoma, tube condition, and overall respiratory status.
  • Monitor for Complications: Be aware of potential complications such as stoma infection, granulation tissue formation, tracheal stenosis, or tracheoesophageal fistula, and report any concerns immediately.
  • Weaning Considerations: Discuss the possibility and process of tracheostomy weaning and decannulation with the medical team when appropriate, based on the patient's clinical stability.
  • Humidification: Ensure adequate humidification of inspired air to prevent drying of secretions and tracheal irritation.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Stoma Assessment: Regularly assess the stoma site for redness, swelling, discharge, skin breakdown, or signs of infection.
  • Suctioning Technique: Perform sterile suctioning using appropriate catheter size, depth, and negative pressure, limiting suction time to 10-15 seconds per pass.
  • Inner Cannula Care: Clean or replace the inner cannula as per protocol to prevent mucus plugging and maintain airway patency.
  • Tracheostomy Tie/Dressing Change: Change tracheostomy ties or holders and dressings when soiled or at least daily, ensuring the tube remains secure during the process.
  • Patient Education: Educate patients and families on self-care techniques, signs of complications, and when to seek medical attention.
  • Communication Facilitation: Assist patients with communication by providing appropriate tools and support, recognizing the psychological impact of a tracheostomy.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Tracheostomy Tube Types: Understand the differences between cuffed, uncuffed, fenestrated, and metal tracheostomy tubes and their indications.
  • Humidification Methods: Utilize heat and moisture exchangers (HMEs), nebulizers, or heated humidifiers to provide adequate moisture to the airway.
  • Speaking Valves: Familiarize with the use and contraindications of speaking valves (e.g., Passy-Muir Valve) to facilitate verbal communication.
  • Decannulation Criteria: Understand the clinical criteria and procedures involved in the decannulation process, including cuff deflation trials and capping trials.
  • Emergency Airway Management: Be proficient in emergency tracheostomy tube changes, including the use of an obturator and understanding the role of a tracheostomy dilator kit.
  • Stoma Care Products: Knowledge of various stoma dressings, cleaning solutions (e.g., normal saline), and skin protectants used in tracheostomy care.
