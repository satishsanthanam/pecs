Diagnostic bronchoscopy

🏠 Home / Lung disease / Tests and procedures

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Procedure Overview: Diagnostic bronchoscopy is a procedure where a thin, flexible tube (bronchoscope) is inserted through the nose or mouth into the airways to visualize the trachea and bronchi.
  • Purpose: It is performed to examine the airways for abnormalities, collect tissue samples (biopsy), fluid (bronchoalveolar lavage), or cells (brushings) to diagnose various lung conditions.
  • Sedation: The procedure is typically performed under moderate sedation to ensure patient comfort and minimize discomfort.
  • Duration: The procedure usually takes 30-60 minutes, but preparation and recovery time will extend the overall visit.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Persistent Cough: Unexplained chronic cough that does not respond to conventional treatments.
  • Hemoptysis: Coughing up blood or blood-tinged sputum.
  • Abnormal Chest Imaging: Suspicious findings on chest X-ray or CT scan, such as masses, nodules, infiltrates, or enlarged lymph nodes.
  • Unexplained Shortness of Breath: Dyspnea without a clear cause, especially if progressive.
  • Recurrent Lung Infections: Repeated bouts of pneumonia or bronchitis that suggest an underlying airway obstruction or abnormality.
  • Stridor or Wheezing: New onset or persistent abnormal breath sounds.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Fasting Requirements: Strict NPO (nothing by mouth) for at least 6-8 hours prior to the procedure to prevent aspiration during sedation.
  • Medication Review: Inform your physician about all medications, especially blood thinners (anticoagulants/antiplatelets), which may need to be stopped several days before.
  • Allergies: Disclose any known allergies to medications, latex, or local anesthetics.
  • Respiratory Status: The procedure may be contraindicated or require careful consideration in patients with severe hypoxemia, unstable cardiac conditions, or uncorrected coagulopathy.
  • Post-Procedure Monitoring: Closely monitor for signs of complications such as difficulty breathing, chest pain, fever, or significant hemoptysis.
  • Avoid Driving: Due to sedation, arrange for a responsible adult to drive you home and stay with you for the first 24 hours.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Pre-Procedure Fasting: Absolutely no food or drink, including water, for 6-8 hours before the scheduled procedure time. This is critical to prevent aspiration.
  • Post-Sedation Reintroduction: Once your gag reflex has fully returned (typically 1-2 hours post-procedure), you may start with sips of water or clear liquids.
  • Gradual Progression: If clear liquids are tolerated, you can gradually progress to soft, bland foods. Avoid hot liquids, spicy foods, or anything that might irritate your throat for the first 24 hours.
  • Alcohol Restriction: Avoid alcohol for at least 24 hours after the procedure, as it can interact with residual sedative effects.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Transportation: A designated driver is mandatory to transport the patient home after the procedure due to the effects of sedation. Public transport or taxis are not permissible unless accompanied by a responsible adult.
  • Post-Procedure Supervision: A responsible adult must remain with the patient for at least 24 hours post-procedure to monitor for any adverse effects or complications.
  • Emergency Contact: Ensure the attendant has contact information for the medical team and knows when to seek emergency care (e.g., severe breathing difficulty, significant bleeding, persistent chest pain).
  • Activity Restriction: The patient should avoid making important decisions, operating machinery, or engaging in strenuous activities for 24 hours following sedation.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Informed Consent: It is paramount that patients fully understand the indications, risks, benefits, and alternatives to diagnostic bronchoscopy before proceeding.
  • Risk-Benefit Assessment: Each case requires a careful assessment of the potential diagnostic yield against the inherent risks, especially in patients with significant comorbidities.
  • Pre-Procedure Optimization: Ensure any underlying conditions, such as coagulopathy or respiratory insufficiency, are optimized prior to the procedure to minimize complications.
  • Post-Procedure Communication: Clear communication regarding expected recovery, potential complications, and when to expect results is crucial for patient reassurance and compliance.
  • Multidisciplinary Approach: Collaboration with pulmonologists, radiologists, and pathologists is essential for accurate diagnosis and subsequent management.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Pre-Procedure Assessment: Verify NPO status, confirm informed consent, assess vital signs, review medication history, and identify any allergies.
  • Patient Education: Educate the patient on what to expect during and after the procedure, including the sensation of throat numbness and potential for a sore throat.
  • Intra-Procedure Monitoring: Continuously monitor vital signs, oxygen saturation, cardiac rhythm, and level of sedation. Assist the physician with specimen collection and bronchoscope manipulation.
  • Post-Procedure Care: Prioritize airway management, assess for return of gag reflex, monitor for signs of respiratory distress, bleeding, or pneumothorax. Provide clear discharge instructions.
  • Specimen Handling: Ensure proper labeling, handling, and timely transport of all collected specimens (BAL, biopsies, brushings) to pathology.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Indications: Evaluation of persistent cough, hemoptysis, abnormal chest imaging (masses, infiltrates, lymphadenopathy), suspected malignancy, interstitial lung disease, infectious processes, and airway obstruction.
  • Techniques: Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL), endobronchial biopsy (EBB), transbronchial biopsy (TBB), bronchial brushings, transbronchial needle aspiration (TBNA), endobronchial ultrasound (EBUS)-guided biopsy.
  • Potential Complications: Pneumothorax, hemorrhage, infection, laryngospasm, bronchospasm, hypoxemia, adverse reaction to sedation, vocal cord paralysis (rare).
  • Equipment: Flexible fiberoptic or video bronchoscope, light source, suction apparatus, biopsy forceps, cytology brushes, lavage fluid, local anesthetic (e.g., lidocaine).
  • Anesthesia: Topical anesthesia of the upper airway combined with conscious sedation (e.g., midazolam and fentanyl) is commonly used.
