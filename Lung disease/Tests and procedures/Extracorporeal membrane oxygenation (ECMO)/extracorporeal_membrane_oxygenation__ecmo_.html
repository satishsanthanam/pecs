Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO)

🏠 Home / Lung disease / Tests and procedures

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Life Support System: Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) is an advanced life support technique that provides prolonged cardiac and respiratory support to patients whose heart and lungs are unable to provide an adequate amount of gas exchange or perfusion to sustain life.
  • Temporary Organ Support: ECMO acts as an artificial lung and/or heart, allowing the patient's own organs to rest and heal from severe illness or injury.
  • Two Main Types: Veno-venous (VV) ECMO primarily supports the lungs by oxygenating blood and removing carbon dioxide. Veno-arterial (VA) ECMO supports both the heart and lungs by oxygenating blood and providing circulatory support.
  • Critical Care Intervention: It is typically used in intensive care units for critically ill patients with severe, life-threatening heart or lung failure refractory to conventional medical management.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Severe Respiratory Distress: Profound shortness of breath, rapid breathing, and gasping for air despite maximal oxygen therapy and mechanical ventilation.
  • Refractory Hypoxemia: Dangerously low blood oxygen levels that do not improve with conventional ventilator settings.
  • Hypercapnia: Excessively high carbon dioxide levels in the blood, indicating severe respiratory failure.
  • Cardiogenic Shock: Signs of severe heart failure, including very low blood pressure, rapid heart rate, and poor organ perfusion.
  • Cyanosis: Bluish discoloration of the skin and mucous membranes due to inadequate oxygenation.
  • Altered Mental Status: Confusion, lethargy, or unresponsiveness due to severe hypoxemia or hypercapnia.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Anticoagulation Management: Patients on ECMO require continuous anticoagulation to prevent blood clots within the circuit, which carries a significant risk of bleeding complications, including intracranial hemorrhage.
  • Infection Prevention: Strict aseptic technique is paramount during cannula insertion and maintenance to prevent central line-associated bloodstream infections (CLABSI) and other healthcare-associated infections.
  • Neurological Monitoring: Continuous assessment for signs of stroke, seizures, or intracranial hemorrhage is vital due to the risks associated with anticoagulation and systemic inflammation.
  • Cannula Site Care: Meticulous care of the insertion sites is crucial to prevent infection, bleeding, and accidental dislodgement of the cannulae.
  • Hemodynamic Stability: Close monitoring and management of blood pressure, heart rate, and fluid balance are essential to maintain adequate organ perfusion and prevent complications.
  • Sedation and Analgesia: Appropriate sedation and pain management are necessary to ensure patient comfort, minimize agitation, and prevent self-extubation or cannula dislodgement.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Nutritional Support: Patients on ECMO are typically critically ill and unable to eat orally. Nutritional support is provided via enteral feeding (through a nasogastric or orogastric tube) or parenteral nutrition (intravenous feeding).
  • Strict NPO Status: Oral intake is strictly prohibited due to intubation, sedation, and the risk of aspiration or gastrointestinal complications.
  • Fluid Management: Fluid intake is meticulously monitored and often restricted to prevent fluid overload, which can worsen lung function and cardiac strain.
  • Electrolyte Balance: Close attention is paid to electrolyte levels, which are frequently deranged in critically ill patients and require careful repletion or restriction.
  • Gut Perfusion Assessment: Regular assessment of gut function is performed, as compromised perfusion can necessitate adjustments to feeding strategies.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Limited Visitation: Due to the critical nature of the patient's condition and the need for a sterile environment, visitation may be restricted to immediate family members for short periods.
  • Do Not Touch Equipment: Family members must be instructed not to touch any part of the ECMO machine, tubing, or patient's lines without explicit permission and guidance from the nursing staff.
  • Call for Assistance: Any alarms, changes in the patient's appearance, or concerns should be immediately reported to the bedside nurse.
  • Emotional Support: Families should be encouraged to seek emotional support and utilize hospital resources, as ECMO is a stressful and often prolonged treatment.
  • Communication with Team: Regular updates from the medical team will be provided; families should designate a primary contact person for communication.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Last-Resort Intervention: ECMO is a highly specialized and resource-intensive therapy reserved for patients with severe, life-threatening cardiac or respiratory failure who have exhausted conventional treatments.
  • Multidisciplinary Team: Successful ECMO management requires a dedicated multidisciplinary team, including intensivists, cardiothoracic surgeons, perfusionists, nurses, and respiratory therapists.
  • Complex Decision-Making: The decision to initiate or withdraw ECMO involves careful consideration of the patient's underlying condition, prognosis, potential for recovery, and risk of complications.
  • Bridge to Recovery or Transplant: ECMO serves as a bridge to recovery, bridge to transplant (heart or lung), or bridge to another long-term mechanical support device.
  • High Risk, High Reward: While ECMO can be life-saving, it carries significant risks of severe complications, including bleeding, thrombosis, infection, and neurological injury.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Continuous Monitoring: Meticulous monitoring of vital signs, ECMO circuit parameters (flows, pressures, oxygenator function), arterial blood gases, activated clotting time (ACT), and complete blood counts is essential.
  • Anticoagulation Management: Administering and titrating anticoagulants (e.g., heparin) based on ACT or anti-Xa levels, and monitoring for signs of bleeding or clotting.
  • Cannula Site Assessment: Regular assessment of cannula insertion sites for bleeding, swelling, signs of infection, and proper positioning.
  • Medication Administration: Managing continuous infusions of sedatives, analgesics, vasopressors, and other critical medications.
  • Repositioning and Skin Care: Frequent repositioning and meticulous skin care to prevent pressure injuries, especially given the patient's immobility and critical status.
  • Troubleshooting Alarms: Understanding and responding promptly to ECMO circuit alarms, identifying potential issues such as air in the circuit, low flow, or high pressures.
  • Family Support: Providing empathetic support and clear, concise education to family members about the ECMO process and patient's condition.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • ECMO Circuit Components: Consists of cannulae (venous and/or arterial), a pump (centrifugal or roller), an oxygenator (membrane lung), and a heat exchanger.
  • Physiological Principles: Mimics the function of the lungs (gas exchange: oxygenation and CO2 removal) and/or heart (circulatory support: blood flow and pressure).
  • Indications: Severe Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS), refractory cardiogenic shock, bridge to lung/heart transplant, severe pneumonia, massive pulmonary embolism, cardiac arrest (ECPR).
  • Contraindications: Irreversible underlying disease with poor prognosis, severe neurological injury, uncontrolled systemic bleeding, prolonged CPR without return of spontaneous circulation.
  • Common Complications: Hemorrhage (most common), thrombosis (circuit or patient), infection, neurological events (stroke, seizures), limb ischemia, renal failure, hemolysis.
  • Decannulation Criteria: Weaning from ECMO is considered when the patient's native heart and/or lung function shows significant improvement, evidenced by stable hemodynamics, adequate gas exchange on minimal ventilator settings, and reduced vasopressor support.
