Small bowel resection

🏠 Home / Digestive system / Tests and procedures

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Procedure Overview: Small bowel resection is a surgical procedure to remove a diseased or damaged section of the small intestine.
  • Primary Indications: Commonly performed for conditions such as Crohn's disease, tumors (benign or malignant), bowel obstruction, diverticulitis, or injuries to the small intestine.
  • Surgical Approaches: Can be performed via open surgery (laparotomy) or minimally invasive laparoscopic techniques, depending on the complexity and patient condition.
  • Anastomosis: After removal, the remaining healthy ends of the small intestine are reconnected (anastomosis) to restore continuity of the digestive tract.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Persistent Abdominal Pain: Chronic or acute severe abdominal pain, often localized or cramping, which may indicate inflammation, obstruction, or mass.
  • Unexplained Weight Loss: Significant and unintentional weight loss, potentially due to malabsorption, inflammation, or malignancy affecting the small bowel.
  • Bowel Obstruction Signs: Nausea, vomiting, abdominal distension, inability to pass gas or stool, suggesting a blockage in the small intestine.
  • Gastrointestinal Bleeding: Presence of blood in stool (melena or hematochezia) or anemia due to chronic blood loss from lesions in the small bowel.
  • Signs of Perforation: Sudden, severe abdominal pain, rigidity, and fever, indicating a potential rupture of the bowel wall.
  • Symptoms of Crohn's Disease: Chronic diarrhea, fatigue, fever, and abdominal pain, often leading to strictures or fistulas requiring resection.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Pre-operative Medication Review: Disclose all medications, especially anticoagulants (blood thinners), NSAIDs, and supplements, as they may need to be stopped prior to surgery.
  • Allergy Disclosure: Inform the medical team of all known allergies to medications, latex, or other substances.
  • Bowel Preparation: Follow specific instructions for bowel cleansing, which may include a clear liquid diet and laxatives, to reduce infection risk.
  • Post-operative Incision Care: Keep the surgical incision clean and dry; monitor for signs of infection such as redness, swelling, warmth, or discharge.
  • Monitoring for Complications: Be vigilant for signs of anastomotic leak (severe abdominal pain, fever, tachycardia), ileus (prolonged absence of bowel function), or deep vein thrombosis (DVT).
  • Activity Restrictions: Avoid heavy lifting and strenuous activities for several weeks post-surgery to prevent strain on the surgical site.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Pre-operative Fasting: Strictly adhere to NPO (nothing by mouth) guidelines for the specified period before surgery, typically 6-8 hours, to prevent aspiration.
  • Post-operative Diet Progression: Diet will gradually advance from NPO to clear liquids, then full liquids, soft foods, and finally a low-residue diet as tolerated.
  • Avoid Irritating Foods: Initially, avoid high-fiber foods, greasy or fried foods, spicy foods, and caffeine, which can irritate the healing bowel.
  • Small, Frequent Meals: Consume smaller, more frequent meals to aid digestion and reduce the burden on the healing intestine.
  • Adequate Hydration: Maintain good hydration by drinking plenty of fluids, as directed by the healthcare team.
  • Nutritional Supplementation: Depending on the extent of resection, vitamin B12, fat-soluble vitamins, or other supplements may be necessary to prevent deficiencies.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Monitor for Fever and Chills: Immediately report any elevation in body temperature or chills, which could indicate an infection.
  • Observe Wound Site: Regularly check the surgical incision for increased redness, swelling, warmth, pus-like drainage, or opening of the wound.
  • Pain Management: Assist the patient with prescribed pain medications and report if pain is uncontrolled or worsening.
  • Encourage Mobility: Help the patient with early and frequent ambulation as instructed, to prevent complications like DVT and promote bowel function.
  • Report Changes in Bowel Habits: Note and report any prolonged absence of bowel movements, severe diarrhea, or persistent nausea and vomiting.
  • Emergency Signs: Seek immediate medical attention for severe, sudden abdominal pain, difficulty breathing, heavy bleeding from the incision, or signs of shock.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Surgical Indication: Small bowel resection is a definitive treatment for various conditions when conservative management fails or complications arise.
  • Risk-Benefit Assessment: The decision for surgery involves a careful evaluation of the patient's overall health, the extent of the disease, and potential surgical risks versus benefits.
  • Post-operative Surveillance: Close monitoring for complications such as anastomotic leak, infection, ileus, and nutritional deficiencies is crucial in the immediate post-operative period.
  • Long-term Management: Patients may require ongoing medical management for underlying conditions (e.g., Crohn's disease) and nutritional support, especially in cases of extensive resection.
  • Prognosis: The prognosis is generally good, but it varies depending on the underlying condition, the extent of the resection, and the presence of complications.
  • Follow-up Care: Regular follow-up appointments are essential to monitor recovery, assess bowel function, and address any long-term issues.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Pre-operative Teaching: Educate patients on the procedure, expected post-operative course, pain management, and importance of early ambulation.
  • Fluid and Electrolyte Balance: Monitor IV fluid administration, urine output, and electrolyte levels, especially in patients with prolonged NPO status or high output from drains.
  • Pain Assessment and Management: Utilize appropriate pain scales and administer analgesics as prescribed, evaluating their effectiveness and managing side effects.
  • Wound Care Principles: Perform sterile dressing changes, assess the incision for signs of infection, and educate patients on self-care upon discharge.
  • Bowel Function Monitoring: Assess for return of bowel sounds, passage of flatus, and first bowel movement to indicate resolution of post-operative ileus.
  • Nutritional Support: Collaborate with dietitians to ensure appropriate dietary progression and identify patients at risk for malnutrition or short bowel syndrome.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Anastomosis: Surgical connection of two tubular structures, in this case, the ends of the small intestine.
  • Ileus: A temporary arrest of intestinal peristalsis, common after abdominal surgery.
  • Short Bowel Syndrome (SBS): A malabsorption disorder caused by the surgical removal of a large segment of the small intestine.
  • Crohn's Disease: A chronic inflammatory bowel disease that can affect any part of the gastrointestinal tract, often requiring resection.
  • Mesenteric Ischemia: Reduced blood flow to the small intestine, which can lead to tissue damage and necessitate resection.
  • Adhesions: Bands of scar tissue that can form after surgery, potentially causing bowel obstruction.
  • Laparoscopy: A minimally invasive surgical technique using small incisions and a camera.
  • Enterostomy: A surgical procedure to create an opening from the small intestine to the outside of the body, forming a stoma (e.g., ileostomy).
