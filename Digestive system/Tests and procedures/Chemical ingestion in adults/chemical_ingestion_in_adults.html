Chemical ingestion in adults

🏠 Home / Digestive system / Tests and procedures

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Immediate Danger: Chemical ingestion can cause severe burns to the esophagus, stomach, and other internal organs, leading to perforation, bleeding, and systemic toxicity.
  • Types of Chemicals: Common culprits include household cleaners (bleach, drain cleaner), industrial solvents, pesticides, and certain medications.
  • Urgency is Key: Rapid identification of the substance and immediate medical attention are critical to minimize damage and improve outcomes.
  • Do NOT Induce Vomiting: Re-exposure to the chemical during vomiting can cause further injury to the esophagus and increase the risk of aspiration into the lungs.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Oral & Pharyngeal Pain: Severe burning pain in the mouth, throat, and chest immediately after ingestion.
  • Dysphagia & Odynophagia: Difficulty and pain with swallowing, often leading to refusal to eat or drink.
  • Gastrointestinal Distress: Abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting (which may contain blood or chemical residue), and hematemesis.
  • Respiratory Symptoms: Hoarseness, stridor, shortness of breath, or coughing if aspiration has occurred or if there is laryngeal edema.
  • Systemic Signs: Dizziness, confusion, shock, metabolic acidosis, or signs of organ damage depending on the chemical's toxicity.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Secure the Substance: Immediately remove the patient from further exposure and secure the chemical container for identification by medical personnel.
  • Avoid Neutralization: Do not attempt to neutralize the chemical with acids or bases, as this can generate heat and worsen tissue damage.
  • No Forced Fluids: Do not give water or milk unless specifically instructed by poison control, as it can induce vomiting or spread the chemical.
  • Airway Protection: Be prepared for potential airway compromise due to swelling; monitor for signs of respiratory distress.
  • Personal Protective Equipment: First responders should wear appropriate PPE to prevent secondary exposure to the chemical.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Strict NPO Status: The patient must remain NPO (Nil Per Os) immediately after ingestion until a thorough medical assessment, including endoscopy, determines the extent of injury.
  • Avoid Oral Intake: Do not offer any food or drink, as this can exacerbate injury, induce vomiting, or increase the risk of aspiration.
  • IV Hydration: Hydration and nutritional support will be provided intravenously until oral intake is deemed safe.
  • Gradual Reintroduction: If no significant injury is found or once initial healing occurs, oral intake may be gradually reintroduced, starting with clear liquids, then soft foods, under strict medical supervision.
  • Avoid Irritants Post-Injury: Once oral intake is permitted, avoid acidic, spicy, very hot, or very cold foods and beverages that could irritate damaged tissues.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Call Emergency Services: Immediately dial 911 or your local emergency number and activate poison control.
  • Provide Information: Clearly state what was ingested, how much, when, and any observed symptoms. Have the chemical container ready for reference.
  • Do NOT Induce Vomiting: Reiterate this crucial instruction to anyone assisting the patient.
  • Monitor & Reassure: Keep the patient calm, monitor their breathing and level of consciousness, and position them comfortably, ideally on their side if vomiting occurs.
  • Do NOT Transport Alone: Wait for emergency medical personnel; do not attempt to drive the patient to the hospital yourself unless instructed to do so.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Rapid Assessment & Stabilization: Prioritize airway, breathing, and circulation (ABC) management, including intubation if necessary for airway protection.
  • Early Endoscopy: Esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) is crucial within 12-24 hours to assess the extent and grade of mucosal injury, guiding treatment decisions.
  • Imaging Studies: Chest X-ray or CT scan may be performed to rule out aspiration pneumonitis or perforation.
  • Supportive Care: Aggressive pain management, fluid resuscitation, antiemetics, and proton pump inhibitors are often initiated.
  • Surgical Consultation: Early consultation with surgery is vital for suspected perforation, severe necrosis, or impending complications.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Detailed History Taking: Obtain a precise history of the ingested substance, quantity, time, and any first-aid measures attempted.
  • Continuous Monitoring: Closely monitor vital signs, respiratory status, pain levels, and signs of gastrointestinal bleeding or perforation.
  • IV Access & Fluid Management: Establish secure IV access for fluid resuscitation and medication administration.
  • Patient & Family Education: Provide clear instructions on NPO status, explain procedures, and offer psychological support, especially in cases of intentional ingestion.
  • Long-Term Follow-up: Educate on the importance of follow-up for potential complications like esophageal strictures or increased cancer risk.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Caustic Ingestion: Injury caused by strong acids (coagulation necrosis) or alkalis (liquefaction necrosis), with alkalis typically causing deeper, more extensive damage.
  • Esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD): The gold standard for evaluating the severity and extent of upper gastrointestinal tract injury.
  • Zargar Classification: An endoscopic grading system (Grade 0 to IV) used to classify the severity of caustic esophageal injury.
  • Mediastinitis/Peritonitis: Life-threatening complications resulting from esophageal or gastric perforation, requiring immediate surgical intervention.
  • Toxicology Screen: Laboratory analysis of blood or urine to identify and quantify ingested substances, especially in cases of unknown or multiple ingestions.
