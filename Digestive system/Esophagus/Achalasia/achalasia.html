Achalasia

🏠 Home / Digestive system / Esophagus

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Definition: Achalasia is a rare, chronic esophageal motility disorder characterized by the inability of the lower esophageal sphincter (LES) to relax and the absence of effective peristalsis in the esophageal body.
  • Pathophysiology: It results from the degeneration of inhibitory neurons in the myenteric (Auerbach's) plexus of the esophagus, leading to a functional obstruction.
  • Consequence: Food and liquids accumulate in the esophagus, causing it to dilate over time, often referred to as a 'megaesophagus'.
  • Progression: The condition is progressive, with symptoms typically worsening over months to years if left untreated.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Dysphagia: Difficulty swallowing both solids and liquids, often progressive and the most common symptom.
  • Regurgitation: Undigested food and saliva returning to the mouth, especially after meals or at night, posing an aspiration risk.
  • Chest Pain: Non-cardiac chest pain or discomfort, often described as a squeezing or burning sensation behind the sternum.
  • Heartburn (Pyrosis): Despite the LES not relaxing, patients may experience heartburn-like symptoms due to esophageal irritation from retained food.
  • Weight Loss: Significant and unintentional weight loss due to reduced oral intake and fear of eating.
  • Coughing/Choking: Especially at night, due to aspiration of regurgitated esophageal contents into the trachea.
  • Halitosis: Bad breath caused by the fermentation of retained food in the dilated esophagus.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Aspiration Risk: Patients are at high risk for aspiration pneumonia, especially during sleep. Elevate the head of the bed and avoid eating close to bedtime.
  • Esophageal Perforation: A significant risk during endoscopic procedures like pneumatic dilation or peroral endoscopic myotomy (POEM). Monitor for severe pain, fever, or signs of sepsis post-procedure.
  • Nutritional Deficiencies: Monitor for malnutrition and weight loss. Nutritional support may be required in severe cases.
  • Esophageal Cancer Surveillance: Long-standing achalasia is associated with an increased risk of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma. Regular endoscopic surveillance may be recommended.
  • Medication Interactions: Be aware of potential interactions with medications that affect esophageal motility or LES pressure, though pharmacological treatments for achalasia are limited.
  • Post-Procedure Monitoring: After any intervention (dilation, myotomy), closely monitor for signs of bleeding, infection, or persistent dysphagia.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Small, Frequent Meals: Consume smaller portions more frequently to reduce the volume of food in the esophagus at one time.
  • Soft, Pureed Foods: Prioritize foods with a soft or liquid consistency that are easier to swallow and pass through the constricted LES.
  • Thorough Chewing: Chew food meticulously to a fine consistency before attempting to swallow.
  • Drink Water with Meals: Sip water or other liquids during meals to help wash food down the esophagus.
  • Avoid Trigger Foods: Identify and avoid foods that exacerbate symptoms, such as very spicy, acidic, extremely hot, or very cold items.
  • Elevate Head During/After Eating: Remain upright for at least 2-3 hours after meals to aid gravity in food passage and reduce regurgitation.
  • Avoid Late-Night Eating: Refrain from eating within 3-4 hours of bedtime to minimize nocturnal regurgitation and aspiration risk.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Patient Education: Provide comprehensive education on the nature of achalasia, its progressive course, and the importance of adherence to dietary and lifestyle modifications.
  • Aspiration Precautions: Instruct patients and caregivers on recognizing signs of aspiration and implementing strategies to minimize risk, such as upright positioning during and after meals.
  • Post-Procedure Care: Detail specific post-operative or post-procedural instructions, including pain management, diet progression, and signs of complications requiring immediate medical attention.
  • Medication Management: Explain the purpose and proper administration of any prescribed medications, even if they offer only symptomatic relief.
  • Psychological Support: Acknowledge the significant impact of chronic dysphagia on quality of life and offer resources for psychological support if needed.
  • Emergency Preparedness: Advise on when to seek urgent medical care for severe chest pain, persistent vomiting, or signs of esophageal perforation.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Diagnostic Approach: High-resolution esophageal manometry is the gold standard for diagnosis, demonstrating absent peristalsis and impaired LES relaxation. Barium swallow typically shows a 'bird's beak' appearance. Endoscopy is crucial to rule out pseudoachalasia caused by malignancy.
  • Treatment Goals: The primary goal is to relieve symptoms by reducing LES pressure, thereby improving esophageal emptying and preventing complications.
  • Treatment Modalities: Options include pharmacological agents (nitrates, calcium channel blockers for temporary relief), endoscopic interventions (botulinum toxin injection, pneumatic dilation, peroral endoscopic myotomy - POEM), and surgical myotomy (Laparoscopic Heller myotomy with partial fundoplication).
  • Individualized Care: Treatment selection is highly individualized, considering patient age, comorbidities, esophageal anatomy, and patient preference.
  • Long-term Management: Achalasia is a chronic condition requiring ongoing monitoring for symptom recurrence, complications, and the potential need for repeat interventions.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Pathophysiology Review: Understand the role of the myenteric plexus and the loss of nitric oxide-producing inhibitory neurons in the development of achalasia.
  • Nursing Assessment: Focus on detailed history taking regarding dysphagia (solids vs. liquids), regurgitation, weight loss, and aspiration events. Assess nutritional status.
  • Patient Education Focus: Emphasize practical strategies for eating, such as small bites, thorough chewing, drinking with meals, and maintaining an upright posture.
  • Post-Intervention Care: Monitor vital signs, pain levels, and signs of complications (e.g., fever, tachycardia, abdominal rigidity indicating perforation) after pneumatic dilation or myotomy.
  • Aspiration Prevention: Implement and teach aspiration precautions, including head-of-bed elevation, thickened liquids if appropriate, and monitoring for respiratory distress.
  • Differential Diagnosis: Be aware of conditions that mimic achalasia, such as Chagas disease or pseudoachalasia caused by esophageal or gastric cardia malignancy.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • ICD-10 Code: K22.0 (Achalasia of cardia).
  • Gold Standard Diagnostic Test: High-resolution esophageal manometry.
  • Characteristic Barium Swallow Finding: 'Bird's beak' appearance of the distal esophagus.
  • Primary Surgical Treatment: Laparoscopic Heller myotomy, often combined with a partial fundoplication (e.g., Dor or Toupet) to prevent reflux.
  • Key Endoscopic Treatments: Pneumatic dilation, Peroral Endoscopic Myotomy (POEM), Botulinum toxin injection.
  • Associated Complications: Aspiration pneumonia, malnutrition, esophageal dilation (megaesophagus), increased risk of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma.
  • Pharmacological Agents (Limited Efficacy): Nitrates (e.g., isosorbide dinitrate), Calcium Channel Blockers (e.g., nifedipine) for temporary symptom relief.
