GI bleed

🏠 Home / Digestive system / GI bleeding

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Definition: Gastrointestinal (GI) bleeding refers to any bleeding that occurs within the digestive tract, from the esophagus to the rectum.
  • Types: Classified as upper GI bleed (esophagus, stomach, duodenum) or lower GI bleed (small intestine beyond duodenum, large intestine, rectum, anus).
  • Severity: Can range from microscopic, chronic blood loss to acute, life-threatening hemorrhage requiring immediate medical intervention.
  • Common Causes: Peptic ulcers, esophageal varices, gastritis, diverticulosis, hemorrhoids, inflammatory bowel disease, and certain medications like NSAIDs.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Hematemesis: Vomiting of fresh red blood or 'coffee-ground' material, typically indicating an upper GI bleed.
  • Melena: Black, tarry, foul-smelling stools, usually a sign of an upper GI bleed or slow bleeding from the small intestine.
  • Hematochezia: Bright red or maroon blood in the stool, commonly associated with lower GI bleeding.
  • Abdominal Pain: Discomfort, cramping, or tenderness in the abdomen, which may accompany the bleeding.
  • Signs of Blood Loss: Dizziness, lightheadedness, weakness, pallor, fatigue, shortness of breath, and syncope (fainting) due to anemia or hypovolemia.
  • Tachycardia & Hypotension: Increased heart rate and low blood pressure, indicating significant blood volume loss.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Emergency Care: Seek immediate medical attention for any signs of significant GI bleeding (e.g., vomiting blood, black tarry stools, large amounts of red blood in stool, dizziness).
  • Medication Review: Avoid over-the-counter NSAIDs (e.g., ibuprofen, naproxen) and aspirin unless specifically prescribed by a physician, especially if you have a history of ulcers or GI bleeding.
  • Anticoagulant Management: If on blood thinners, discuss with your doctor about the risks and benefits, and never stop them without medical advice.
  • Alcohol & Smoking Cessation: Limit or avoid alcohol consumption and smoking, as these can irritate the GI lining and exacerbate bleeding risks.
  • Underlying Condition Management: Adhere to treatment plans for conditions like peptic ulcer disease, GERD, or liver cirrhosis to prevent re-bleeding.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Acute Bleed: Nil per os (NPO) status is typically required until the source of bleeding is identified and controlled.
  • Post-Stabilization: Gradual reintroduction of clear liquids, progressing to a bland, soft diet as tolerated, under medical supervision.
  • Avoid Irritants: Strictly avoid alcohol, caffeine, spicy foods, highly acidic foods (e.g., citrus, tomatoes), and foods high in fat, which can irritate the GI lining.
  • Small, Frequent Meals: Once oral intake is permitted, consume small, frequent meals to minimize gastric distension and irritation.
  • Hydration: Maintain adequate hydration with non-irritating fluids once cleared by the medical team.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Monitor for Worsening: Observe the patient closely for changes in stool color/consistency, increased vomiting, signs of dizziness, confusion, or severe abdominal pain.
  • Medication Adherence: Ensure the patient takes all prescribed medications, especially proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) or H2 blockers, as directed.
  • Emotional Support: Provide reassurance and a calm environment, as GI bleeding can be a frightening experience for the patient.
  • Emergency Preparedness: Know how to contact emergency services and be prepared to provide relevant medical history.
  • Dietary Compliance: Strictly enforce dietary restrictions and progression as advised by the healthcare team.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Prompt Diagnosis: Early identification of the bleeding source via endoscopy (EGD for upper, colonoscopy for lower) or other imaging (e.g., CT angiography, capsule endoscopy) is crucial.
  • Hemodynamic Stabilization: Initial management focuses on fluid resuscitation, blood transfusions if necessary, and correction of coagulopathy.
  • Therapeutic Interventions: Endoscopic hemostasis (e.g., clipping, cautery, band ligation) is often the first-line treatment. Pharmacological agents (e.g., PPIs, octreotide) are used adjunctively.
  • Surgical Consultation: Surgery may be indicated for massive, refractory bleeding or when endoscopic methods fail.
  • Risk Stratification: Utilize scoring systems (e.g., Rockall, Blatchford) to assess re-bleeding risk and guide management decisions.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Assessment: Perform frequent vital sign monitoring, assess for signs of hypovolemic shock, and monitor characteristics of emesis and stool for blood.
  • Fluid Management: Administer intravenous fluids and blood products as ordered, monitoring for fluid overload.
  • Medication Administration: Administer prescribed medications (e.g., PPIs, antiemetics, vasopressin analogs) and monitor for efficacy and side effects.
  • Patient Education: Educate patients and families on signs of re-bleeding, medication adherence, dietary restrictions, and the importance of follow-up care.
  • Preparation for Procedures: Assist in preparing patients for endoscopic procedures, including NPO status and consent.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • ICD-10 Codes: K92.2 (Gastrointestinal hemorrhage, unspecified), K25.- (Gastric ulcer with hemorrhage), K26.- (Duodenal ulcer with hemorrhage), I85.01 (Esophageal varices with bleeding).
  • Key Medications: Proton Pump Inhibitors (PPIs - e.g., omeprazole, pantoprazole), H2 Receptor Blockers (e.g., famotidine), Octreotide, Vasopressin.
  • Diagnostic Procedures: Esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD), Colonoscopy, Capsule Endoscopy, Angiography, CT Angiography, Fecal Occult Blood Test (FOBT).
  • Scoring Systems: Rockall Score (for upper GI bleed risk assessment), Blatchford Score (for predicting need for intervention in upper GI bleed).
  • Medical Terms: Hematemesis, Melena, Hematochezia, Hypovolemic Shock, Peptic Ulcer Disease, Esophageal Varices, Diverticulosis.
