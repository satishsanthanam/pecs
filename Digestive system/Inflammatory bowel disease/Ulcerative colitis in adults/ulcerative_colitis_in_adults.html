Ulcerative colitis in adults

🏠 Home / Digestive system / Inflammatory bowel disease

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Chronic Inflammation: Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a chronic inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) affecting the lining of the large intestine (colon and rectum).
  • Continuous Spread: Inflammation typically starts in the rectum and spreads continuously upwards through the colon.
  • Ulcer Formation: Characterized by inflammation and the development of tiny ulcers on the inner lining of the colon.
  • Flare-Ups & Remission: The disease course involves periods of active symptoms (flare-ups) and periods of minimal or no symptoms (remission).
  • Autoimmune Component: Believed to involve an abnormal immune response in genetically susceptible individuals.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Bloody Diarrhea: Frequent, urgent bowel movements often containing blood and mucus.
  • Abdominal Pain: Cramping and discomfort, typically in the lower abdomen, which may be relieved by defecation.
  • Tenesmus: A persistent feeling of needing to pass stool, even when the bowel is empty.
  • Urgency: Sudden and strong urges to defecate, sometimes leading to incontinence.
  • Weight Loss: Unintentional weight loss due to malabsorption, decreased appetite, and chronic inflammation.
  • Fatigue: Persistent tiredness and lack of energy, often associated with anemia and chronic inflammation.
  • Anemia: Iron deficiency anemia resulting from chronic blood loss in the stool.
  • Extraintestinal Manifestations: Symptoms outside the digestive tract, such as joint pain (arthralgia/arthritis), skin lesions (erythema nodosum, pyoderma gangrenosum), and eye inflammation (uveitis, episcleritis).

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Medication Adherence: Strictly follow prescribed medication regimens (e.g., 5-ASAs, corticosteroids, immunomodulators, biologics) to induce and maintain remission and prevent disease progression.
  • Regular Endoscopic Surveillance: Adhere to scheduled colonoscopies, especially after 8-10 years of disease, due to an increased risk of colorectal cancer.
  • Flare-Up Recognition: Be vigilant for early signs of a flare-up (increased stool frequency, blood, pain) and contact your healthcare provider promptly for intervention.
  • Avoid NSAIDs: Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) can exacerbate UC symptoms and should generally be avoided; discuss pain relief alternatives with your physician.
  • Hydration: Maintain adequate fluid intake, particularly during active disease, to prevent dehydration from frequent diarrhea.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Individualized Triggers: Identify and avoid specific foods that trigger or worsen symptoms during flares; these vary significantly among individuals.
  • Low-Residue Diet During Flares: Consider a low-fiber, low-residue diet during active disease to reduce bowel movements and minimize irritation to the inflamed colon.
  • Nutrient-Dense Foods: Focus on consuming nutrient-dense foods to combat potential malnutrition and weight loss, ensuring adequate protein and calorie intake.
  • Hydration: Drink plenty of water, broths, and electrolyte-rich fluids, especially during episodes of diarrhea, to prevent dehydration.
  • Limit Irritants: During flares, consider limiting caffeine, alcohol, spicy foods, and high-fat foods, which can sometimes exacerbate symptoms.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Emergency Symptoms: Seek immediate medical attention for severe abdominal pain, high fever, profuse bloody diarrhea, signs of toxic megacolon (e.g., severe abdominal distension, rapid heart rate), or persistent vomiting.
  • Medication Side Effects: Be aware of potential side effects of prescribed medications (e.g., increased infection risk with immunosuppressants, bone density issues with long-term corticosteroids) and report any concerns to your physician.
  • Vaccination Status: Discuss necessary vaccinations with your healthcare provider, especially if on immunosuppressive therapy, to prevent opportunistic infections.
  • Mental Health Support: Recognize the significant impact of chronic illness on mental well-being; seek support if experiencing anxiety, depression, or stress related to UC.
  • Smoking Cessation: While smoking is generally protective in UC, it is strongly discouraged due to its overall detrimental health effects and increased risk of other diseases.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Personalized Treatment Plans: Management of UC is highly individualized, focusing on inducing and maintaining remission, managing symptoms, and preventing complications.
  • Multidisciplinary Care: A comprehensive approach often involves gastroenterologists, dietitians, surgeons, and mental health professionals to optimize patient outcomes.
  • Long-Term Management: UC is a chronic condition requiring ongoing surveillance and adjustment of therapy to minimize disease progression and improve quality of life.
  • Surgical Considerations: Colectomy (surgical removal of the colon) may be indicated for severe, refractory disease, toxic megacolon, or the development of dysplasia/colorectal cancer.
  • Proactive Monitoring: Regular monitoring for disease activity and potential complications is crucial for effective long-term management.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Patient Education: Provide thorough education on medication administration, potential side effects, dietary considerations, and symptom monitoring to empower patients in self-management.
  • Fluid & Electrolyte Balance: Closely monitor patients for signs of dehydration and electrolyte imbalances, especially during severe flares with significant diarrhea.
  • Pain Assessment & Management: Assess abdominal pain characteristics and severity, implementing pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions as appropriate.
  • Psychosocial Support: Offer emotional support, active listening, and connect patients with support groups or mental health resources to help cope with the challenges of chronic illness.
  • Stoma Care Education: For patients undergoing colectomy with ileostomy, provide comprehensive pre- and post-operative stoma care education, including appliance management and skin integrity.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Pathophysiology: Chronic inflammation of the colonic mucosa, typically continuous and confined to the large intestine, characterized by crypt abscesses and ulceration.
  • Diagnosis: Primarily based on clinical symptoms, endoscopic findings (colonoscopy with biopsy showing characteristic inflammation), and exclusion of other causes (e.g., infectious colitis).
  • Disease Classification: Categorized by extent (ulcerative proctitis, left-sided colitis, pancolitis) and severity (mild, moderate, severe) based on clinical and endoscopic criteria.
  • Complications: Toxic megacolon, colorectal cancer, primary sclerosing cholangitis, anemia, malnutrition, strictures (less common than in Crohn's).
  • Pharmacology: 5-aminosalicylates (e.g., mesalamine), corticosteroids (e.g., prednisone, budesonide), immunomodulators (e.g., azathioprine, mercaptopurine), biologics (e.g., infliximab, vedolizumab, ustekinumab), and Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors (e.g., tofacitinib).
