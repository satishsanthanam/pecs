H. pylori infection

🏠 Home / Digestive system / Peptic ulcers

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Common Bacterial Infection: Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) is a bacterium that infects the lining of the stomach.
  • Primary Cause of Ulcers: It is the leading cause of peptic ulcers (sores in the stomach or duodenum).
  • Gastritis and Cancer Risk: Chronic H. pylori infection can lead to gastritis (stomach inflammation) and significantly increases the risk of gastric cancer.
  • Transmission Routes: Often acquired in childhood, spread primarily through oral-oral or fecal-oral routes, especially in areas with poor sanitation.
  • Asymptomatic Carriers: Many infected individuals remain asymptomatic throughout their lives, never developing related complications.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Abdominal Pain: Burning or gnawing pain in the upper abdomen, often worse on an empty stomach and relieved by eating or antacids.
  • Nausea and Vomiting: Persistent feelings of sickness and occasional episodes of vomiting.
  • Bloating and Indigestion: Frequent sensations of fullness, bloating, and discomfort after eating.
  • Unexplained Weight Loss: Significant and unintentional reduction in body weight, particularly in advanced or complicated cases.
  • Gastrointestinal Bleeding: Signs such as dark, tarry stools (melena) or vomiting blood (hematemesis) indicate a bleeding ulcer, requiring urgent medical attention.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Hand Hygiene: Practice meticulous handwashing with soap and water, especially before preparing food, eating, and after using the restroom.
  • Safe Food and Water: Consume food and water from trusted sources, ensuring proper cooking and sanitation, particularly when traveling.
  • Avoid Sharing Utensils: Refrain from sharing eating utensils, cups, or personal items that may come into contact with saliva.
  • Complete Treatment Course: Adhere strictly to the full course of prescribed antibiotics and acid suppressants to ensure complete eradication and prevent resistance.
  • Follow-up Testing: Undergo recommended follow-up tests (e.g., urea breath test, stool antigen test) to confirm successful H. pylori eradication.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Avoid Irritants: Limit or avoid spicy foods, acidic foods (e.g., citrus, tomatoes), caffeine, and carbonated beverages that can irritate the stomach lining.
  • Moderate Alcohol Intake: Reduce or eliminate alcohol consumption, as it can exacerbate gastritis and ulcer symptoms.
  • Probiotic Support: Incorporate probiotic-rich foods (e.g., yogurt, kefir) or supplements to help maintain healthy gut flora during antibiotic treatment.
  • Small, Frequent Meals: Eat smaller, more frequent meals throughout the day to reduce the burden on the stomach and minimize acid surges.
  • Hydration: Maintain adequate hydration with water and non-acidic, non-caffeinated beverages.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Monitor for Bleeding: Watch for signs of gastrointestinal bleeding, such as black, tarry stools, coffee-ground vomit, or bright red blood in vomit, and seek immediate medical help.
  • Medication Adherence: Ensure the patient takes all prescribed medications exactly as directed, even if symptoms improve, to prevent treatment failure and antibiotic resistance.
  • Report Severe Side Effects: Be aware of potential antibiotic side effects (e.g., severe diarrhea, allergic reactions) and report them promptly to a healthcare provider.
  • Symptom Persistence: Understand that some symptoms may persist for a period even after successful H. pylori eradication, requiring ongoing symptomatic management.
  • Emergency Signs: Recognize and act quickly on emergency signs like sudden, severe abdominal pain, fainting, or difficulty breathing, which could indicate a perforation or severe bleeding.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Diagnostic Confirmation: Diagnosis is typically confirmed via non-invasive tests (urea breath test, stool antigen test) or invasive endoscopy with biopsy.
  • Eradication Therapy: Treatment involves a combination of two antibiotics and a proton pump inhibitor (PPI) for 7-14 days (triple or quadruple therapy).
  • Resistance Management: Consider local antibiotic resistance patterns when selecting treatment regimens, especially in cases of treatment failure.
  • Post-Treatment Verification: Confirm eradication with a follow-up test (e.g., urea breath test) at least 4 weeks after completing therapy and discontinuing PPIs.
  • Long-term Surveillance: Patients with a history of gastric atrophy, intestinal metaplasia, or gastric cancer in first-degree relatives may require long-term endoscopic surveillance.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Patient Education: Educate patients thoroughly on the importance of medication adherence, potential side effects, and the need for follow-up testing to confirm eradication.
  • Symptom Assessment: Conduct comprehensive assessments for gastrointestinal symptoms, including pain characteristics, nausea, vomiting, and signs of bleeding.
  • Dietary Counseling: Provide practical dietary advice to minimize gastric irritation and support recovery during and after H. pylori treatment.
  • Medication Administration: Understand the specific administration guidelines and potential interactions for various antibiotics and PPIs used in H. pylori eradication.
  • Complication Monitoring: Monitor patients for signs of complications such as ulcer bleeding, perforation, or severe adverse drug reactions.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Urea Breath Test (UBT): Non-invasive diagnostic test for active H. pylori infection, measuring labeled CO2 in breath after ingesting labeled urea.
  • Stool Antigen Test (SAT): Non-invasive test detecting H. pylori antigens in stool, useful for both diagnosis and confirming eradication.
  • Endoscopy with Biopsy: Invasive procedure allowing direct visualization of gastric mucosa and histological examination for H. pylori, inflammation, and malignancy.
  • Triple Therapy: Standard H. pylori eradication regimen typically comprising a PPI, clarithromycin, and amoxicillin (or metronidazole) for 7-14 days.
  • Quadruple Therapy: Bismuth-based regimen often used for clarithromycin-resistant strains or as a salvage therapy, including a PPI, bismuth subsalicylate, tetracycline, and metronidazole.
  • Gastric Adenocarcinoma: A type of stomach cancer strongly associated with chronic H. pylori infection, highlighting the importance of eradication.
