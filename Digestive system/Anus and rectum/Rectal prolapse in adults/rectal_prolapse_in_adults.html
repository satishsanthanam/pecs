Rectal prolapse in adults

🏠 Home / Digestive system / Anus and rectum

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Definition: Rectal prolapse occurs when the rectum turns inside out and protrudes through the anus.
  • Types: Can be full-thickness (complete) involving all layers of the rectal wall, or mucosal (partial) involving only the lining.
  • Causes: Often linked to weakened pelvic floor muscles, chronic straining during bowel movements, age, and sometimes prior pelvic surgery or neurological conditions.
  • Prevalence: More common in older women, though it can affect individuals of any age or gender.
  • Impact: Can significantly affect quality of life due to discomfort, incontinence, and hygiene challenges.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Visible Protrusion: A red, cylinder-shaped mass extending from the anus, especially during bowel movements, coughing, or standing.
  • Fecal Incontinence: Difficulty controlling bowel movements, leakage of stool, or mucus discharge.
  • Constipation: Chronic straining, feeling of incomplete evacuation, or difficulty passing stool.
  • Rectal Bleeding: Due to irritation, ulceration, or trauma to the prolapsed tissue.
  • Mucus Discharge: From the exposed rectal lining, leading to perianal irritation.
  • Rectal Discomfort: Aching, pressure, or a sensation of fullness in the rectal area.
  • Feeling of Mass: Sensation of something 'falling out' or a persistent lump in the anal region.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Avoid Straining: During bowel movements, use proper toilet posture and avoid prolonged sitting on the toilet.
  • Manage Constipation: Maintain regular, soft bowel movements through diet and hydration to prevent excessive straining.
  • Pelvic Floor Exercises: Regular Kegel exercises may help strengthen pelvic floor muscles, particularly in mild cases or post-operatively.
  • Manual Reduction: If a prolapse occurs, gentle manual reduction may be possible, but always seek medical guidance for proper technique.
  • Avoid Heavy Lifting: Minimize activities that significantly increase intra-abdominal pressure, which can worsen prolapse.
  • Prompt Medical Evaluation: Seek professional advice for any new or worsening symptoms to prevent complications like incarceration or strangulation.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • High-Fiber Diet: Incorporate plenty of fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and legumes to promote soft, bulky stools and prevent constipation.
  • Adequate Hydration: Drink sufficient water throughout the day to keep stools soft and facilitate easy passage.
  • Limit Processed Foods: Reduce intake of foods low in fiber and high in refined sugars, which can contribute to constipation.
  • Avoid Irritants: Some individuals may find spicy foods or excessive caffeine/alcohol irritate the bowel or contribute to dehydration, potentially worsening constipation.
  • Post-Operative Diet: Follow specific surgeon instructions, typically starting with clear liquids, progressing to soft foods, and then a regular diet as tolerated.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Emergency Care: Seek immediate medical attention if the prolapsed tissue becomes trapped, discolored (dark red/purple), extremely painful, or cannot be manually reduced, as this may indicate incarceration or strangulation.
  • Hygiene: Maintain meticulous perianal hygiene to prevent skin irritation, infection, and breakdown, especially if there is mucus discharge.
  • Pain Management: Adhere strictly to prescribed pain relief regimens, particularly post-surgically, and report any uncontrolled pain.
  • Activity Restrictions: Follow all post-operative instructions regarding lifting, strenuous activity, and return to normal daily routines to ensure proper healing and prevent recurrence.
  • Medication Adherence: Take prescribed stool softeners, laxatives, or other medications as directed to manage bowel function and prevent straining.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Accurate Diagnosis: Essential to differentiate rectal prolapse from hemorrhoids or polyps; often requires a thorough physical examination, sometimes with dynamic imaging like defecography.
  • Treatment Options: Range from conservative management (diet, lifestyle) for mild cases to various surgical repairs, which are the definitive treatment for most complete prolapses.
  • Surgical Consultation: Patients should discuss the risks, benefits, and recurrence rates of different surgical approaches (e.g., abdominal rectopexy, perineal procedures) with a colorectal surgeon.
  • Address Underlying Causes: It is crucial to identify and manage contributing factors such as chronic constipation, neurological conditions, or pelvic floor dysfunction to optimize outcomes.
  • Post-Operative Care: Emphasize the importance of adhering to post-surgical guidelines for diet, activity, and bowel management to ensure successful recovery and minimize recurrence risk.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Patient Education: Provide comprehensive education on proper bowel habits, dietary modifications, signs of complications, and post-operative care instructions.
  • Pre-operative Preparation: Ensure patients understand bowel preparation protocols, NPO status, and complete pre-surgical assessments and consents.
  • Post-operative Monitoring: Closely monitor vital signs, pain levels, surgical site for bleeding/infection, urinary output, and return of bowel function.
  • Wound Care: Instruct on appropriate wound care for surgical sites, including dressing changes and signs of infection.
  • Pelvic Floor Rehabilitation: Educate patients on the importance of pelvic floor exercises and refer to physical therapy for specialized training as indicated.
  • Psychosocial Support: Address patient embarrassment, anxiety, and the impact of the condition on quality of life, offering empathetic support and resources.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • ICD-10 Codes: K62.3 (Rectal prolapse).
  • Diagnostic Modalities: Defecography (dynamic proctography), anorectal manometry, colonoscopy, MRI of the pelvis, physical examination (often with patient straining).
  • Surgical Procedures: Abdominal approaches (e.g., suture rectopexy, mesh rectopexy, resection rectopexy), Perineal approaches (e.g., Altemeier procedure, Delorme procedure, STARR procedure).
  • Differential Diagnosis: Hemorrhoids (internal/external), rectal polyps, intussusception, procidentia, mucosal prolapse.
  • Risk Factors: Advanced age, female gender (especially multiparous), chronic constipation/straining, neurological disorders (e.g., stroke, spinal cord injury), prior pelvic surgery, connective tissue disorders.
