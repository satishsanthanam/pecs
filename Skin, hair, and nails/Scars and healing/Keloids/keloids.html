Keloids

🏠 Home / Skin, hair, and nails / Scars and healing

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Definition: Keloids are raised, firm, and often shiny scars that extend beyond the original wound boundaries. They result from an overgrowth of granulation tissue (collagen type III) at the site of a healed skin injury.
  • Distinction from Hypertrophic Scars: Unlike hypertrophic scars, which remain confined to the original wound area and often regress over time, keloids invade surrounding healthy tissue and rarely regress spontaneously.
  • Common Locations: Frequently appear on the chest (especially the sternum), shoulders, earlobes, neck, and back, though they can occur anywhere on the body.
  • Risk Factors: Genetic predisposition, darker skin types (African, Asian, Hispanic descent), and certain types of skin trauma (e.g., burns, piercings, surgical incisions, acne, vaccinations) increase susceptibility.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Appearance: Characteristically raised, often dome-shaped or nodular, with a smooth, shiny surface. Color can range from pink or red to purple or dark brown, often contrasting with surrounding skin.
  • Sensations: Patients commonly report persistent itching, tenderness, or pain within the keloid, which can be exacerbated by friction or pressure.
  • Functional Impairment: Large keloids, especially those near joints, can restrict movement and cause discomfort or contractures.
  • Psychological Impact: Due to their prominent appearance and chronic nature, keloids can lead to significant cosmetic distress, self-consciousness, and impact quality of life.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Minimizing Skin Trauma: Individuals with a known history of keloids or strong family history should avoid unnecessary skin injuries, elective cosmetic procedures, or piercings.
  • Careful Wound Management: For unavoidable injuries or surgeries, meticulous wound closure with minimal tension and early application of silicone sheets or gel can help reduce keloid formation risk.
  • Sun Protection: Existing keloids should be protected from sun exposure using high-SPF sunscreen or protective clothing, as UV radiation can cause hyperpigmentation and worsen their appearance.
  • Early Consultation: Any suspicious scar development that appears to be growing beyond the wound margins or causing significant symptoms should prompt early consultation with a dermatologist or plastic surgeon.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Nutritional Support for Skin Health: While no specific dietary restrictions or directions directly prevent or treat keloids, a balanced diet rich in vitamins (especially C and E), minerals (like zinc), and protein supports overall skin health and optimal wound healing processes.
  • Hydration: Adequate hydration is essential for maintaining skin elasticity and supporting cellular functions involved in tissue repair.
  • Anti-inflammatory Foods: Incorporating foods with anti-inflammatory properties, such as omega-3 fatty acids found in fish, may contribute to overall skin health, though direct impact on keloid formation is not established.
  • Avoidance of Unproven Remedies: Patients should be advised against relying on unproven dietary supplements or extreme diets marketed for scar reduction, as these lack scientific evidence for keloid management.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Avoid Self-Treatment: Attempting to remove or treat keloids at home can lead to further trauma, infection, and potentially worsen the scar.
  • Recurrence Risk: Patients must be informed that keloids have a high propensity for recurrence, even after surgical excision, often requiring adjunctive therapies.
  • Specialist Referral: Management of keloids typically requires a multidisciplinary approach involving dermatologists, plastic surgeons, or radiation oncologists.
  • Long-Term Management: Keloid treatment is often a long-term process, requiring patience and adherence to prescribed therapies and follow-up schedules.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Multimodal Treatment: Effective keloid management often involves a combination of therapies, including intralesional corticosteroid injections, cryotherapy, silicone gel sheeting, pressure therapy, laser therapy, surgical excision (often with adjuvant therapy), and radiation therapy.
  • Individualized Approach: Treatment plans must be tailored to the individual patient, considering keloid size, location, patient age, skin type, previous treatments, and response to therapy.
  • Realistic Expectations: It is crucial to set realistic expectations with patients, emphasizing that complete eradication without recurrence is challenging, and the primary goal is often symptom control and cosmetic improvement.
  • Early Intervention: Early diagnosis and intervention, particularly for new or rapidly growing keloids, can lead to better outcomes and potentially prevent significant enlargement.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Patient Education: Nurses play a vital role in educating patients about keloid prevention strategies, proper wound care, the importance of adherence to treatment regimens, and managing expectations regarding outcomes.
  • Wound Assessment: Regular assessment of surgical sites or healing wounds for early signs of keloid formation, such as persistent redness, itching, or thickening beyond wound margins.
  • Application of Topical Therapies: Instructing patients on the correct application of silicone sheets/gels and monitoring for skin irritation or compliance issues.
  • Psychosocial Support: Providing empathetic support to patients dealing with the cosmetic and psychological impact of keloids, and referring to support groups or counseling as needed.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Pathophysiology: Characterized by excessive deposition of collagen type I and III, increased fibroblast proliferation, and reduced collagenase activity, leading to an imbalance in extracellular matrix remodeling.
  • Histological Features: Histopathology reveals thick, hyalinized collagen bundles arranged haphazardly, often with a nodular configuration, and an increased number of fibroblasts.
  • Genetic Predisposition: Associated with specific genetic loci, including those on chromosomes 2, 7, 10, 15, and 18, suggesting a polygenic inheritance pattern.
  • Differential Diagnosis: Key differentiators include hypertrophic scars (confined to wound, often regress), dermatofibromas (benign fibrous histiocytomas, usually solitary), and rarely, certain skin tumors.
  • ICD-10 Code: L91.0 (Keloid scar).
