Cellulitis around the eye – Discharge instructions

🏠 Home / Skin, hair, and nails / Skin infections

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Understanding Cellulitis: Cellulitis around the eye is a bacterial infection of the skin and underlying tissues. It can be preseptal (anterior to the orbital septum, affecting eyelids and surrounding skin) or, more seriously, postseptal (orbital cellulitis, affecting tissues behind the orbital septum).
  • Severity and Progression: Preseptal cellulitis is less severe but can progress to orbital cellulitis if not treated promptly. Orbital cellulitis is a medical emergency due to potential vision loss and intracranial complications.
  • Treatment Cornerstone: Antibiotics are the primary treatment. It is crucial to complete the entire prescribed course, even if symptoms improve, to prevent recurrence and resistance.
  • Watch for Worsening: Close monitoring for signs of worsening infection or development of orbital involvement is essential for timely intervention.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Increased Redness and Swelling: Worsening erythema and edema around the affected eye.
  • Intensified Pain: Growing discomfort or tenderness in the periorbital area.
  • Fever and Chills: Development or persistence of systemic signs of infection.
  • Pain with Eye Movement: A key indicator of potential orbital involvement.
  • Double Vision (Diplopia): Suggests pressure or inflammation affecting extraocular muscles.
  • Decreased Vision: Any reduction in visual acuity is a critical warning sign.
  • Bulging of the Eye (Proptosis): Indicates increased pressure within the orbit.
  • Inability to Move Eye (Ophthalmoplegia): A severe sign of orbital compromise.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Complete Antibiotic Course: Take all prescribed antibiotics exactly as directed, even if symptoms improve, to eradicate the infection fully.
  • Hand Hygiene: Wash hands thoroughly before and after touching the affected eye or applying medications.
  • Avoid Contact Lenses: Do not wear contact lenses until the infection has completely resolved and your physician approves.
  • Warm Compresses: Apply warm, moist compresses to the affected area for 10-15 minutes, 3-4 times daily, to help reduce swelling and promote drainage. Ensure the compress is clean.
  • Do Not Rub or Squeeze: Avoid rubbing, squeezing, or attempting to drain any lesions around the eye, as this can spread the infection.
  • Elevate Head: Sleeping with the head slightly elevated can help reduce periorbital swelling.
  • Follow-up Appointment: Attend all scheduled follow-up appointments to ensure the infection is resolving appropriately and to monitor for complications.
  • Emergency Signs: Seek immediate medical attention if you experience sudden vision changes, severe eye pain, inability to move your eye, bulging of the eye, high fever, or severe headache.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Maintain Hydration: Drink plenty of fluids (water, clear broths, electrolyte solutions) to stay well-hydrated, especially if experiencing fever.
  • Medication with Food: Some antibiotics may cause stomach upset; take them with food as advised by your pharmacist or physician to minimize gastrointestinal discomfort.
  • Avoid Irritants: Limit consumption of alcohol and excessive caffeine, as they can interfere with sleep and overall recovery.
  • Nutrient-Rich Diet: Focus on a balanced diet rich in fruits, vegetables, and lean proteins to support immune function and healing.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Medication Adherence Support: Ensure the patient takes all prescribed medications, especially antibiotics, on schedule and completes the full course.
  • Monitor for Worsening Symptoms: Closely observe for any increase in redness, swelling, pain, fever, or the development of new symptoms like vision changes, double vision, or difficulty moving the eye.
  • Assist with Compresses: Help with the application of warm compresses, ensuring they are clean and applied correctly.
  • Emergency Preparedness: Know when to seek immediate medical attention (e.g., sudden vision loss, severe eye pain, proptosis, high fever, altered mental status) and have emergency contact information readily available.
  • Maintain Hygiene: Encourage and assist the patient with proper hand hygiene to prevent further infection spread.
  • Follow-up Reminders: Remind the patient of upcoming follow-up appointments and assist with transportation if needed.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Timely Intervention is Key: Early diagnosis and aggressive antibiotic treatment are crucial to prevent progression, especially to orbital cellulitis, which carries significant risks.
  • Differentiating Preseptal vs. Orbital: Clinical assessment, sometimes aided by imaging (e.g., CT scan), is vital to distinguish preseptal from orbital cellulitis, as management differs significantly.
  • Risk Factor Management: Address underlying risk factors such as recent trauma, insect bites, sinusitis, or skin breaks to prevent recurrence.
  • Patient Education: Emphasize the importance of completing the antibiotic course and recognizing warning signs for immediate re-evaluation.
  • Prognosis: With appropriate and timely treatment, the prognosis for periorbital cellulitis is generally excellent, but orbital cellulitis can have serious long-term sequelae.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Patient Education Focus: Key teaching points include medication regimen, signs of worsening infection, proper wound care/hygiene, and the importance of follow-up.
  • Assessment Priorities: Regular assessment of visual acuity, extraocular movements, presence of proptosis, pain level, and vital signs.
  • Documentation: Meticulous documentation of patient education, understanding, adherence to treatment, and any changes in symptoms or signs of complications.
  • Infection Control: Reinforce standard precautions and proper hand hygiene for both patient and caregivers.
  • Discharge Planning: Ensure patient has clear instructions, necessary prescriptions, and understands when and how to seek further medical care.
  • Psychosocial Support: Address patient anxiety regarding potential vision loss or cosmetic changes.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Etiology: Commonly caused by *Staphylococcus aureus* and *Streptococcus pyogenes*. In children, *Haemophilus influenzae* type b was historically a concern but is less common due to vaccination.
  • Anatomy: The orbital septum is a fibrous membrane that separates the superficial eyelid structures (preseptal space) from the deeper orbital contents (postseptal space).
  • Diagnosis: Primarily clinical. Imaging (CT scan of orbits with contrast) is indicated if orbital cellulitis is suspected (e.g., proptosis, ophthalmoplegia, vision changes) or if diagnosis is unclear.
  • Treatment: Oral antibiotics (e.g., amoxicillin-clavulanate, clindamycin, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole) for preseptal cellulitis. Intravenous antibiotics (e.g., ceftriaxone, vancomycin, clindamycin) for orbital cellulitis or severe preseptal cases. Surgical drainage may be required for abscess formation.
  • Complications: Orbital cellulitis can lead to vision loss, optic neuropathy, subperiosteal abscess, orbital abscess, cavernous sinus thrombosis, meningitis, and brain abscess.
