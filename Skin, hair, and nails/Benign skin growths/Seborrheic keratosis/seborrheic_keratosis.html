Seborrheic keratosis

🏠 Home / Skin, hair, and nails / Benign skin growths

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Benign Growth: Seborrheic keratosis is a common, non-cancerous skin growth that originates from keratinocytes in the epidermis.
  • Appearance: Often appears as a waxy, 'stuck-on' lesion, varying in color from light tan to dark brown or black. They can be flat or raised.
  • Prevalence: Highly common, especially in middle-aged and older adults, with incidence increasing with age.
  • Location: Can appear anywhere on the body except the palms and soles, frequently found on the face, scalp, chest, and back.
  • No Malignant Potential: These growths are benign and do not transform into skin cancer.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Characteristic Lesions: Presence of well-demarcated, often pigmented lesions with a 'pasted-on' or 'warty' appearance.
  • Texture: Lesions can feel rough, greasy, or crumbly to the touch.
  • Pruritus: Occasional itching, especially if the lesion becomes irritated by clothing or scratching.
  • Bleeding: May bleed if traumatized, scratched, or rubbed, leading to inflammation.
  • Leser-Trélat Sign: A sudden eruption of multiple seborrheic keratoses, often accompanied by pruritus, which can be a paraneoplastic phenomenon indicating an underlying internal malignancy.
  • Slow Growth: Typically grow slowly over time, but new lesions can appear.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Professional Diagnosis: Always seek a dermatologist's evaluation for any new or changing skin lesion to rule out malignancy, particularly melanoma.
  • Avoid Irritation: Refrain from picking, scratching, or attempting to remove seborrheic keratoses yourself, as this can lead to irritation, inflammation, infection, or scarring.
  • Sun Protection: While not directly caused by sun, consistent use of broad-spectrum sunscreen and protective clothing supports overall skin health and may reduce the development of other sun-related skin conditions.
  • Self-Monitoring: Regularly perform skin self-exams to identify any new growths or changes in existing lesions, reporting concerns to a healthcare provider.
  • Biopsy Consideration: If a lesion exhibits atypical features, rapid growth, or spontaneous bleeding, a biopsy may be necessary to confirm diagnosis and exclude malignancy.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • No Direct Link: There is no specific diet or dietary restriction directly linked to the prevention or treatment of seborrheic keratosis.
  • Antioxidant-Rich Foods: A diet rich in fruits, vegetables, and whole grains provides antioxidants that support overall skin health and cellular integrity.
  • Hydration: Adequate water intake is essential for maintaining skin hydration and barrier function.
  • Omega-3 Fatty Acids: Incorporating foods rich in omega-3 fatty acids (e.g., fatty fish, flaxseeds) may contribute to general skin health and reduce inflammation.
  • Limit Processed Foods: Reducing intake of highly processed foods, excessive sugars, and unhealthy fats can support overall health, which indirectly benefits skin.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Differential Diagnosis: Clinicians must differentiate seborrheic keratosis from other pigmented lesions, especially melanoma, basal cell carcinoma, and squamous cell carcinoma.
  • Leser-Trélat Investigation: The sudden appearance of numerous seborrheic keratoses warrants a thorough medical workup to screen for potential underlying internal malignancies.
  • Biopsy for Atypia: Any seborrheic keratosis that is rapidly changing, bleeding, ulcerated, or has suspicious features (e.g., variegated color, irregular borders) should be biopsied.
  • Cosmetic vs. Medical Removal: Removal is typically performed for cosmetic reasons, chronic irritation, or diagnostic uncertainty, not due to malignant potential.
  • Patient Education: Emphasize the benign nature of seborrheic keratosis to patients, while also educating them on signs that would necessitate re-evaluation.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Clinical Diagnosis: Most seborrheic keratoses can be diagnosed clinically, often aided by dermoscopy, which reveals characteristic features like milia-like cysts and comedo-like openings.
  • Reassurance is Key: It is crucial to reassure patients about the benign nature of these lesions and their lack of malignant transformation.
  • Treatment Options: Discuss various removal methods such as cryotherapy (liquid nitrogen), shave excision, electrocautery, or laser ablation, based on lesion size, location, and patient preference.
  • Monitoring Strategy: Advise patients on self-monitoring for any changes in existing lesions or the appearance of new, suspicious growths.
  • Biopsy Threshold: Maintain a low threshold for biopsy if there is any doubt in diagnosis, especially for lesions that mimic melanoma.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Patient Education Role: Nurses are vital in educating patients about seborrheic keratosis, explaining its benign nature, and providing instructions for post-procedure care if removed.
  • Skin Assessment Skills: Develop proficiency in identifying seborrheic keratoses during routine skin assessments, noting their characteristic appearance and differentiating them from other skin lesions.
  • Pre- and Post-Procedure Care: Assist with patient preparation for procedures like cryotherapy or shave excision, and provide clear wound care instructions to prevent infection and promote healing.
  • Documentation Accuracy: Accurately document the location, size, and characteristics of seborrheic keratoses, as well as any patient concerns or symptoms.
  • Referral Pathways: Understand when to refer patients to a dermatologist for evaluation of suspicious lesions or for cosmetic removal.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Histopathology: Characterized by proliferation of basaloid cells, hyperkeratosis, acanthosis, and often numerous horn cysts (pseudohorn cysts) and melanin within the epidermis.
  • Dermoscopic Features: Key features include milia-like cysts, comedo-like openings, hairpin blood vessels, and cerebriform patterns.
  • ICD-10 Codes: L82.0 for Inflamed seborrheic keratosis; L82.1 for Other seborrheic keratosis.
  • Pathogenesis: Associated with activating mutations in FGFR3 and PIK3CA genes, and activation of the RAS/MAPK pathway, leading to epidermal proliferation.
  • Clinical Variants: Includes common seborrheic keratosis, stucco keratosis (small, white-gray lesions on distal extremities), dermatosis papulosa nigra (multiple small, dark lesions on the face/neck of individuals with darker skin types).
