Angioedema

🏠 Home / Skin, hair, and nails / Hives and angioedema

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Definition: Angioedema is a sudden, localized swelling of the deeper layers of the skin or mucous membranes, often affecting the face, lips, tongue, throat, and extremities.
  • Mechanism: It can be histamine-mediated (often associated with hives and allergies) or bradykinin-mediated (less common, not typically itchy, and can be life-threatening if it affects the airway).
  • Triggers: Common triggers include allergic reactions (foods, medications, insect stings), certain medications (especially ACE inhibitors), hereditary factors, and autoimmune conditions.
  • Distinction: Unlike urticaria (hives), angioedema involves deeper tissue swelling and may not be itchy, but can be painful or cause a burning sensation.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Deep Swelling: Characterized by non-pitting, localized swelling that develops rapidly, often over minutes to hours.
  • Common Sites: Frequently affects the lips, eyelids, tongue, hands, feet, genitals, and gastrointestinal tract.
  • Discomfort: Patients may experience a sensation of tightness, pain, or burning in the affected areas, rather than itching.
  • Airway Compromise: Swelling of the larynx or pharynx can lead to hoarseness, difficulty swallowing (dysphagia), stridor, and severe breathing difficulties (dyspnea), which is a medical emergency.
  • Gastrointestinal Manifestations: Abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea can occur due to swelling of the intestinal wall.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Trigger Identification: Work with a clinician to identify and strictly avoid known triggers, including specific foods, medications, or environmental factors.
  • Emergency Medication: Patients at risk for severe reactions, especially those with hereditary angioedema (HAE) or a history of laryngeal involvement, must carry prescribed emergency medications (e.g., epinephrine auto-injector, C1-INH concentrate, bradykinin receptor antagonist) and know how to use them.
  • Medication Review: Inform all healthcare providers about a history of angioedema, particularly before starting new medications, as ACE inhibitors and NSAIDs are common culprits.
  • Medical Alert: Consider wearing a medical alert bracelet or carrying an information card detailing the type of angioedema and emergency treatment plan.
  • Airway Monitoring: Be vigilant for any signs of throat or tongue swelling, hoarseness, or difficulty breathing, and seek immediate medical attention if these occur.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Allergen Avoidance: For allergic angioedema, strictly avoid any identified food allergens (e.g., peanuts, shellfish, dairy, soy, wheat) that trigger reactions.
  • Pseudoallergen Consideration: Some individuals may react to food additives, preservatives, or natural salicylates; a trial elimination diet under medical supervision may be considered.
  • Soft Diet During Episodes: If oral or pharyngeal swelling is present, consume soft, easy-to-swallow foods and liquids to minimize discomfort and reduce the risk of aspiration.
  • Hydration: Maintain adequate hydration, especially during episodes, to support overall physiological function.
  • Alcohol Restriction: Alcohol can sometimes exacerbate angioedema in susceptible individuals, and its consumption should be discussed with a healthcare provider.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Recognize Severity: Immediately identify signs of a severe angioedema attack, especially swelling of the face, lips, tongue, or throat, which can rapidly lead to airway obstruction.
  • Call Emergency Services: If there are any signs of breathing difficulty, stridor, or significant throat swelling, call emergency medical services (e.g., 911) without delay.
  • Administer Emergency Medication: If the patient has prescribed emergency medication (e.g., epinephrine auto-injector, C1-INH concentrate), assist them in administering it as directed.
  • Maintain Airway: Position the patient to optimize breathing, typically upright, and loosen any tight clothing around the neck.
  • Stay with Patient: Do not leave the patient unattended. Monitor their vital signs and level of consciousness until medical professionals arrive.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Accurate Diagnosis: Differentiating between histamine-mediated, bradykinin-mediated (HAE, AAE, drug-induced), and idiopathic angioedema is crucial for effective management.
  • Comprehensive Evaluation: A thorough history, physical examination, and specific laboratory tests (e.g., C4, C1 esterase inhibitor levels and function, genetic testing) are essential for diagnosis.
  • Tailored Management: Treatment strategies vary widely; antihistamines and corticosteroids for histamine-mediated, while C1-INH concentrate, icatibant, or ecallantide are used for bradykinin-mediated forms.
  • Patient Education: Emphasize the importance of trigger avoidance, carrying emergency medication, and having a clear action plan for acute attacks.
  • Long-Term Care: For chronic or hereditary forms, consider prophylactic treatments and regular follow-up to monitor disease activity and adjust therapy.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Airway Assessment: Prioritize continuous assessment of airway patency, respiratory status, and oxygen saturation, especially during acute episodes.
  • Medication Administration: Be proficient in administering emergency medications, including subcutaneous epinephrine and intravenous C1-INH concentrate, as per protocol.
  • Patient Education: Educate patients and their families on recognizing symptoms, identifying triggers, proper use of emergency medications, and when to seek immediate medical care.
  • Psychosocial Support: Provide emotional support and resources for patients coping with the chronic nature and potential life-threatening aspects of angioedema.
  • Documentation: Meticulously document all episodes, including triggers, symptoms, interventions, patient response, and education provided.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Types of Angioedema: Hereditary Angioedema (HAE), Acquired Angioedema (AAE), Drug-induced Angioedema (e.g., ACE inhibitor-induced), Allergic Angioedema, Idiopathic Angioedema.
  • Pathophysiology: Involves either mast cell degranulation (histamine-mediated) or dysregulation of the kallikrein-kinin system leading to excessive bradykinin production (bradykinin-mediated).
  • Diagnostic Markers: C4 complement levels, C1 esterase inhibitor quantitative and functional assays, C1q levels (for AAE), genetic testing for SERPING1 mutations (for HAE).
  • Acute Treatment Agents: Epinephrine, antihistamines, corticosteroids (for histamine-mediated); C1 esterase inhibitor concentrate, icatibant (bradykinin B2 receptor antagonist), ecallantide (kallikrein inhibitor) for bradykinin-mediated.
  • Prophylactic Treatments: Attenuated androgens (e.g., danazol), C1-INH concentrate, lanadelumab (kallikrein inhibitor) for HAE.
