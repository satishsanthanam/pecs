Hives

🏠 Home / Skin, hair, and nails / Hives and angioedema

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Definition: Hives, medically known as urticaria, are itchy, red, or skin-colored welts (wheals) that appear on the skin. They can vary in size and shape, often appearing suddenly and disappearing within hours, only to reappear elsewhere.
  • Causes: Hives are typically triggered by an allergic reaction to food, medication, insect stings, or environmental factors like pollen or pet dander. Non-allergic triggers include stress, infections, physical stimuli (cold, heat, pressure), and certain medical conditions.
  • Acute vs. Chronic: Acute hives last less than six weeks and are often linked to a specific trigger. Chronic hives persist for more than six weeks, and their cause can be more challenging to identify, sometimes being autoimmune.
  • Angioedema: Hives can sometimes be accompanied by angioedema, which is swelling in the deeper layers of the skin, often affecting the eyelids, lips, tongue, or genitals.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Itchy Welts (Wheals): The primary symptom is the appearance of raised, red, or skin-colored bumps that are intensely itchy. These welts may blanch (turn white) when pressed.
  • Variable Appearance: Hives can appear as small spots, blotches, or large patches, and they can change shape, move around, and disappear and reappear over short periods.
  • Angioedema Swelling: Deeper swelling, particularly around the eyes, lips, tongue, throat, or genitals, which may be painful or cause a burning sensation rather than itching.
  • Systemic Symptoms (Severe Cases): In severe allergic reactions (anaphylaxis), hives may be accompanied by difficulty breathing, wheezing, dizziness, rapid heart rate, abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, or a sudden drop in blood pressure.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Trigger Identification: Diligently work to identify and avoid known triggers, whether they are foods, medications, environmental allergens, or physical stimuli. Maintain a symptom diary to track potential causes.
  • Avoid Scratching: Refrain from scratching the affected areas, as this can worsen the itching, damage the skin, and increase the risk of secondary skin infections.
  • Monitor for Anaphylaxis: Be vigilant for signs of a severe allergic reaction, such as difficulty breathing, swelling of the throat or tongue, dizziness, or a sudden feeling of weakness. Seek immediate emergency medical attention if these symptoms occur.
  • Epinephrine Auto-Injector: If prescribed an epinephrine auto-injector for severe allergies, ensure it is always carried and that you and your caregivers know how to use it correctly.
  • Medication Review: Discuss all current medications, including over-the-counter drugs and supplements, with your healthcare provider, as some can induce or exacerbate hives.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Identify Food Triggers: If food allergies are suspected, work with an allergist to identify specific dietary triggers such as nuts, shellfish, dairy, eggs, soy, wheat, or certain food additives and preservatives.
  • Elimination Diet: Under medical supervision, an elimination diet may be recommended to systematically remove potential food allergens and then reintroduce them to pinpoint the exact cause of hives.
  • Avoid Known Allergens: Strictly avoid any foods or ingredients that have been identified as triggers for your hives. Always check food labels carefully.
  • Hydration: Maintain adequate hydration by drinking plenty of water, which supports overall skin health and can help manage discomfort.
  • Histamine-Releasing Foods: Some individuals with chronic hives may benefit from temporarily limiting foods known to release histamine, such as strawberries, tomatoes, spinach, fermented foods, and aged cheeses, under professional guidance.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Emergency Care: Seek immediate emergency medical attention if hives are accompanied by difficulty breathing, swelling of the face or throat, dizziness, or a sudden drop in blood pressure, as these are signs of anaphylaxis.
  • Physician Consultation: Consult a healthcare provider if hives are persistent (lasting more than a few days), severe, recurrent, or if they are accompanied by angioedema or other concerning symptoms.
  • Avoid Self-Diagnosis: Do not attempt to self-diagnose or self-treat severe or chronic hives without professional medical advice, as underlying conditions may require specific management.
  • Medication Adherence: If prescribed antihistamines or other medications, take them as directed by your physician to manage symptoms and prevent recurrence.
  • Allergy Testing: Your doctor may recommend allergy testing (skin prick tests or blood tests) to identify specific allergens contributing to your hives.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Comprehensive Assessment: A thorough medical history, including details about symptom onset, duration, potential triggers, and associated symptoms, is crucial for diagnosis. A physical examination will confirm the presence of wheals and assess for angioedema.
  • Diagnostic Approach: For acute hives, the cause is often evident. For chronic hives, investigations may include blood tests to rule out underlying infections, autoimmune conditions, or thyroid disorders, and allergy testing to identify specific allergens.
  • Treatment Strategy: First-line treatment typically involves non-sedating H1 antihistamines, often at higher-than-standard doses for chronic cases. Oral corticosteroids may be used for severe, acute flares.
  • Trigger Management: Emphasize the importance of identifying and avoiding triggers. For physical urticaria, specific avoidance strategies (e.g., avoiding cold for cold urticaria) are key.
  • Specialist Referral: Patients with chronic, severe, or refractory hives, or those with suspected autoimmune urticaria, should be referred to an allergist or immunologist for specialized management, which may include advanced therapies like omalizumab.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Pathophysiology: Hives result from the release of histamine and other inflammatory mediators from mast cells and basophils in the skin, leading to vasodilation, increased vascular permeability, and dermal edema.
  • Nursing Assessment: Conduct a detailed skin assessment, noting the location, size, color, morphology, and distribution of wheals. Assess for signs of angioedema, particularly airway involvement. Monitor vital signs and patient comfort level.
  • Patient Education: Educate patients on trigger identification and avoidance strategies, proper use of prescribed medications (antihistamines, epinephrine auto-injectors), and when to seek emergency care. Discuss the importance of not scratching.
  • Documentation: Accurately document the characteristics of the hives, any associated symptoms, patient's response to interventions, and all patient education provided.
  • Emergency Preparedness: Ensure patients at risk for anaphylaxis understand their emergency action plan and how to administer epinephrine.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Urticaria: The medical term for hives, characterized by transient, pruritic, erythematous, and edematous wheals.
  • Angioedema: Swelling of the deeper dermal and subcutaneous tissues, often associated with urticaria, and potentially life-threatening if it affects the airway.
  • Histamine: A potent vasoactive amine released by mast cells and basophils, central to the pathogenesis of urticaria and allergic reactions.
  • Wheal: A transient, circumscribed, elevated papule or plaque of edema in the dermis, typically red and itchy, characteristic of urticaria.
  • Anaphylaxis: A severe, potentially life-threatening systemic allergic reaction that can include hives, angioedema, respiratory distress, and cardiovascular collapse.
  • Dermatographism: A form of physical urticaria where hives develop in response to firm stroking or scratching of the skin.
  • Chronic Spontaneous Urticaria (CSU): Hives that occur without an identifiable external trigger for more than six weeks.
