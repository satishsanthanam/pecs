Scabies

🏠 Home / Skin, hair, and nails / Insects and parasites

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Nature of Infestation: Scabies is a highly contagious skin condition caused by the microscopic mite, Sarcoptes scabiei.
  • Transmission: Primarily spreads through direct, prolonged skin-to-skin contact. Less commonly, it can be transmitted via shared clothing, bedding, or towels.
  • Incubation Period: Symptoms typically appear 2-6 weeks after initial infestation in individuals without prior exposure, but within 1-4 days in re-infested individuals.
  • Vulnerability: Anyone can get scabies, regardless of age, gender, race, or socioeconomic status. Crusted (Norwegian) scabies is a severe form, particularly affecting immunocompromised individuals, the elderly, or those with neurological impairments.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Intense Pruritus: Severe, relentless itching, which is often worse at night and can significantly disrupt sleep.
  • Characteristic Rash: Small, red bumps, blisters, or pustules, often appearing in lines or clusters.
  • Scabies Burrows: Tiny, irregular, grayish-white or skin-colored lines on the skin surface, representing the mite's tunnels. These are often difficult to see.
  • Common Locations: Most frequently found in the webbing between fingers, wrists, elbows, armpits, waistline, nipples, genitalia, and buttocks. In infants and young children, the palms, soles, head, and neck can also be affected.
  • Secondary Infections: Persistent scratching can lead to skin breakdown, increasing the risk of bacterial infections such as impetigo or cellulitis.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Simultaneous Treatment: All household members and close contacts of an infested individual should be treated at the same time, even if they show no symptoms, to prevent re-infestation.
  • Laundry Protocol: Wash all clothing, bedding, and towels used by the infested person (and close contacts) in hot water (at least 122°F/50°C) and dry on high heat.
  • Non-Washable Items: Items that cannot be washed should be sealed in a plastic bag for at least 72 hours to starve the mites.
  • Environmental Cleaning: Vacuum carpets and upholstered furniture thoroughly, then discard the vacuum bag immediately.
  • Avoid Sharing: Do not share clothing, towels, or bedding with others, especially during an active infestation.
  • Contact Avoidance: Limit direct skin-to-skin contact with others until the treatment course is completed and confirmed effective.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Hydration Support: Maintain adequate fluid intake to support overall skin health and aid in the healing process, especially if scratching has led to skin irritation or breakdown.
  • Nutrient-Rich Diet: Consume a balanced diet rich in vitamins (e.g., Vitamin C for collagen synthesis) and minerals (e.g., Zinc for immune function) to support skin repair and immune response.
  • Medication Adherence: If oral medications like ivermectin are prescribed, follow specific instructions regarding food intake (e.g., taking with food to enhance absorption and reduce gastrointestinal upset) to ensure optimal efficacy.
  • Avoid Irritants: While not directly impacting mites, avoiding excessive alcohol or highly processed foods may help reduce general skin inflammation or flushing, which could indirectly exacerbate discomfort from itching.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Complete Treatment Course: It is imperative to complete the entire prescribed course of medication, even if symptoms appear to improve, to ensure all mites and eggs are eradicated.
  • Re-application Schedule: A second application of topical medication is often recommended 7-14 days after the first to kill any newly hatched mites that may have survived the initial treatment.
  • Post-Treatment Itching: Itching can persist for several weeks after successful treatment due to an allergic reaction to dead mites and mite products. This does not necessarily indicate treatment failure.
  • Monitor for Re-infestation: Watch for new burrows or rashes. If symptoms persist or worsen after completing the full treatment regimen, consult a healthcare provider for re-evaluation.
  • Special Populations: For infants, young children, the elderly, or immunocompromised individuals, ensure medication is applied to the entire body, including the scalp, face (avoiding eyes and mouth), and neck.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Accurate Diagnosis: Clinical diagnosis is often based on characteristic symptoms and lesion distribution, ideally confirmed by identifying mites, eggs, or fecal matter (scybala) from skin scrapings under a microscope.
  • Treatment Modalities: First-line treatment typically involves topical scabicides such as permethrin cream. Oral ivermectin may be used for widespread infestations, crusted scabies, or when topical treatments are impractical or ineffective.
  • Patient Education Imperative: Thorough patient and family education on medication application, environmental decontamination, and prevention of re-infestation is critical for successful outcomes.
  • Contact Tracing: Advise patients to inform all close contacts (sexual partners, household members) so they can seek simultaneous treatment, even if asymptomatic.
  • Differential Diagnosis: Consider other pruritic dermatoses such as eczema, allergic contact dermatitis, or insect bite reactions when evaluating patients with itching and rash.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Comprehensive Assessment: Conduct a thorough skin assessment, noting the location, morphology of lesions, presence of burrows, and severity of pruritus, especially at night.
  • Medication Application Education: Provide detailed instructions to patients and caregivers on the correct application technique for topical scabicides, emphasizing full body coverage (from the neck down, or head to toe for specific populations).
  • Infection Control: Implement contact precautions in healthcare settings for patients with suspected or confirmed scabies to prevent nosocomial transmission.
  • Environmental Decontamination Guidance: Educate patients on proper laundry and cleaning protocols for clothing, bedding, and household items to eliminate mites from the environment.
  • Psychosocial Support: Address the patient's distress related to intense itching, sleep disturbance, and potential social stigma associated with parasitic infestations.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Etiological Agent: Sarcoptes scabiei var. hominis, an obligate human parasite.
  • Pathophysiology: Female mites burrow into the stratum corneum, creating tunnels where they lay eggs and deposit fecal pellets (scybala), triggering a delayed-type hypersensitivity reaction.
  • Diagnostic Tools: Clinical examination, dermoscopy (visualizing the 'delta wing sign' of the mite), and microscopic examination of skin scrapings for mites, eggs, or scybala.
  • Pharmacological Agents: Permethrin (topical synthetic pyrethroid, neurotoxic to mites), Ivermectin (oral antiparasitic, GABA agonist), Crotamiton (topical, less effective), Sulfur ointment (historical, still used in some regions).
  • Potential Complications: Secondary bacterial infections (e.g., impetigo, cellulitis caused by Staphylococcus aureus or Group A Streptococcus), post-scabetic pruritus, and rarely, acute post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis.
  • ICD-10 Code: B86 (Scabies).
