Pressure sores

🏠 Home / Skin, hair, and nails / Pressure sores

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Definition: Pressure sores, also known as decubitus ulcers or bedsores, are localized injuries to the skin and/or underlying tissue, usually over a bony prominence, as a result of pressure, or pressure in combination with shear.
  • Common Locations: Frequently develop on the sacrum, heels, ankles, hips, and elbows, but can occur anywhere sustained pressure is applied.
  • Risk Factors: Immobility, advanced age, poor nutrition, incontinence, impaired sensation, chronic illnesses (e.g., diabetes, peripheral vascular disease), and altered mental status.
  • Staging: Classified into stages (1-4, unstageable, deep tissue injury) based on the extent of tissue damage, guiding treatment and prognosis.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Stage 1: Non-blanchable erythema of intact skin; may be painful, firm, soft, warmer, or cooler compared to adjacent tissue.
  • Stage 2: Partial-thickness skin loss involving epidermis and/or dermis; presents as a shallow open ulcer with a red-pink wound bed, or an intact or ruptured serum-filled blister.
  • Stage 3: Full-thickness skin loss; subcutaneous fat may be visible, but bone, tendon, or muscle are not exposed. Slough may be present.
  • Stage 4: Full-thickness tissue loss with exposed bone, tendon, or muscle. Slough or eschar may be present. Often includes undermining and tunneling.
  • Deep Tissue Injury (DTI): Persistent non-blanchable deep red, maroon, or purple discoloration, or blood-filled blister due to damage of underlying soft tissue from pressure and/or shear.
  • Unstageable: Full-thickness skin and tissue loss in which the extent of tissue damage within the ulcer cannot be confirmed because it is obscured by slough or eschar.
  • Infection Signs: Increased pain, redness, warmth, swelling, purulent drainage, foul odor, and fever.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Regular Repositioning: Turn and reposition bed-bound individuals at least every two hours, and chair-bound individuals every hour, or as per individualized care plan.
  • Skin Assessment: Conduct daily, thorough skin inspections, paying close attention to bony prominences and areas under medical devices. Document findings meticulously.
  • Moisture Management: Keep skin clean and dry, especially in cases of incontinence. Use moisture barriers and absorbent pads as needed.
  • Pressure Redistribution Surfaces: Utilize specialized mattresses, overlays, and cushions (e.g., air-fluidized, low-air-loss, foam) to reduce pressure on vulnerable areas.
  • Nutrition and Hydration: Ensure adequate intake of protein, calories, vitamins (especially C), and minerals (especially zinc) to support skin integrity and wound healing. Maintain optimal hydration.
  • Minimize Shear and Friction: Use proper lifting techniques (e.g., lift sheets) to avoid dragging patients across surfaces. Elevate the head of the bed no more than 30 degrees to reduce shear.
  • Patient Education: Educate patients and caregivers on risk factors, early signs, and preventive measures for pressure sore development.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • High Protein Intake: Essential for tissue repair and collagen synthesis. Encourage lean meats, poultry, fish, eggs, dairy products, legumes, and protein supplements if oral intake is insufficient.
  • Vitamin C Supplementation: Crucial for collagen formation and immune function. Found in citrus fruits, berries, bell peppers, and leafy greens.
  • Zinc Supplementation: Important for cell proliferation, immune function, and wound healing. Sources include red meat, poultry, beans, nuts, and whole grains.
  • Adequate Hydration: Maintain optimal fluid intake to support skin turgor and overall physiological function. Encourage water, clear broths, and other non-caffeinated, non-sugary fluids.
  • Caloric Sufficiency: Ensure sufficient caloric intake to prevent catabolism and provide energy for healing. May require nutrient-dense foods or oral nutritional supplements.
  • Avoid Malnutrition: Screen for and address any signs of malnutrition promptly, as it significantly impairs wound healing.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Strict Repositioning Schedule: Adhere rigorously to the prescribed turning schedule, documenting each turn.
  • Thorough Skin Checks: Perform daily head-to-toe skin assessments, noting any redness, warmth, tenderness, or breaks in skin integrity, especially over bony prominences.
  • Incontinence Care: Provide immediate and thorough perineal care after each incontinent episode, using mild cleansers and applying moisture barrier creams.
  • Proper Positioning Devices: Ensure correct use and placement of pillows, wedges, and pressure-redistributing devices to offload pressure effectively.
  • Foot Care: Pay special attention to heels; use heel protectors or elevate heels off the bed surface.
  • Reporting Changes: Immediately report any new skin breakdown, worsening of existing sores, or signs of infection (e.g., fever, increased pain, purulent drainage) to the nursing staff or physician.
  • Assist with Mobility: Encourage and assist with ambulation or active range of motion exercises as tolerated to improve circulation.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Accurate Staging: Crucial for guiding treatment and prognosis. Re-evaluate staging regularly as the wound evolves.
  • Debridement: Remove necrotic tissue (slough, eschar) through surgical, mechanical, enzymatic, autolytic, or biological methods to promote healing.
  • Wound Dressings: Select appropriate dressings based on wound characteristics (e.g., exudate level, presence of infection, depth) to maintain a moist wound environment and protect from contamination.
  • Infection Management: Obtain wound cultures for suspected infection and initiate systemic or topical antibiotics as indicated. Address underlying osteomyelitis if present.
  • Pain Management: Assess and manage pain effectively, as pressure sores can be highly painful, impacting patient comfort and cooperation.
  • Nutritional Support: Collaborate with dietitians to optimize nutritional status, potentially including enteral or parenteral nutrition for severe cases.
  • Surgical Intervention: Consider surgical repair (e.g., skin grafts, flaps) for deep, non-healing pressure sores, especially Stage 3 and 4, after infection control and nutritional optimization.
  • Multidisciplinary Approach: Emphasize a team-based approach involving nursing, physical therapy, occupational therapy, nutrition, and wound care specialists.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Braden Scale: Utilize the Braden Scale for Predicting Pressure Sore Risk to systematically assess patient risk factors (sensory perception, moisture, activity, mobility, nutrition, friction & shear).
  • Wound Assessment Documentation: Document wound location, size (length, width, depth), stage, presence of tunneling/undermining, exudate type and amount, odor, periwound skin condition, and pain level.
  • Aseptic Technique: Adhere strictly to aseptic technique during wound care to prevent infection.
  • Patient and Family Education: Educate on the importance of repositioning, skin care, nutrition, and early signs of complications. Empower them to participate in care.
  • Pressure Mapping: Understand the principles of pressure mapping to identify high-pressure areas and guide the selection of appropriate support surfaces.
  • Interprofessional Collaboration: Actively participate in interdisciplinary rounds to discuss patient progress, challenges, and care plan adjustments.
  • Evidence-Based Practice: Stay updated on current evidence-based guidelines for pressure injury prevention and management (e.g., NPUAP/EPUAP/PPPIA guidelines).

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • NPUAP/EPUAP/PPPIA: National Pressure Ulcer Advisory Panel / European Pressure Ulcer Advisory Panel / Pan Pacific Pressure Injury Alliance – leading organizations for pressure injury classification and guidelines.
  • Braden Scale: A widely used risk assessment tool for predicting pressure injury risk, scoring six subscales.
  • Deep Tissue Injury (DTI): A pressure injury characterized by persistent non-blanchable deep red, maroon, or purple discoloration, or a blood-filled blister.
  • Undermining: Tissue destruction underlying intact skin at the wound margin, creating a 'lip' or 'shelf'.
  • Tunneling: A channel or pathway extending from the wound into the surrounding tissue.
  • Eschar: A thick, leathery, often black or brown devitalized tissue that adheres to the wound bed.
  • Slough: Yellow, tan, gray, green, or brown non-viable tissue, often stringy or mucinous, that adheres to the wound bed.
  • Offloading: The practice of relieving pressure from a specific area of the body to prevent or treat pressure injuries.
