# 🩺 Medical Content

## 💡 What You Need to Know
* **Immediate Action:** Do NOT rub the affected eye, as this can embed the object further or cause more damage.
* **Severity Assessment:** Distinguish between a superficial irritant (like dust) and a potentially penetrating injury (like metal shards or glass). Superficial objects may be flushed, but embedded objects require immediate professional medical attention.
* **When to Seek Help:** Always seek urgent medical care if the object is embedded, vision is impaired, pain is severe, or if you suspect a chemical splash.
* **Protective Measures:** Emphasize the importance of wearing appropriate protective eyewear in environments where eye injuries are a risk (e.g., construction, gardening, laboratory work).


## 🤒 Associated Symptoms
* **Foreign Body Sensation:** A persistent feeling that something is in the eye, often described as gritty or scratchy.
* **Pain or Discomfort:** Ranging from mild irritation to severe, sharp pain, especially with eye movement or blinking.
* **Excessive Tearing:** The eye's natural response to try and flush out the irritant.
* **Redness:** Conjunctival injection, making the white part of the eye appear red or bloodshot.
* **Light Sensitivity:** Photophobia, where bright lights cause discomfort or pain.
* **Blurred or Decreased Vision:** If the object is on the cornea or has caused significant irritation/damage.
* **Eyelid Spasm:** Involuntary twitching or clamping shut of the eyelid (blepharospasm) due to irritation.


## 🛡️ Crucial Precautions
* **Hand Hygiene:** Always wash hands thoroughly with soap and water before attempting any eye examination or removal to prevent infection.
* **Avoid Rubbing:** Reiterate the critical instruction to never rub the eye, as this can worsen the injury.
* **No Forceful Removal:** Never use sharp objects, cotton swabs, or fingers to forcefully remove an object, especially if it feels embedded.
* **Chemical Exposure:** For chemical splashes, immediately flush the eye with copious amounts of water for at least 15-20 minutes and seek emergency medical care.
* **Penetrating Injuries:** Do not attempt to remove any object that has penetrated the eye. Cover the eye with a protective shield (e.g., a paper cup) and seek immediate emergency medical attention.
* **Post-Removal Monitoring:** Even after successful removal, monitor the eye for signs of infection, persistent pain, redness, or vision changes, and seek follow-up care if symptoms persist.


## 🍽️ Dietary Directions & Restrictions
* **Pre-Procedure Fasting:** If surgical removal under local or general anesthesia is anticipated for an embedded object, adhere strictly to NPO (nil per os) guidelines as instructed by the medical team.
* **Post-Procedure Hydration:** After any sedation or anesthesia, gradually reintroduce clear fluids as tolerated, progressing to a light diet as advised by healthcare providers.
* **Nutritional Support for Healing:** Ensure adequate intake of nutrient-rich foods, particularly those high in Vitamin C and Zinc, to support tissue repair and immune function, especially if there is a risk of infection or significant injury.
* **Avoid Irritants:** While not directly related to eye injury, avoiding highly acidic or spicy foods may be advisable if the patient is experiencing nausea or discomfort from pain or medication.


## ⚠️ Attendant Guidelines
* **Maintain Calm:** Reassure the patient and help them remain calm, as anxiety can worsen involuntary eye movements.
* **Prevent Rubbing:** Gently but firmly prevent the patient, especially children, from rubbing or touching the affected eye.
* **Safe Transport:** If medical attention is required, ensure safe and prompt transport to an emergency department or ophthalmologist, minimizing jostling.
* **Do Not Attempt Removal (Embedded Objects):** Instruct attendants never to attempt removal of objects that appear embedded or are causing significant pain/vision loss.
* **Documentation:** Note the time of injury, the nature of the object, any initial symptoms, and any attempts made to remove the object before professional help arrived.


## 🩺 Physician's Perspective
* **Thorough Ocular Examination:** A comprehensive examination using a slit lamp is crucial to identify the foreign body, assess corneal or conjunctival damage, and rule out intraocular penetration.
* **Visual Acuity Assessment:** Baseline and post-intervention visual acuity measurements are essential for monitoring and prognosis.
* **Topical Anesthesia:** Application of topical anesthetic drops is often necessary to facilitate examination and removal, minimizing patient discomfort.
* **Irrigation Techniques:** Copious irrigation with sterile saline solution is the first-line approach for superficial foreign bodies.
* **Instrumental Removal:** For non-embedded objects not removed by irrigation, sterile instruments (e.g., sterile cotton swab, spud, or fine needle) may be used under magnification.
* **Prophylactic Antibiotics:** Topical antibiotic drops or ointment are typically prescribed post-removal to prevent infection, especially after corneal abrasion.
* **Tetanus Prophylaxis:** Assess tetanus immunization status and administer prophylaxis if indicated, particularly for metallic foreign bodies.
* **Follow-up Care:** Schedule follow-up appointments to monitor healing, ensure complete resolution, and address any persistent symptoms or complications.


## 🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner
* **Patient Assessment:** Systematically assess visual acuity, pupil reaction, extraocular movements, and the presence/location of the foreign body.
* **Patient Education:** Educate patients on proper eye care post-removal, including medication administration, signs of infection (increased pain, discharge, worsening redness), and when to seek further medical attention.
* **Sterile Technique:** Emphasize strict adherence to sterile technique during eye irrigation and any assisted removal procedures to prevent iatrogenic infection.
* **Pain Management:** Administer prescribed topical anesthetics or systemic analgesics as needed, and assess pain levels before and after interventions.
* **Documentation:** Meticulously document the type of foreign body, location, method of removal, patient's response, visual acuity changes, and all post-procedure instructions given.
* **Referral Pathways:** Understand the criteria for immediate referral to an ophthalmologist, especially for suspected intraocular foreign bodies, chemical burns, or significant vision loss.


## 🔬 Clinical Reference Index
* **Terminology:** Foreign Body Sensation (FBS), Corneal Abrasion, Conjunctivitis, Intraocular Foreign Body (IOFB), Blepharospasm, Photophobia, Traumatic Iritis.
* **Diagnostic Procedures:** Slit Lamp Examination, Fluorescein Staining, Ocular Irrigation, Visual Acuity Testing, Ocular Ultrasound (for IOFB), CT Scan (for IOFB).
* **Anatomical Structures:** Cornea, Conjunctiva, Sclera, Limbus, Anterior Chamber, Iris, Pupil, Lens, Retina.
* **Complications:** Corneal Ulcer, Endophthalmitis, Traumatic Cataract, Retinal Detachment, Glaucoma, Vision Loss.
* **ICD-10 Codes:** T15.00XA (Foreign body in cornea, unspecified eye, initial encounter), T15.10XA (Foreign body in conjunctival sac, unspecified eye, initial encounter), H16.11 (Corneal abrasion).
* **Pharmacology:** Topical Anesthetics (e.g., Proparacaine), Topical Antibiotics (e.g., Moxifloxacin, Erythromycin), Cycloplegics (e.g., Cyclopentolate for traumatic iritis).
