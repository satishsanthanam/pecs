Photokeratitis (arc eye)

🏠 Home / Eyes and vision / Eye injuries

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Definition: Photokeratitis, also known as arc eye or snow blindness, is a painful inflammation of the cornea caused by exposure to intense ultraviolet (UV) radiation.
  • Common Causes: Exposure to welding arcs without proper eye protection, prolonged sun exposure on reflective surfaces like snow or water, sunlamps, and tanning beds.
  • Symptom Onset: Symptoms typically develop several hours (usually 6-12 hours) after exposure, often worsening overnight.
  • Prognosis: The condition is usually temporary, resolving within 24-48 hours without permanent vision loss, but can be extremely painful during the acute phase.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Severe Eye Pain: Often described as a gritty sensation, foreign body sensation, or burning pain in both eyes.
  • Photophobia: Extreme sensitivity to light, making it difficult to keep eyes open in bright environments.
  • Lacrimation: Excessive tearing and watery eyes.
  • Conjunctival Redness: Visible redness of the white part of the eyes.
  • Blurred Vision: Temporary decrease in visual acuity.
  • Blepharospasm: Involuntary twitching or spasm of the eyelids.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Wear Protective Eyewear: Always use appropriate UV-blocking eyewear, such as welding helmets with correct shade filters (e.g., ANSI Z87.1 standard), 100% UV-protective sunglasses, or ski goggles.
  • Avoid Direct UV Exposure: Never look directly at a welding arc, sunlamp, or the sun, especially when reflected off snow, water, or sand, without certified eye protection.
  • Workplace Safety: Ensure proper shielding and ventilation in environments where welding or other UV-emitting equipment is used.
  • Contact Lens Removal: Remove contact lenses immediately if symptoms develop, as they can trap irritants and exacerbate discomfort.
  • Avoid Eye Rubbing: Refrain from rubbing the eyes, as this can worsen irritation and potentially cause further corneal damage.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Maintain Hydration: Ensure adequate fluid intake to support overall physiological comfort, especially if experiencing significant lacrimation.
  • Comfort Foods: Opt for soft, easy-to-digest foods if general discomfort or nausea is present due to severe pain.
  • Avoid Irritants: Limit consumption of alcohol or caffeine, which can sometimes contribute to dehydration or exacerbate discomfort in some individuals.
  • Nutrient Support: While not a direct treatment, a diet rich in antioxidants (e.g., Vitamin C, E, Zinc) can support general eye health and recovery.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Seek Medical Attention: Consult an ophthalmologist or emergency department for severe pain, persistent symptoms beyond 48 hours, or any changes in vision.
  • Pain Management: Over-the-counter oral analgesics (e.g., ibuprofen, acetaminophen) can help manage discomfort.
  • Cold Compresses: Apply cool, damp compresses to closed eyelids for symptomatic relief.
  • Dark Environment: Rest in a dark room to minimize photophobia and promote comfort.
  • Driving Restriction: Avoid driving or operating heavy machinery until vision is clear and pain has subsided completely.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Diagnosis: Clinical diagnosis is based on a history of UV exposure and characteristic symptoms. A slit-lamp examination may reveal diffuse punctate corneal epithelial defects.
  • Treatment Approach: Primarily supportive care, which may include topical cycloplegic agents (e.g., cyclopentolate) to reduce ciliary spasm and pain, topical broad-spectrum antibiotics (e.g., erythromycin ointment) to prevent secondary infection if epithelial defects are significant, and oral analgesics.
  • Patient Education: Emphasize the importance of strict adherence to eye protection guidelines to prevent recurrence.
  • Follow-up: Advise patients on the necessity of follow-up if symptoms do not improve or worsen within the expected timeframe.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Patient Assessment: Conduct a thorough pain assessment and evaluate the severity of photophobia and lacrimation.
  • Education on Prevention: Educate patients on the critical role of proper eye protection in various environments (e.g., welding, skiing, tanning).
  • Medication Administration: Administer prescribed topical eye drops (cycloplegics, antibiotics) and oral pain relief as ordered, ensuring proper technique for eye drop instillation.
  • Environmental Modification: Assist patients in creating a comfortable, dark, and quiet environment to minimize discomfort.
  • Monitoring for Complications: Monitor for signs of worsening symptoms, secondary infection, or delayed healing, and report to the physician.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • ICD-10 Code: H16.11 (Photokeratitis).
  • Pathophysiology: UV-B radiation absorption by corneal epithelial cells leads to cellular damage, apoptosis, and subsequent inflammatory response, resulting in epithelial defects.
  • Differential Diagnosis: Corneal abrasion, foreign body sensation, acute conjunctivitis, chemical keratitis, viral keratitis (e.g., herpes simplex keratitis).
  • Pharmacology: Cyclopentolate (0.5-1% drops) for cycloplegia; topical erythromycin or polymyxin B/trimethoprim for prophylactic antibiotic coverage; oral NSAIDs for pain.
  • Prognosis Factors: Generally excellent with complete recovery within 24-48 hours; rare complications include recurrent corneal erosions or persistent discomfort in severe cases.
