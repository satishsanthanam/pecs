Corneal abrasion

🏠 Home / Eyes and vision / Eye injuries

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Definition: A corneal abrasion is a scratch or scrape on the cornea, the clear, dome-shaped outer surface of the eye.
  • Common Causes: Often results from foreign bodies (e.g., dust, sand), trauma (e.g., fingernail, tree branch), or improper contact lens use.
  • Severity: Can range from minor discomfort to severe pain and temporary vision impairment, depending on the size and depth of the abrasion.
  • Healing Process: The corneal epithelium has a remarkable capacity for rapid regeneration, with most simple abrasions healing within 24-72 hours.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Acute Pain: Often described as a sharp, gritty, or foreign body sensation in the affected eye.
  • Photophobia: Increased sensitivity and discomfort when exposed to light.
  • Excessive Tearing: Reflexive watering of the eye in response to irritation and pain.
  • Redness: Conjunctival injection, causing the eye to appear red or bloodshot.
  • Blurred Vision: May occur if the abrasion is large, centrally located, or causes significant corneal edema.
  • Eyelid Spasm: Involuntary twitching or closing of the eyelid due to pain and irritation.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Do Not Rub Eye: Rubbing the affected eye can worsen the abrasion, introduce infection, or embed a foreign body deeper.
  • Seek Prompt Medical Attention: Any suspected corneal abrasion requires immediate evaluation by an ophthalmologist or emergency physician to prevent complications.
  • Avoid Self-Treatment: Do not attempt to remove foreign bodies yourself or use unprescribed eye drops, as this can cause further harm.
  • Contact Lens Hygiene: If a contact lens wearer, discontinue use immediately and ensure strict hygiene practices to prevent future abrasions and infections.
  • Eye Protection: Wear appropriate safety eyewear during activities that pose a risk of eye injury, such as sports, gardening, or industrial work.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Medication-Related Guidelines: If oral pain relievers or antibiotics are prescribed, adhere to specific instructions regarding food intake (e.g., take with food to minimize gastrointestinal upset or enhance absorption).
  • Hydration Status: Maintain adequate fluid intake to support overall physiological processes, including tissue regeneration and immune function, which are vital for healing.
  • Avoid Irritants: While not directly dietary, avoid substances that may cause systemic dehydration or inflammation, which could indirectly impede tissue repair or exacerbate discomfort.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Monitor for Worsening Symptoms: Watch for increasing pain, worsening vision, persistent redness, or any purulent discharge, which could indicate infection.
  • Emergency Care: Seek immediate medical attention if symptoms do not improve within 24-48 hours, or if vision suddenly deteriorates.
  • Follow-up Appointments: Adhere strictly to all scheduled follow-up appointments with the ophthalmologist to monitor healing and prevent complications.
  • Medication Compliance: Use prescribed antibiotic eye drops or ointments exactly as directed, even if symptoms improve, to prevent bacterial infection.
  • Avoid Eye Makeup: Refrain from using eye makeup until the abrasion has fully healed to prevent irritation and potential infection.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Diagnosis Confirmation: Diagnosis is typically made via slit lamp examination after instilling fluorescein dye, which highlights the abrasion.
  • Treatment Modalities: Management usually involves topical antibiotic drops or ointment to prevent infection, cycloplegic agents to relieve ciliary spasm and pain, and sometimes a temporary eye patch for comfort.
  • Pain Management: Oral analgesics may be prescribed for significant pain, and patients should be advised on proper administration.
  • Complication Risk: Untreated or improperly managed abrasions can lead to serious complications such as corneal ulcers, recurrent corneal erosions, or permanent vision impairment.
  • Patient Education: Emphasize the importance of not rubbing the eye, proper medication use, and protective eyewear to prevent recurrence.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Comprehensive Assessment: Obtain a detailed history of the injury, assess visual acuity, evaluate pain level using a pain scale, and inspect the eye for foreign bodies or signs of infection.
  • Medication Administration: Educate patients on the correct technique for administering eye drops or ointment, emphasizing hand hygiene and avoiding contamination of the applicator tip.
  • Pain Management Support: Provide comfort measures and administer prescribed analgesics, ensuring the patient understands their use and potential side effects.
  • Patient Education Reinforcement: Instruct patients on eye protection, avoiding contact lens use until cleared by a physician, and recognizing signs of worsening condition or infection.
  • Documentation: Accurately document the injury, assessment findings, interventions, patient education provided, and the patient's response to treatment.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • ICD-10 Code: S05.0 (Corneal abrasion without foreign body). Specific codes exist for abrasions with foreign bodies.
  • Pathophysiology: Involves a disruption of the corneal epithelium, the outermost layer of the cornea, exposing underlying nerve endings and stroma.
  • Pharmacology: Common topical antibiotics include erythromycin, tobramycin, ciprofloxacin, or moxifloxacin. Cycloplegics like cyclopentolate or homatropine may be used for pain from ciliary spasm.
  • Differential Diagnosis: Includes corneal foreign body, corneal ulcer, recurrent corneal erosion syndrome, acute conjunctivitis, and uveitis.
  • Diagnostic Tools: Slit lamp biomicroscopy with fluorescein staining is the gold standard for visualizing and assessing the extent of the abrasion.
