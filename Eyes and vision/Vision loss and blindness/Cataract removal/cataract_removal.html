Cataract removal

🏠 Home / Eyes and vision / Vision loss and blindness

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Procedure Overview: Cataract removal is a common surgical procedure to replace a cloudy natural lens with an artificial intraocular lens (IOL) to restore clear vision.
  • Primary Goal: To alleviate vision impairment caused by cataracts, such as blurriness, glare, and faded colors, significantly improving quality of life.
  • Technique: Most commonly performed using phacoemulsification, where ultrasound waves break up the cloudy lens, which is then suctioned out.
  • Lens Replacement: A clear, artificial IOL is implanted, which remains permanently in the eye.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Cloudy or Blurry Vision: A gradual, painless blurring or dimming of vision, often described as looking through a frosty window.
  • Difficulty with Night Vision: Increased trouble seeing in low light conditions or driving at night due to glare from headlights.
  • Sensitivity to Light and Glare: Experiencing halos around lights or discomfort from bright lights.
  • Fading or Yellowing of Colors: Colors may appear less vibrant or have a yellowish tint.
  • Frequent Prescription Changes: Needing more frequent changes in eyeglasses or contact lens prescriptions, often with diminishing improvement.
  • Double Vision in One Eye: Occasionally, cataracts can cause monocular diplopia (double vision in a single eye).

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Pre-operative Medication Review: Inform your surgeon about all medications, especially blood thinners (e.g., aspirin, warfarin), which may need to be stopped several days prior to surgery as directed.
  • Fasting Instructions: Adhere strictly to NPO (nil per os) guidelines, typically no food or drink after midnight the day before surgery.
  • Avoid Eye Makeup: Refrain from wearing eye makeup, lotions, or perfumes on the day of surgery to minimize infection risk.
  • Post-operative Eye Protection: Wear the prescribed eye shield or glasses, especially during sleep and for the first week, to protect the healing eye from accidental rubbing or injury.
  • Activity Restrictions: Avoid heavy lifting, strenuous activities, bending at the waist, or any activity that increases pressure on the eye for several weeks post-surgery.
  • Water Exposure: Prevent water, soap, or shampoo from entering the operated eye for at least one week; use a clean, damp cloth to clean around the eye.
  • Avoid Eye Rubbing: Absolutely avoid rubbing or pressing on the operated eye to prevent dislodging the IOL or causing damage.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Pre-Procedure Fasting: Strictly adhere to the NPO (nil per os) order, typically no food or drink for 6-8 hours before the scheduled surgery time, to prevent aspiration during anesthesia.
  • Post-Procedure Hydration: Once cleared by medical staff, begin with sips of water or clear fluids to ensure tolerance before gradually reintroducing a light diet.
  • Alcohol Restriction: Avoid alcohol consumption for at least 24-48 hours post-surgery, especially if sedatives were administered, as it can interfere with medication and recovery.
  • Normal Diet Resumption: Unless otherwise instructed by your surgeon, you can typically resume your normal diet once you feel well and tolerate fluids.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Arranged Transportation: Ensure a responsible adult is available to drive you home after the procedure, as your vision may be blurry and you may be affected by sedatives.
  • Post-operative Assistance: Have someone available to assist with daily tasks and eye drop administration for the first 24-48 hours, especially if living alone.
  • Monitor for Complications: Watch for any signs of infection or complications, including sudden severe pain, significant redness, discharge from the eye, sudden decrease in vision, or flashes of light/floaters.
  • Emergency Contact: Know how to contact your surgeon or ophthalmologist immediately if any concerning symptoms arise outside of normal business hours.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Adherence to Eye Drop Regimen: Meticulously follow the prescribed schedule for antibiotic and anti-inflammatory eye drops to prevent infection and manage inflammation.
  • Regular Follow-up Appointments: Attend all scheduled post-operative visits to monitor healing, check intraocular pressure, and assess visual acuity.
  • Realistic Expectations: Understand that vision improvement is gradual, and complete healing may take several weeks. Some patients may still require glasses for certain activities.
  • Report Changes Promptly: Immediately contact your ophthalmologist if you experience any new or worsening symptoms such as severe pain, vision loss, excessive redness, or discharge.
  • Long-term Eye Health: Continue with regular comprehensive eye exams to monitor for other eye conditions and overall ocular health.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Pre-operative Assessment: Conduct thorough patient history, medication reconciliation, visual acuity testing, and reinforce NPO status and pre-op instructions.
  • Patient Education: Provide clear, concise instructions on post-operative care, including eye drop administration technique, activity restrictions, and signs/symptoms of complications.
  • Pain Management: Assess and manage post-operative discomfort, typically mild, with prescribed or over-the-counter analgesics.
  • Monitoring for Complications: Educate patients on recognizing and reporting signs of infection (e.g., endophthalmitis), retinal detachment, or increased intraocular pressure.
  • Discharge Planning: Ensure the patient has a clear understanding of follow-up appointments, medication schedules, and who to contact for concerns.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Phacoemulsification: A modern cataract surgery method using ultrasonic energy to emulsify the lens, allowing for removal through a small incision.
  • Intraocular Lens (IOL): An artificial lens implanted to replace the natural lens. Types include monofocal, multifocal, toric (for astigmatism), and extended depth of focus (EDOF) IOLs.
  • Posterior Capsular Opacification (PCO): A common late complication where the posterior capsule (which supports the IOL) becomes cloudy, treatable with YAG laser capsulotomy.
  • Endophthalmitis: A rare but severe post-operative infection inside the eye, requiring urgent treatment to preserve vision.
  • Retinal Detachment: A rare but serious complication where the retina separates from its underlying support tissue, requiring immediate surgical intervention.
  • Biometry: Pre-operative measurements of the eye's axial length and corneal curvature to calculate the appropriate IOL power.
