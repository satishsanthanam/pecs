Cellulitis around the eye

🏠 Home / Eyes and vision / Eye infections

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Definition: Cellulitis around the eye is an acute bacterial infection of the skin and subcutaneous tissues surrounding the eye.
  • Types: It is categorized into preseptal (periorbital) cellulitis, which affects tissues anterior to the orbital septum, and orbital (postseptal) cellulitis, which involves tissues posterior to the septum.
  • Severity: Preseptal cellulitis is generally less severe, while orbital cellulitis is a medical emergency due to its potential for vision loss and intracranial complications.
  • Common Causes: Often arises from local trauma, insect bites, skin infections, or spread from adjacent structures like the sinuses (especially for orbital cellulitis).

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Eyelid Swelling & Redness: Marked swelling, erythema, warmth, and tenderness of the eyelids are common in both types.
  • Pain: Localized pain around the eye, which can worsen with eye movement in orbital cellulitis.
  • Proptosis (Bulging Eye): A key indicator of orbital cellulitis, where the eyeball protrudes forward. Absent in preseptal cellulitis.
  • Ophthalmoplegia: Restricted or painful eye movements, a critical sign of orbital involvement.
  • Decreased Visual Acuity: Any reduction in vision is a severe symptom, strongly suggesting orbital cellulitis or optic nerve compression.
  • Fever & Malaise: Systemic symptoms like fever, chills, and general unwellness are more common with orbital cellulitis.
  • Diplopia (Double Vision): May occur due to impaired eye muscle function in orbital cellulitis.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Urgent Medical Evaluation: Seek immediate medical attention for any suspected cellulitis around the eye to differentiate between preseptal and orbital forms.
  • Avoid Self-Treatment: Do not attempt to self-diagnose, apply home remedies, or squeeze any lesions, as this can worsen the infection.
  • Monitor for Worsening Symptoms: Watch for signs like increasing pain with eye movement, vision changes, proptosis, or inability to move the eye, which indicate progression to orbital cellulitis.
  • Complete Antibiotic Course: Adhere strictly to the prescribed antibiotic regimen, even if symptoms improve, to prevent recurrence and complications.
  • Hand Hygiene: Practice meticulous hand hygiene and avoid touching or rubbing the affected eye to prevent further spread of bacteria.
  • Underlying Conditions: Ensure any underlying conditions, such as sinusitis or dental infections, are appropriately managed to prevent recurrence.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Hydration: Maintain adequate fluid intake, especially if fever is present, to support overall recovery and prevent dehydration.
  • Nutrient-Rich Diet: Consume a balanced diet rich in vitamins and minerals to support immune function during the infection.
  • Avoid Irritants: Limit intake of highly processed foods, excessive sugar, and alcohol, which may contribute to inflammation or impair immune response.
  • Soft Foods (if applicable): If facial swelling or pain makes chewing difficult, opt for softer, easy-to-swallow foods like soups, smoothies, or mashed vegetables.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Emergency Protocol: Orbital cellulitis is an ophthalmic emergency requiring immediate hospitalization, intravenous antibiotics, and often a multidisciplinary approach.
  • Diagnostic Imaging: A CT scan of the orbits and sinuses with contrast is essential to confirm diagnosis, differentiate preseptal from orbital cellulitis, and identify abscess formation.
  • Surgical Intervention: Surgical drainage of an orbital abscess or infected sinuses may be necessary if medical treatment fails or if vision is threatened.
  • Complication Monitoring: Closely monitor for potential severe complications such as vision loss, cavernous sinus thrombosis, meningitis, or brain abscess.
  • Pediatric Considerations: Children are particularly susceptible and require prompt and aggressive management due to higher risks of complications.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Prompt Differentiation: The most critical step is to rapidly distinguish between preseptal and orbital cellulitis, as management and prognosis differ significantly.
  • Empiric Antibiotics: Initial treatment typically involves broad-spectrum antibiotics covering common pathogens like *Staphylococcus aureus* and *Streptococcus pyogenes*, often administered intravenously for orbital cases.
  • Ophthalmology Consultation: Early consultation with an ophthalmologist is crucial for accurate diagnosis, monitoring, and management, especially for orbital involvement.
  • Serial Examinations: Regular clinical assessments of visual acuity, pupillary responses, extraocular movements, and proptosis are vital to track disease progression and treatment response.
  • Addressing Source: Identify and treat the underlying source of infection, such as sinusitis, dental infection, or skin trauma, to prevent recurrence.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Comprehensive Assessment: Perform thorough assessments including vital signs, visual acuity, pupillary light reflex, extraocular movements, and presence of proptosis.
  • Medication Management: Administer prescribed intravenous and oral antibiotics accurately, monitoring for adverse reactions and ensuring adherence.
  • Pain Management: Assess pain levels regularly and administer analgesics as ordered to ensure patient comfort.
  • Patient Education: Educate patients and caregivers on the importance of completing the antibiotic course, recognizing signs of worsening infection, and when to seek urgent medical care.
  • Infection Control: Implement standard infection control precautions to prevent the spread of infection.
  • Emotional Support: Provide emotional support to patients and families, especially given the potential severity and anxiety associated with eye infections.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • ICD-10 Codes: H00.0 (Hordeolum and chalazion - potential precursor), H05.01 (Acute inflammation of orbit), H05.03 (Cellulitis of orbit).
  • Differential Diagnosis: Allergic reaction, insect bite, orbital pseudotumor, rhabdomyosarcoma (in children), dacryocystitis, orbital fracture with emphysema.
  • Common Pathogens: *Staphylococcus aureus* (including MRSA), *Streptococcus pyogenes*, *Haemophilus influenzae* (less common due to vaccination).
  • Diagnostic Modalities: Clinical examination, complete blood count (CBC), blood cultures, wound cultures (if drainage present), CT scan of orbits and sinuses with contrast.
  • Treatment Modalities: Systemic antibiotics (e.g., amoxicillin-clavulanate, clindamycin, vancomycin + ceftriaxone/cefotaxime for orbital cases), surgical drainage of abscesses.
