Subconjunctival hemorrhage

🏠 Home / Eyes and vision / Eye inflammation

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Definition: A subconjunctival hemorrhage is a common and usually harmless condition where a small blood vessel breaks just beneath the conjunctiva, the clear membrane covering the white part of your eye.
  • Appearance: It presents as a bright red patch on the sclera (white part of the eye), which can range in size from a small spot to covering a significant portion.
  • Cause: Often results from minor trauma, sudden increases in venous pressure (e.g., coughing, sneezing, straining, vomiting), or can occur spontaneously without an obvious cause.
  • Nature: Typically painless and does not affect vision. It is analogous to a bruise on the skin and usually resolves on its own within one to two weeks.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Visible Red Patch: The primary and most noticeable symptom is a distinct, bright red area on the white of the eye.
  • Mild Irritation: Some individuals may experience a slight feeling of fullness or mild scratchiness on the eye surface, but usually no significant pain.
  • Absence of Vision Changes: Crucially, subconjunctival hemorrhage typically does not cause blurred vision, vision loss, or light sensitivity.
  • No Discharge: There is usually no associated eye discharge, tearing, or pus.
  • Clinical Indication for Concern: Recurrent hemorrhages, associated eye pain, vision changes, or a history of significant trauma warrant immediate medical evaluation.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Avoid Eye Rubbing: Refrain from rubbing the affected eye, as this can exacerbate irritation or potentially cause further vessel damage.
  • Monitor for Changes: Observe the hemorrhage for any increase in size, new pain, vision changes, or discharge, which could indicate a more serious underlying condition.
  • Manage Underlying Conditions: If you have conditions such as high blood pressure, diabetes, or bleeding disorders, ensure they are well-controlled, as these can increase the risk of recurrent hemorrhages.
  • Review Medications: Inform your physician about any anticoagulant or antiplatelet medications you are taking, as these can contribute to the severity or duration of the hemorrhage.
  • Avoid Strenuous Activities (Temporarily): If the hemorrhage is recent and extensive, consider avoiding activities that significantly increase intraocular pressure (e.g., heavy lifting, intense straining) until it begins to resolve.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Hydration: Maintain adequate fluid intake to support overall vascular health and systemic well-being.
  • Vitamin K Intake: If on anticoagulant therapy (e.g., warfarin), maintain a consistent intake of Vitamin K-rich foods (e.g., leafy greens) as advised by your healthcare provider to avoid fluctuations in clotting factors. Do not alter intake without medical guidance.
  • Alcohol Consumption: Limit or avoid excessive alcohol intake, as it can have a mild blood-thinning effect and may potentially prolong bleeding or increase the risk of recurrence.
  • Avoidance of Unnecessary Supplements: Refrain from taking any unprescribed supplements that may affect blood clotting (e.g., high-dose Vitamin E, ginkgo biloba) without consulting a physician, especially if you have a bleeding tendency.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • When to Seek Medical Attention: Consult a healthcare provider if the hemorrhage is accompanied by pain, vision changes, discharge, a history of significant eye trauma, or if you experience recurrent subconjunctival hemorrhages.
  • Self-Care for Irritation: Over-the-counter artificial tears can be used to alleviate any mild irritation or dryness associated with the hemorrhage.
  • Avoid Aspirin/NSAIDs (if not prescribed): If you are not already on these medications for a medical condition, avoid taking aspirin or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) unnecessarily, as they can thin the blood and potentially worsen or prolong the hemorrhage.
  • Cold Compress: A cold compress applied gently to the closed eyelid may provide comfort in the initial stages, though it does not directly affect the resolution of the hemorrhage.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Reassurance: Most subconjunctival hemorrhages are benign and resolve spontaneously without intervention.
  • Thorough Examination: A comprehensive eye examination is crucial to rule out other, more serious causes of eye redness, especially if there is pain, vision loss, or a history of trauma.
  • Investigation of Underlying Causes: For recurrent or unusually large hemorrhages, investigation into systemic conditions such as hypertension, bleeding disorders, or medication side effects (e.g., anticoagulants) is warranted.
  • Observation and Symptomatic Relief: Treatment typically involves observation. Artificial tears may be recommended for comfort.
  • Prognosis: The blood reabsorbs over 1-2 weeks, with the color gradually changing from bright red to orange/yellow before disappearing completely.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Patient Education: Educate patients on the benign nature of subconjunctival hemorrhage, its typical resolution time, and signs/symptoms that warrant further medical evaluation.
  • Assessment: Obtain a thorough patient history, including recent trauma, Valsalva maneuvers, medication use (especially anticoagulants), history of hypertension, diabetes, or bleeding disorders. Assess visual acuity and perform a focused eye examination.
  • Vital Signs Monitoring: Pay close attention to blood pressure readings, as uncontrolled hypertension can be a contributing factor.
  • Documentation: Accurately document the size, location, and color of the hemorrhage, as well as any associated symptoms or patient complaints.
  • Referral Guidelines: Understand when to refer to an ophthalmologist or primary care physician for further evaluation of underlying systemic causes or for persistent/worsening symptoms.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Pathophysiology: Rupture of small, fragile conjunctival blood vessels (capillaries or venules) located between the conjunctiva and the sclera.
  • Etiology: Common causes include Valsalva maneuvers (coughing, sneezing, straining), vomiting, eye rubbing, minor trauma, hypertension, bleeding disorders (e.g., thrombocytopenia, coagulopathies), and anticoagulant/antiplatelet medication use.
  • Differential Diagnosis: Must differentiate from more serious conditions such as acute conjunctivitis, episcleritis, scleritis, ocular trauma with globe rupture, foreign body, or acute angle-closure glaucoma.
  • Diagnostic Workup: Usually clinical diagnosis. For recurrent cases or suspicion of systemic disease, may include blood pressure measurement, complete blood count (CBC), prothrombin time (PT), international normalized ratio (INR), and activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT).
  • Management: Primarily supportive and observational. Artificial tears for symptomatic relief. Address and manage any identified underlying systemic conditions.
