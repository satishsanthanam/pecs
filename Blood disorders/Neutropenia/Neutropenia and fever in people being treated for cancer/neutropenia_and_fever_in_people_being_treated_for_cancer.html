Neutropenia and fever in people being treated for cancer

🏠 Home / Blood disorders / Neutropenia

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Understanding Neutropenia: Neutropenia is a condition where you have a critically low number of neutrophils, a type of white blood cell crucial for fighting infection. It's a common side effect of cancer treatments like chemotherapy.
  • Febrile Neutropenia (FN): This is a medical emergency defined as neutropenia combined with a fever. It indicates a severe infection risk due to a weakened immune system and requires immediate medical attention.
  • High Risk of Infection: With fewer neutrophils, your body cannot effectively fight off bacteria, viruses, or fungi, making even minor infections potentially life-threatening.
  • Prompt Action is Key: Early recognition of fever and immediate contact with your healthcare team can significantly improve outcomes and prevent serious complications.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Fever: An oral temperature of 100.4°F (38.0°C) or higher, or a single temperature of 101°F (38.3°C). This is the primary and most critical symptom.
  • Chills and Sweats: Often accompany fever, indicating your body's response to a potential infection.
  • Sore Throat or Mouth Sores: Can be signs of infection in the oral cavity or pharynx.
  • Cough or Shortness of Breath: May indicate a respiratory infection like pneumonia.
  • Painful Urination or Frequent Urination: Suggestive of a urinary tract infection.
  • Redness, Swelling, or Pus: At a catheter site, surgical wound, or any skin break, indicating localized infection.
  • New or Worsening Pain: Any new pain, especially in the abdomen or joints, could signal an underlying infection.
  • Unusual Fatigue or Weakness: Beyond typical treatment-related fatigue, which might signal a systemic infection.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Strict Hand Hygiene: Wash hands frequently and thoroughly with soap and water or use an alcohol-based hand sanitizer, especially before eating and after using the restroom. Ensure caregivers also practice this.
  • Avoid Crowds and Sick Individuals: Limit exposure to large gatherings and anyone showing signs of illness (colds, flu, etc.) to minimize infection risk.
  • Maintain Skin Integrity: Avoid cuts, scrapes, and nicks. Use an electric razor instead of a blade. Be gentle with skin and nails.
  • Oral Care: Use a soft toothbrush and rinse your mouth as directed by your care team to prevent oral infections.
  • Food Safety: Follow specific dietary guidelines to avoid foodborne illnesses (detailed in the dietary section).
  • Avoid Stagnant Water: Do not handle standing water in flower vases, humidifiers, or pet water bowls, as these can harbor bacteria.
  • Pet Care: Avoid cleaning pet litter boxes or handling animal waste. If unavoidable, wear gloves and wash hands immediately.
  • Report Symptoms Promptly: Immediately contact your oncology team if you develop a fever or any other signs of infection.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Thoroughly Cook All Foods: Ensure all meats, poultry, eggs, and seafood are cooked to their recommended internal temperatures to kill bacteria.
  • Wash Fruits and Vegetables: Thoroughly wash all fresh fruits and vegetables under running water. Peel fruits and vegetables when possible.
  • Avoid Raw or Undercooked Foods: This includes raw sprouts (alfalfa, bean sprouts), sushi, sashimi, raw eggs, and unpasteurized dairy products.
  • Safe Dairy Products: Consume only pasteurized milk, cheese, and yogurt. Avoid soft cheeses unless specified as pasteurized.
  • Deli Meats: Avoid deli meats unless they are reheated to steaming hot.
  • Safe Water Sources: Drink bottled water or filtered tap water. Avoid well water unless tested and deemed safe.
  • Avoid Cross-Contamination: Use separate cutting boards for raw meats and produce. Wash utensils and surfaces thoroughly after contact with raw foods.
  • Leftovers: Refrigerate leftovers promptly and consume within 24 hours. Reheat thoroughly.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Immediate Medical Attention: If you develop a fever (100.4°F/38.0°C or higher), contact your oncology team or go to the nearest emergency department immediately. Do not wait.
  • Do Not Self-Medicate Fever: Avoid taking acetaminophen (Tylenol), ibuprofen (Advil), or other fever-reducing medications before consulting your doctor, as they can mask a fever and delay diagnosis.
  • Know Your Nadir: Understand when your blood counts are expected to be at their lowest (nadir) after chemotherapy, as this is your highest risk period for febrile neutropenia.
  • Carry Emergency Information: Always have your cancer diagnosis, current treatment, oncology team's contact information, and a list of medications readily available.
  • Stay Hydrated: Maintain adequate fluid intake unless otherwise instructed by your physician.
  • Avoid Invasive Procedures: Discuss with your doctor before undergoing any non-essential invasive procedures (e.g., dental work, colonoscopy) during periods of neutropenia.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Prompt Empiric Antibiotics: Febrile neutropenia is a medical emergency requiring immediate administration of broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics, often within one hour of presentation, to cover common bacterial pathogens.
  • Risk Stratification: Patients are risk-stratified using tools like the MASCC (Multinational Association for Supportive Care in Cancer) score to determine the likelihood of serious complications and guide management (e.g., outpatient vs. inpatient).
  • Granulocyte Colony-Stimulating Factors (G-CSFs): Prophylactic G-CSFs may be considered for patients at high risk of developing febrile neutropenia to stimulate neutrophil production. They are generally not recommended for established FN unless specific indications exist.
  • Source Identification: While empiric antibiotics are started, efforts to identify the source of infection (e.g., blood cultures, imaging) are crucial to tailor therapy.
  • Consideration of Fungal/Viral Etiology: If fever persists despite appropriate antibacterial therapy, evaluation for fungal or viral infections and initiation of antifungal/antiviral agents may be necessary.
  • Patient Education: Emphasize the critical importance of patient and caregiver education regarding fever monitoring and the need for urgent medical evaluation.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Comprehensive Assessment: Perform a thorough head-to-toe assessment, including vital signs, focused assessment on potential infection sites (oral cavity, skin, IV sites, perineum, lungs, abdomen), and pain assessment.
  • Timely Antibiotic Administration: Ensure antibiotics are administered within the prescribed timeframe, often within 60 minutes of diagnosis of febrile neutropenia, following institutional protocols.
  • Blood Culture Collection: Obtain blood cultures (peripheral and from all lumens of central venous catheters) prior to antibiotic administration, adhering to sterile technique to prevent contamination.
  • Patient and Family Education: Educate patients and their families on signs and symptoms of infection, proper hand hygiene, safe food practices, and when to seek immediate medical attention.
  • Monitoring for Complications: Continuously monitor for signs of sepsis, septic shock, and adverse reactions to antibiotics.
  • Fluid Management: Administer intravenous fluids as prescribed to maintain hydration and support organ perfusion, especially in hypotensive patients.
  • Documentation: Meticulously document all assessments, interventions, medications, and patient responses.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Definition of Neutropenia: Absolute Neutrophil Count (ANC) < 500 cells/µL, or ANC < 1000 cells/µL with a predicted decline to < 500 cells/µL within 48 hours.
  • Definition of Fever: A single oral temperature ≥ 101°F (38.3°C) or a temperature ≥ 100.4°F (38.0°C) sustained for > 1 hour.
  • Common Pathogens: Gram-negative bacteria (e.g., *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*, *E. coli*, *Klebsiella* spp.), Gram-positive bacteria (e.g., Coagulase-negative Staphylococci, *Staphylococcus aureus*, *Streptococcus* spp.), and less commonly fungi (*Candida* spp., *Aspergillus* spp.) or viruses.
  • Diagnostic Workup: Complete Blood Count (CBC) with differential, comprehensive metabolic panel, blood cultures (at least two sets, one peripheral and one from each lumen of a CVC), urinalysis with culture, chest X-ray, and other site-specific cultures/imaging as indicated.
  • Empiric Antibiotic Regimens: Typically broad-spectrum monotherapy with an anti-pseudomonal beta-lactam (e.g., piperacillin-tazobactam, cefepime, meropenem). Vancomycin or other Gram-positive coverage may be added for specific indications (e.g., catheter-related infection, skin/soft tissue infection, hemodynamic instability).
  • MASCC Risk Index: A validated tool to stratify patients into low-risk (score ≥ 21) or high-risk (score < 21) categories for complications, guiding decisions on outpatient vs. inpatient management.
  • G-CSF Role: Primary prophylaxis with G-CSF is recommended for patients receiving chemotherapy regimens associated with a ≥ 20% incidence of FN. Not routinely used for treatment of established FN unless specific conditions apply.
