Factor V Leiden

🏠 Home / Blood disorders / Clotting disorders

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Genetic Mutation: Factor V Leiden is a common inherited disorder of blood clotting, caused by a mutation in the Factor V gene.
  • Increased Clot Risk: Individuals with this mutation have an increased tendency to form abnormal blood clots, primarily in the veins (venous thromboembolism or VTE).
  • Autosomal Dominant: It is inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern, meaning only one copy of the mutated gene is needed to increase risk.
  • APC Resistance: The mutation makes Factor V resistant to inactivation by activated protein C (APC), a natural anticoagulant, leading to prolonged clotting activity.
  • Clinical Manifestation: Many individuals with Factor V Leiden never develop clots, but the risk is significantly elevated, especially in combination with other risk factors.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Deep Vein Thrombosis (DVT): Symptoms often include swelling, pain, tenderness, warmth, and redness in the affected leg or arm.
  • Pulmonary Embolism (PE): Symptoms may include sudden shortness of breath, chest pain (worsened by deep breathing), rapid heart rate, lightheadedness, or coughing up blood.
  • Recurrent Miscarriages: In some women, Factor V Leiden has been associated with an increased risk of recurrent pregnancy loss due to placental clots.
  • Asymptomatic Presentation: Many individuals with Factor V Leiden are asymptomatic until a thrombotic event occurs.
  • Superficial Thrombophlebitis: Less commonly, it can be associated with clots in veins just under the skin, causing localized pain and inflammation.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Avoid Prolonged Immobility: During long flights, car rides, or periods of bed rest, perform leg exercises, walk frequently, and stay hydrated to prevent blood stasis.
  • Inform Healthcare Providers: Always disclose your Factor V Leiden diagnosis to all medical professionals, especially before surgeries, during pregnancy, or when considering hormonal therapies.
  • Hormonal Contraceptives/HRT: Discuss alternative contraception or hormone replacement therapy options with your physician, as estrogen-containing medications significantly increase clot risk.
  • Hydration: Maintain adequate fluid intake, as dehydration can increase blood viscosity and clot risk.
  • Post-Surgical Prophylaxis: Ensure appropriate thromboprophylaxis (e.g., low-molecular-weight heparin, compression stockings) is administered after surgery or during prolonged hospitalization.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Consistent Vitamin K Intake (if on Warfarin): If prescribed warfarin, maintain a consistent intake of vitamin K-rich foods (e.g., leafy green vegetables) rather than restricting them, to ensure stable INR levels.
  • Hydration: Emphasize consistent daily fluid intake to support optimal blood flow and reduce blood viscosity.
  • Alcohol Consumption: Limit or avoid excessive alcohol intake, as it can interact with anticoagulant medications and affect liver function, which is crucial for clotting factor production.
  • Balanced Nutrition: Focus on a generally healthy, balanced diet to support overall cardiovascular health and manage other potential risk factors for thrombosis.
  • Avoid Unproven Supplements: Consult with your physician before taking any dietary supplements, especially those marketed for 'blood thinning' or 'circulation,' as they may interact with prescribed medications.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Emergency Recognition: Seek immediate medical attention if you experience symptoms of DVT (e.g., sudden leg swelling, severe pain) or PE (e.g., sudden shortness of breath, chest pain).
  • Medication Adherence: If prescribed anticoagulants, strictly adhere to the dosage and schedule, and never stop medication without consulting your physician.
  • Bleeding Risk Awareness: Be aware of increased bleeding risk if on anticoagulants; report unusual bruising, prolonged bleeding, or blood in urine/stools to your doctor.
  • Genetic Counseling: Consider genetic counseling for family members, especially if they are planning pregnancies or have a history of unexplained clotting events.
  • Avoid Self-Treatment: Do not use over-the-counter pain relievers like NSAIDs (e.g., ibuprofen, naproxen) without medical advice, as they can increase bleeding risk when combined with anticoagulants.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Risk Assessment: A thorough assessment of individual thrombotic risk factors, including family history, is crucial for patients with Factor V Leiden.
  • Indications for Anticoagulation: Prophylactic anticoagulation is generally not recommended for asymptomatic carriers; treatment is typically initiated after a first thrombotic event.
  • Duration of Therapy: The duration of anticoagulant therapy post-thrombosis is individualized, considering the provoking factors and recurrence risk.
  • Pregnancy Management: Close monitoring and often prophylactic anticoagulation are recommended during pregnancy and the postpartum period for affected women.
  • Patient Education: Educating patients on symptom recognition, lifestyle modifications, and the importance of medication adherence is paramount for long-term management.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Patient Education on VTE: Nurses play a vital role in educating patients and families about the signs and symptoms of DVT and PE, and when to seek urgent care.
  • Anticoagulant Management: Understanding different anticoagulant medications, their administration, monitoring parameters (e.g., INR for warfarin), and potential side effects is essential.
  • Post-Operative Care: Implementing and reinforcing thromboprophylaxis protocols (e.g., early ambulation, compression devices) for surgical patients with Factor V Leiden.
  • Medication Reconciliation: Ensuring accurate medication reconciliation, especially regarding anticoagulants and medications that may interact with them.
  • Genetic Counseling Referral: Identifying patients who may benefit from genetic counseling and facilitating appropriate referrals.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Gene Mutation: G1691A point mutation in the F5 gene, leading to the substitution of arginine with glutamine at position 506 (R506Q).
  • Mechanism: Impaired cleavage of activated Factor V by activated protein C (APC), resulting in prolonged procoagulant activity.
  • Diagnostic Testing: Initial screening often involves an activated protein C resistance (APCR) assay; confirmation is by genetic testing for the F5 G1691A mutation.
  • Prevalence: Most common inherited thrombophilia, affecting 3-7% of individuals of European descent.
  • Clinical Significance: Homozygous individuals have a higher thrombotic risk than heterozygous carriers, but both are at increased risk compared to the general population.
