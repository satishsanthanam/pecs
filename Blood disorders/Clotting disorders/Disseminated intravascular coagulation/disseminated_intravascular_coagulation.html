Disseminated intravascular coagulation

🏠 Home / Blood disorders / Clotting disorders

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Life-Threatening Condition: Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) is a severe, systemic disorder characterized by widespread activation of the coagulation cascade.
  • Dual Pathology: It leads to simultaneous formation of microthrombi throughout the microvasculature and consumption of clotting factors and platelets, paradoxically resulting in severe bleeding.
  • Secondary Disorder: DIC is always a complication of an underlying severe illness, such as sepsis, severe trauma, malignancy, or obstetric emergencies.
  • High Mortality: The prognosis is often poor, with high mortality rates primarily due to multi-organ failure and uncontrolled hemorrhage.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Bleeding Manifestations: Petechiae, purpura, ecchymoses, epistaxis, gingival bleeding, hematuria, gastrointestinal bleeding, and intracranial hemorrhage.
  • Thrombotic Manifestations: Ischemic changes in digits (acrocyanosis), signs of organ dysfunction (e.g., acute kidney injury, acute respiratory distress syndrome, altered mental status) due to microvascular clots.
  • Signs of Underlying Cause: Symptoms related to the primary trigger, such as fever, hypotension, tachycardia (sepsis), or signs of trauma or advanced malignancy.
  • Neurological Changes: Confusion, delirium, or focal neurological deficits due to cerebral microthrombi or hemorrhage.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Early Recognition: Prompt identification of the underlying cause and subtle signs of DIC is paramount for timely intervention and improved outcomes.
  • Bleeding Risk Management: Meticulous care during all procedures; avoid intramuscular injections, use smallest gauge needles for venipuncture, and apply prolonged pressure to injection sites.
  • Thrombosis Monitoring: Regular assessment for signs of ischemia in extremities (e.g., color, temperature, capillary refill) and monitoring for organ dysfunction.
  • Medication Safety: Extreme caution with all medications, especially those affecting coagulation; careful titration of anticoagulants or procoagulants as indicated.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Oral Intake Restriction: Oral intake is often restricted or withheld due to critical illness, risk of gastrointestinal bleeding, or altered mental status.
  • Nutritional Support: Patients typically require parenteral or enteral nutrition to meet metabolic demands and support organ function during critical illness.
  • Intravenous Hydration: Aggressive intravenous fluid resuscitation is crucial to maintain hemodynamic stability and ensure adequate organ perfusion.
  • Electrolyte Management: Close monitoring and correction of electrolyte imbalances, which are common due to organ dysfunction and fluid shifts.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Continuous Monitoring: Implement continuous monitoring of vital signs, neurological status, urine output, and frequent assessment for new bleeding or thrombotic events.
  • Laboratory Surveillance: Perform frequent laboratory tests including complete blood count (with platelet count), coagulation panel (PT, aPTT, fibrinogen), D-dimer, and renal/hepatic function tests.
  • Blood Product Preparedness: Ensure immediate availability of blood products (platelets, fresh frozen plasma, cryoprecipitate) and adhere to rapid transfusion protocols.
  • Treat Underlying Cause: Aggressive and definitive treatment of the primary disorder (e.g., antibiotics for sepsis, surgical intervention for trauma) is the cornerstone of DIC management.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Diagnostic Acumen: DIC diagnosis is primarily clinical, supported by a constellation of laboratory abnormalities, requiring a high index of suspicion in critically ill patients.
  • Therapeutic Priority: The most effective treatment for DIC is to eliminate or manage the underlying cause; supportive care focuses on balancing bleeding and thrombotic risks.
  • Individualized Approach: Treatment strategies, including the use of blood products, anticoagulants, or procoagulants, must be highly individualized based on the predominant clinical manifestation and patient's overall status.
  • Multidisciplinary Collaboration: Optimal management often necessitates a multidisciplinary team approach involving hematologists, intensivists, surgeons, and other specialists.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Pathophysiology Comprehension: Understand the complex interplay of systemic inflammation, widespread coagulation, and subsequent consumption coagulopathy that defines DIC.
  • Assessment Proficiency: Develop keen assessment skills to identify subtle signs of bleeding (e.g., petechiae, oozing from sites) and thrombosis (e.g., changes in peripheral perfusion, altered mentation).
  • Medication Vigilance: Be acutely aware of the indications, contraindications, and potential adverse effects of blood products and medications used in DIC management.
  • Family Support: Provide empathetic and clear communication to families regarding the severity, unpredictable course, and complex management of DIC.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Diagnostic Criteria: International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis (ISTH) scoring system, utilizing platelet count, D-dimer, PT prolongation, and fibrinogen levels for overt DIC.
  • Key Biomarkers: Elevated D-dimer, decreased fibrinogen, prolonged prothrombin time (PT) and activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT), thrombocytopenia, and presence of schistocytes on peripheral blood smear.
  • Therapeutic Modalities: Transfusion of fresh frozen plasma (FFP), cryoprecipitate, platelet concentrates; heparin (in specific cases of predominant thrombosis); recombinant activated protein C (limited use).
  • Major Complications: Acute multi-organ failure, severe hemorrhage, limb ischemia, stroke, and high risk of mortality.
