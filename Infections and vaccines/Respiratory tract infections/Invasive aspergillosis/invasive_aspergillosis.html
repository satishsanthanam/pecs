Invasive aspergillosis

🏠 Home / Infections and vaccines / Respiratory tract infections

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Definition: Invasive aspergillosis is a severe, life-threatening fungal infection caused by *Aspergillus* species, primarily affecting immunocompromised individuals.
  • Primary Risk Factors: Patients with prolonged neutropenia, hematologic malignancies, hematopoietic stem cell or solid organ transplants, prolonged corticosteroid use, or advanced AIDS are at highest risk.
  • Transmission: Infection occurs through inhalation of airborne *Aspergillus* spores, which are ubiquitous in the environment; it is not transmitted person-to-person.
  • Common Sites: The lungs are the most common site of infection, but it can disseminate to other organs including the brain, skin, and kidneys.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Persistent Fever: Unresponsive to broad-spectrum antibiotics, often the earliest and most common symptom in immunocompromised patients.
  • Respiratory Symptoms: Cough (which may be productive of blood-tinged sputum), dyspnea (shortness of breath), pleuritic chest pain, and hemoptysis.
  • Systemic Manifestations: Fatigue, weight loss, and general malaise can occur.
  • Disseminated Disease: Symptoms vary based on the affected organ, e.g., neurological deficits (seizures, altered mental status) with CNS involvement, or skin lesions.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Environmental Control: Implement strict measures in healthcare settings for high-risk patients, including HEPA filtration, positive pressure rooms, and avoiding construction areas.
  • Prophylactic Antifungals: Consider antifungal prophylaxis in highly immunocompromised patients as per clinical guidelines to prevent infection.
  • Early Recognition: Maintain a high index of suspicion for invasive aspergillosis in at-risk patients presenting with new or worsening respiratory symptoms or unexplained fever.
  • Avoidance of Exposure: Advise immunocompromised patients to avoid activities that may expose them to high concentrations of mold spores (e.g., gardening, composting, visiting dusty areas).

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • No Direct Dietary Restrictions: Invasive aspergillosis itself does not impose specific dietary restrictions.
  • Immunocompromised Diet: For patients with severe immunosuppression, adherence to a neutropenic or low-microbial diet may be recommended to prevent other opportunistic infections. This includes avoiding raw fruits/vegetables, unpasteurized dairy, and undercooked meats.
  • Hydration: Ensure adequate fluid intake, especially if the patient has fever or increased respiratory effort, to support overall health and mucociliary clearance.
  • Medication Administration: Follow specific instructions for antifungal medications regarding food intake (e.g., some antifungals require administration with a high-fat meal for optimal absorption, while others are taken on an empty stomach).

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Prompt Diagnosis and Treatment: Delay in initiating appropriate antifungal therapy significantly increases mortality rates; rapid diagnostic workup is critical.
  • Antifungal Drug Interactions: Be vigilant for potential drug-drug interactions, especially with azole antifungals, which are potent inhibitors or inducers of cytochrome P450 enzymes.
  • Monitoring for Toxicity: Closely monitor patients for adverse effects of antifungal medications, including liver function, renal function, and electrolyte imbalances.
  • Patient Education: Educate high-risk patients and their caregivers on the signs and symptoms of infection and the importance of immediate medical attention if they occur.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Diagnostic Challenges: Diagnosis can be complex, often requiring a combination of clinical suspicion, radiological findings (e.g., halo sign, air crescent sign on CT), and mycological evidence (e.g., galactomannan antigen, PCR, culture from sterile sites).
  • First-Line Therapy: Voriconazole is typically the first-line agent for invasive aspergillosis, with alternatives including isavuconazole, posaconazole, and lipid formulations of amphotericin B.
  • Multidisciplinary Management: Optimal care often involves a team approach including infectious disease specialists, pulmonologists, oncologists, and critical care physicians.
  • Prognosis: Prognosis is highly dependent on the patient's underlying immune status, extent of disease, and the timeliness and effectiveness of antifungal treatment.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Comprehensive Assessment: Regularly assess respiratory status, vital signs, oxygen saturation, and signs of infection progression or dissemination.
  • Medication Management: Administer prescribed antifungal agents, monitor for side effects (e.g., visual disturbances with voriconazole, nephrotoxicity with amphotericin B), and ensure correct dosing and administration.
  • Infection Control: Implement strict infection control measures, including hand hygiene, appropriate personal protective equipment, and environmental precautions for immunocompromised patients.
  • Supportive Care: Provide respiratory support (e.g., oxygen therapy), pain management, and nutritional support as needed to optimize patient outcomes.
  • Patient and Family Education: Educate patients and families on the disease, treatment plan, potential side effects, and the importance of reporting any new or worsening symptoms.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Etiology: Primarily *Aspergillus fumigatus*, but also *A. flavus*, *A. niger*, *A. terreus*, and others.
  • Pathogenesis: Inhalation of conidia, germination in the lung, angioinvasion, tissue infarction, and potential hematogenous dissemination.
  • Diagnostic Markers: Galactomannan antigen (serum, bronchoalveolar lavage), beta-D-glucan, PCR, high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) of the chest.
  • Treatment Modalities: Systemic antifungal therapy (voriconazole, isavuconazole, posaconazole, lipid amphotericin B), surgical debridement in selected cases (e.g., localized disease, aspergilloma).
  • Histopathology: Characterized by septate hyphae with acute angle branching (typically 45 degrees) invading blood vessels and causing tissue necrosis.
