Pneumonia in children

🏠 Home / Infections and vaccines / Respiratory tract infections

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Definition: Pneumonia is an infection that inflames the air sacs in one or both lungs, which may fill with fluid or pus.
  • Common Causes: In children, pneumonia is frequently caused by viruses (e.g., RSV, influenza) or bacteria (e.g., Streptococcus pneumoniae, Mycoplasma pneumoniae).
  • Vulnerability: Infants, young children, and those with weakened immune systems or underlying chronic conditions are at higher risk.
  • Severity: The condition can range from mild to severe, potentially requiring hospitalization, and necessitates prompt medical evaluation.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Respiratory Distress: Rapid breathing (tachypnea), difficulty breathing (dyspnea), nasal flaring, grunting sounds, and chest retractions (sucking in of skin between ribs or under the breastbone).
  • Persistent Cough: A cough that may be dry or productive, sometimes producing mucus or phlegm, and can worsen over time.
  • Fever and Chills: High fever, often accompanied by chills, indicating a systemic response to infection.
  • General Malaise: Lethargy, irritability, decreased appetite, poor feeding in infants, and reduced activity levels.
  • Other Signs: Chest pain (especially with deep breaths), wheezing, or crackling sounds heard during breathing.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Vaccination: Ensure children receive all recommended immunizations, including the pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV), Haemophilus influenzae type b (Hib) vaccine, and annual influenza vaccine.
  • Hand Hygiene: Practice frequent and thorough hand washing for both children and caregivers to prevent the spread of respiratory pathogens.
  • Avoid Exposure: Minimize contact with individuals who are sick, especially during peak respiratory virus seasons.
  • Environmental Factors: Protect children from exposure to secondhand smoke, which significantly increases the risk and severity of respiratory infections.
  • Early Recognition: Seek immediate medical attention if a child develops symptoms suggestive of pneumonia or worsening respiratory distress.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Hydration: Encourage frequent sips of clear fluids (water, oral rehydration solutions, clear broths) to prevent dehydration and help thin respiratory secretions.
  • Small, Frequent Meals: Offer small, easily digestible, and nutrient-dense meals to maintain caloric intake, as appetite may be reduced during illness.
  • Avoid Irritants: Limit foods and beverages that may irritate the throat or worsen coughing, such as very cold drinks or highly acidic juices.
  • Breastfeeding: Continue breastfeeding for infants, as breast milk provides essential antibodies and nutrients that support recovery.
  • Soft Foods: Focus on soft, bland foods like pureed fruits, mashed vegetables, and cooked cereals when the child's appetite begins to return.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Monitor Breathing: Continuously observe for any signs of worsening respiratory distress, such as increased breathing effort, cyanosis (bluish discoloration of lips or skin), or extreme lethargy.
  • Medication Adherence: Administer all prescribed medications, especially antibiotics for bacterial pneumonia, exactly as directed and complete the full course, even if symptoms improve.
  • Fever Management: Use antipyretics (e.g., acetaminophen, ibuprofen) as advised by a physician to manage fever and discomfort, ensuring correct pediatric dosing.
  • Adequate Rest: Ensure the child receives ample rest to conserve energy and support the body's healing process.
  • Follow-up Care: Attend all scheduled follow-up appointments with the healthcare provider to ensure complete resolution of the infection and monitor for any complications.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Early Diagnosis: Prompt clinical assessment, often supported by a chest X-ray and sometimes laboratory tests, is crucial for accurate diagnosis and guiding appropriate treatment.
  • Treatment Strategy: Bacterial pneumonia typically requires antibiotic therapy, while viral pneumonia is managed with supportive care. Differentiating between the two is key to effective treatment.
  • Oxygen Support: Severe cases may necessitate supplemental oxygen, intravenous fluids, or, in rare instances, mechanical ventilation to support respiratory function.
  • Complication Vigilance: Physicians must be vigilant for potential complications such as pleural effusion, empyema, lung abscess, or sepsis, which require urgent intervention.
  • Parental Education: Educating parents on warning signs, home care strategies, and when to seek urgent medical re-evaluation is paramount for optimal outcomes.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Respiratory Assessment: Develop strong skills in pediatric respiratory assessment, including accurate counting of respiratory rate, auscultation for adventitious breath sounds, and recognition of subtle signs of distress.
  • Oxygen Therapy: Understand the principles of oxygen delivery in children, including appropriate flow rates, device selection (e.g., nasal cannula, oxygen hood), and monitoring for effectiveness and adverse effects.
  • Fluid Balance: Meticulously monitor fluid intake and output, hydration status, and electrolyte balance, especially in febrile children with increased insensible fluid losses.
  • Medication Administration: Safely administer pediatric dosages of antibiotics, antipyretics, and bronchodilators, ensuring correct routes, timing, and patient identification.
  • Family-Centered Care: Provide comprehensive education and emotional support to families, empowering them with knowledge about the child's condition, treatment plan, and home care instructions.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Etiology: Common bacterial pathogens include *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, *Haemophilus influenzae* type b, *Staphylococcus aureus*, and *Mycoplasma pneumoniae*. Viral causes include Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV), influenza, parainfluenza, and adenovirus.
  • Diagnosis: Clinical presentation, chest X-ray (showing lobar consolidation, interstitial infiltrates, or pleural effusions), complete blood count (leukocytosis or leukopenia), blood cultures, and viral PCR panels.
  • Treatment: Empiric antibiotics (e.g., amoxicillin, azithromycin) for suspected bacterial pneumonia, antiviral agents (e.g., oseltamivir for influenza), and supportive care including oxygen, intravenous fluids, and antipyretics.
  • Complications: Pleural effusion, empyema, pneumothorax, necrotizing pneumonia, lung abscess, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), and sepsis.
  • Prevention: Routine childhood immunizations (PCV13, Hib, DTaP), annual influenza vaccination, and strict infection control measures.
