Bronchiolitis and RSV in babies and children

🏠 Home / Infections and vaccines / Respiratory tract infections

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Common Viral Infection: Bronchiolitis is a common lower respiratory tract infection, primarily affecting infants and young children, typically under two years of age.
  • RSV as Primary Cause: Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV) is the most frequent cause of bronchiolitis, responsible for the majority of cases.
  • Highly Contagious: The virus spreads easily through respiratory droplets from coughs and sneezes, and direct contact with contaminated surfaces.
  • Seasonal Peak: Bronchiolitis and RSV infections are most prevalent during the colder months, typically from late fall through early spring.
  • Impact on Infants: Can lead to significant respiratory distress, especially in infants younger than 6 months, due to their smaller airways.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Initial Cold-like Symptoms: Often begins with mild upper respiratory symptoms such as a runny nose, nasal congestion, and a low-grade fever.
  • Persistent Cough: A characteristic cough that may worsen and become more frequent.
  • Wheezing and Rapid Breathing: Development of a high-pitched whistling sound during breathing (wheezing) and an increased respiratory rate (tachypnea).
  • Difficulty Breathing: Visible signs of increased work of breathing, including nasal flaring, chest wall retractions (sucking in of skin between ribs or at the neck), and grunting.
  • Poor Feeding and Irritability: Infants may show decreased interest in feeding, become irritable, or appear unusually tired and lethargic.
  • Apnea in Young Infants: Brief pauses in breathing (apnea) can occur, particularly in premature infants or those under 2 months of age.
  • Cyanosis (Severe Cases): Bluish discoloration of the lips or skin, indicating severe oxygen deprivation, requires immediate medical attention.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Strict Hand Hygiene: Emphasize frequent and thorough hand washing with soap and water for at least 20 seconds, especially before touching infants.
  • Limit Exposure to Sick Individuals: Avoid close contact between infants and anyone exhibiting cold or flu-like symptoms, particularly during peak RSV season.
  • Environmental Disinfection: Regularly clean and disinfect frequently touched surfaces and objects in the home, such as toys and doorknobs.
  • Avoid Tobacco Smoke: Protect infants from exposure to tobacco smoke, as it can worsen respiratory symptoms and increase the risk of severe illness.
  • Vaccination for Household Members: Ensure all eligible household members receive annual influenza and COVID-19 vaccinations to reduce the risk of co-infections.
  • Palivizumab Prophylaxis: For high-risk infants (e.g., premature babies, those with chronic lung disease or congenital heart disease), discuss palivizumab (Synagis) injections with a physician for RSV prevention.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Prioritize Hydration: Offer frequent, small amounts of fluids (breast milk, formula, or oral rehydration solution) to prevent dehydration, especially during fever or increased respiratory effort.
  • Monitor Fluid Intake: Observe for signs of dehydration such as decreased wet diapers, lack of tears, or a sunken fontanelle in infants.
  • Avoid Force-Feeding: Do not attempt to force feeds if the child is experiencing significant respiratory distress, as this can increase the risk of aspiration.
  • Small, Frequent Feeds: If the child is able to feed, offer smaller volumes more frequently to conserve energy and reduce the risk of vomiting due to coughing.
  • Nasal Clearance Before Feeds: Clear nasal passages with saline drops and a bulb syringe before feeding to facilitate easier breathing and sucking.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Monitor for Worsening Symptoms: Continuously observe for signs of increasing respiratory distress, such as faster breathing, deeper retractions, or grunting.
  • Seek Emergency Care: Immediately transport to an emergency department if the child's lips or skin turn blue, breathing becomes extremely labored, or they become unresponsive or unusually lethargic.
  • Nasal Suctioning: Perform gentle nasal suctioning with saline drops and a bulb syringe or nasal aspirator, especially before feeds and sleep, to clear mucus.
  • Maintain Airway Patency: Position the infant with the head slightly elevated, if safe and age-appropriate, to help ease breathing.
  • Avoid Over-the-Counter Medications: Do not administer cough and cold medications to infants and young children, especially those under 2 years, as they are ineffective and can be harmful.
  • Humidification: Use a cool-mist humidifier in the child's room to help loosen secretions and soothe irritated airways.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Supportive Care is Key: Management of bronchiolitis is primarily supportive, focusing on maintaining adequate oxygenation, hydration, and airway clearance.
  • Hospitalization Criteria: Consider hospitalization for infants with significant respiratory distress, hypoxemia (low blood oxygen), dehydration, or a history of apnea.
  • Limited Role for Medications: Bronchodilators, corticosteroids, and antibiotics are generally not recommended for routine bronchiolitis unless there are specific indications (e.g., co-existing asthma, bacterial co-infection).
  • Antivirals Rarely Used: Ribavirin is reserved for severe cases in immunocompromised patients and is not routinely used for healthy infants with RSV.
  • Parental Education: Emphasize educating parents on recognizing warning signs, proper home care techniques, and when to seek urgent medical attention.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Comprehensive Respiratory Assessment: Conduct thorough assessments of respiratory rate, effort, oxygen saturation, presence of adventitious sounds (wheezing, crackles), and signs of distress.
  • Hydration Monitoring: Closely monitor fluid intake and output, assess skin turgor, mucous membranes, and fontanelles for signs of dehydration.
  • Airway Management: Implement interventions such as nasal suctioning, oxygen administration as prescribed, and positioning to optimize airway patency.
  • Isolation Precautions: Apply droplet and contact precautions in hospital settings to prevent nosocomial spread of RSV.
  • Family Education and Support: Provide clear instructions to parents on home care, medication administration (if applicable), symptom monitoring, and when to return for follow-up or emergency care.
  • Developmental Considerations: Understand that infants' smaller, more compliant airways and immature immune systems make them highly vulnerable to severe respiratory compromise from bronchiolitis.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Etiology: Predominantly Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV), but also human metapneumovirus, parainfluenza virus, adenovirus, and rhinovirus.
  • Pathophysiology: Viral infection leads to inflammation, edema, and necrosis of bronchiolar epithelial cells, resulting in mucus production, cellular debris, and subsequent small airway obstruction.
  • Diagnosis: Primarily a clinical diagnosis based on age, seasonal presentation, and characteristic signs and symptoms. Nasopharyngeal swab for viral antigen detection or PCR can confirm the specific viral agent.
  • Risk Factors for Severe Disease: Prematurity (especially <32 weeks gestation), chronic lung disease (e.g., bronchopulmonary dysplasia), congenital heart disease, immunodeficiency, neuromuscular disorders, and age less than 3 months.
  • Complications: Apnea, respiratory failure requiring mechanical ventilation, secondary bacterial infections (e.g., otitis media, pneumonia), and potential association with recurrent wheezing or asthma later in childhood.
