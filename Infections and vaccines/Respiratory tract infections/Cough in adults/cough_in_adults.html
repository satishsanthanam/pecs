Cough in adults

🏠 Home / Infections and vaccines / Respiratory tract infections

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Cough Reflex: A natural protective mechanism to clear irritants, mucus, or foreign bodies from the respiratory tract.
  • Acute vs. Chronic: Acute cough lasts less than 3 weeks, often due to viral infections. Chronic cough persists for more than 8 weeks, requiring thorough investigation.
  • Productive vs. Non-productive: Productive cough brings up sputum or phlegm, while non-productive (dry) cough does not.
  • Common Causes: Viral upper respiratory infections (common cold, flu), post-nasal drip, asthma, gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), and chronic bronchitis are frequent culprits.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Sore Throat: Often accompanies viral infections causing cough.
  • Runny or Stuffy Nose: Common with colds, allergies, or post-nasal drip.
  • Fever and Chills: Suggestive of an underlying infection, such as influenza or pneumonia.
  • Chest Discomfort: Can range from mild irritation to sharp pain, especially with forceful coughing.
  • Shortness of Breath or Wheezing: May indicate asthma, COPD exacerbation, or other lower respiratory conditions.
  • Fatigue and Malaise: General symptoms often present with acute infections.
  • Hoarseness: Can occur due to vocal cord irritation from persistent coughing or laryngitis.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Infection Control: Practice meticulous hand hygiene and cover coughs and sneezes with a tissue or your elbow to prevent transmission.
  • Avoid Irritants: Minimize exposure to smoke, strong chemical fumes, and known allergens that can trigger or worsen a cough.
  • Hydration: Maintain adequate fluid intake to help thin mucus and soothe the throat.
  • Medication Adherence: If prescribed, take all medications as directed, especially for underlying conditions like asthma or GERD.
  • Monitor Symptoms: Watch for worsening symptoms such as high fever, difficulty breathing, chest pain, or coughing up blood, and seek immediate medical attention if they occur.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Warm Fluids: Consume warm liquids like herbal tea with honey, clear broths, or warm water to soothe the throat and help loosen mucus.
  • Avoid Dehydrating Beverages: Limit caffeine and alcohol intake, as they can contribute to dehydration and potentially irritate the throat.
  • Soft, Bland Foods: Opt for easily digestible foods that won't irritate a sore throat or trigger reflux, such as oatmeal, mashed potatoes, or bananas.
  • Honey: For adults, a teaspoon of honey can help soothe a cough, but it should not be given to infants under one year old.
  • Avoid Irritating Foods: Steer clear of very spicy, acidic, or excessively cold foods and drinks that might exacerbate throat irritation or trigger coughing.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Persistent Cough: Any cough lasting longer than 3 weeks warrants medical evaluation to rule out serious underlying conditions.
  • Red Flag Symptoms: Seek urgent medical attention for cough accompanied by high fever, severe shortness of breath, chest pain, coughing up blood (hemoptysis), or unexplained weight loss.
  • Medication Caution: Over-the-counter cough and cold medications should be used with caution, especially in individuals with pre-existing conditions or those taking other medications, due to potential interactions or side effects.
  • Smoking Cessation: For smokers, cessation is paramount as smoking is a major cause of chronic cough and exacerbates respiratory conditions.
  • Environmental Factors: Be aware of environmental triggers such as mold, dust mites, or occupational irritants that may contribute to chronic cough.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Thorough Assessment: A detailed history, including cough characteristics, associated symptoms, and exposure history, is crucial for accurate diagnosis.
  • Differential Diagnosis: Consider a broad range of etiologies including viral infections, bacterial pneumonia, asthma, COPD, GERD, post-nasal drip syndrome, ACE inhibitor-induced cough, and less commonly, lung malignancy or heart failure.
  • Targeted Treatment: Management should be directed at the underlying cause, which may involve antibiotics for bacterial infections, bronchodilators for asthma, proton pump inhibitors for GERD, or antihistamines for allergies.
  • Patient Education: Emphasize the importance of completing prescribed medication courses, avoiding triggers, and recognizing warning signs that necessitate further medical review.
  • Vaccination Status: Advise on appropriate vaccinations, such as influenza and pneumococcal vaccines, to prevent common infectious causes of cough.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Cough Assessment: Systematically assess cough frequency, intensity, duration, quality (barking, hacking, wheezing), and presence/character of sputum (color, consistency, odor).
  • Respiratory Monitoring: Closely monitor respiratory rate, effort, oxygen saturation, and lung sounds for adventitious sounds like crackles or wheezes.
  • Patient Education: Educate patients on proper cough etiquette, hydration, humidification, and the correct use of inhalers or nebulizers if prescribed.
  • Comfort Measures: Implement interventions such as elevating the head of the bed, providing warm compresses, or offering throat lozenges to enhance patient comfort.
  • Medication Administration: Administer prescribed antitussives, expectorants, bronchodilators, or antibiotics, and monitor for therapeutic effects and adverse reactions.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Cough Reflex Arc: Involves afferent pathways (vagus, glossopharyngeal, trigeminal nerves) sensing irritants, a central processing unit in the medulla, and efferent pathways (vagus, phrenic, spinal nerves) leading to glottic closure, forced expiration, and glottic opening.
  • Diagnostic Modalities: Chest X-ray, spirometry, allergy testing, pH monitoring (for GERD), high-resolution CT scan, and bronchoscopy may be utilized to identify the etiology of chronic cough.
  • Pharmacological Interventions: Includes antitussives (e.g., dextromethorphan, codeine), expectorants (e.g., guaifenesin), bronchodilators (e.g., albuterol), inhaled corticosteroids, and antihistamines/decongestants.
  • Common Chronic Cough Etiologies (PNDS, Asthma, GERD): Post-nasal drip syndrome (PNDS), asthma, and gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) collectively account for over 90% of chronic cough cases in non-smoking, immunocompetent adults not taking ACE inhibitors.
  • ACE Inhibitor Cough: A common side effect of angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, typically dry and non-productive, resolving within 1-4 weeks after discontinuation.
