Erythema infectiosum (fifth disease)

🏠 Home / Infections and vaccines / Viral infections

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Viral Origin: Erythema infectiosum, commonly known as Fifth Disease, is caused by Parvovirus B19.
  • Primary Affects Children: It is a common, mild childhood illness, though adults can also contract it.
  • Distinctive Rash: Characterized by a bright red 'slapped cheek' rash on the face, followed by a lacy, reticulated rash on the body and limbs.
  • Transmission Route: Spreads through respiratory secretions (e.g., coughing, sneezing) from person to person.
  • Contagious Period: Individuals are most contagious *before* the rash appears. Once the rash develops, they are generally no longer infectious.
  • Incubation Time: Symptoms typically appear 4 to 21 days after exposure to the virus.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Prodromal Phase: Mild, non-specific symptoms may precede the rash, including low-grade fever, headache, runny nose, and sore throat.
  • Facial Rash: A characteristic bright red rash on both cheeks, giving a 'slapped cheek' appearance, often sparing the area around the mouth.
  • Body Rash: Following the facial rash, a lacy, net-like (reticulated) rash appears on the trunk, arms, and legs, which can wax and wane, often worsening with heat or sun exposure.
  • Joint Pain (Arthralgia): More common in adults, particularly women, affecting joints such as hands, wrists, knees, and ankles.
  • Pruritus: Itching may accompany the body rash.
  • Fatigue: General tiredness or malaise can be present, especially during the prodromal phase.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Hand Hygiene: Emphasize frequent and thorough hand washing, especially in childcare settings and schools, to prevent viral spread.
  • Avoid Contact with High-Risk Groups: Individuals, especially those with symptoms, should avoid close contact with pregnant women and immunocompromised individuals.
  • Pregnant Women Alert: Exposure during pregnancy can lead to serious fetal complications, including hydrops fetalis, severe anemia, or miscarriage. Immediate medical consultation is vital if a pregnant woman is exposed or develops symptoms.
  • Immunocompromised Individuals: Patients with weakened immune systems are at risk for persistent infection and chronic anemia; close monitoring is required.
  • Individuals with Hemolytic Anemia: Those with conditions like sickle cell disease or thalassemia are at risk for transient aplastic crisis if infected.
  • No Vaccine Available: Currently, there is no vaccine to prevent Parvovirus B19 infection.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Maintain Hydration: Encourage ample fluid intake, such as water, clear broths, and electrolyte solutions, especially if fever is present.
  • Soft, Bland Foods: Offer easily digestible foods like toast, rice, applesauce, and bananas if the individual experiences a sore throat or general malaise.
  • Nutrient-Rich Diet: Support the immune system with a balanced intake of fruits, vegetables, and lean proteins as tolerated.
  • Avoid Irritants: Limit acidic, spicy, or very hot foods if oral discomfort or a sore throat is present.
  • Small, Frequent Meals: If appetite is reduced, offer smaller, more frequent meals to ensure adequate caloric and nutrient intake.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Monitor for Complications: Observe for signs of severe anemia (e.g., extreme pallor, unusual fatigue, shortness of breath) in at-risk individuals.
  • Pregnant Patient Exposure Protocol: Any pregnant woman exposed to Parvovirus B19 should immediately contact her obstetrician for serologic testing and fetal monitoring.
  • Immunocompromised Patient Management: Be vigilant for prolonged or severe symptoms in immunocompromised patients, as they may require specific interventions like intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG).
  • Symptom Relief: Advise on appropriate over-the-counter medications for fever, pain, and itching, such as acetaminophen or ibuprofen, as directed by a healthcare provider.
  • Rash Characteristics: Inform patients that the lacy rash can reappear or intensify with changes in temperature, sun exposure, or emotional stress.
  • School/Work Exclusion: Generally, exclusion from school or work is not necessary once the rash appears, as the individual is typically no longer contagious.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Clinical Diagnosis: Diagnosis is primarily clinical, based on the characteristic 'slapped cheek' and lacy body rash. Serologic testing (IgM for acute infection, IgG for past immunity) can confirm diagnosis, especially in atypical cases or for at-risk populations.
  • Supportive Care: Treatment is symptomatic, focusing on managing fever, pain, and itching. There is no specific antiviral therapy for Parvovirus B19.
  • Risk Stratification: Crucial to identify and counsel high-risk individuals, particularly pregnant women, those with chronic hemolytic anemias, and immunocompromised patients, regarding potential complications.
  • Fetal Monitoring: For pregnant women with confirmed infection, close fetal monitoring (e.g., ultrasound for hydrops) is essential.
  • IVIG Consideration: Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) may be considered for immunocompromised patients with chronic Parvovirus B19 infection leading to persistent anemia.
  • Differential Diagnosis: Consider other viral exanthems (e.g., rubella, measles, enterovirus) and drug eruptions in the differential diagnosis.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Patient Education: Educate patients and caregivers on the benign, self-limiting nature of Fifth Disease in most healthy children and the importance of symptomatic relief.
  • Infection Control: Reinforce standard precautions, including meticulous hand hygiene. For hospitalized patients during the prodromal phase, droplet precautions may be indicated.
  • Risk Assessment & Counseling: Identify and provide targeted education to high-risk groups, such as pregnant women, regarding the risks and necessary follow-up.
  • Symptom Management: Administer and educate on appropriate antipyretics, analgesics, and antipruritics to enhance patient comfort.
  • Documentation: Accurately document the onset, characteristics, and progression of the rash, associated symptoms, and all patient education provided.
  • Psychosocial Support: Offer reassurance to parents, as the rash can be alarming, explaining its typical course and lack of contagiousness once present.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Etiologic Agent: Parvovirus B19, a small, non-enveloped, single-stranded DNA virus.
  • Target Cells: Primarily infects and lyses erythroid progenitor cells in the bone marrow, leading to a temporary halt in red blood cell production.
  • Complications: Transient aplastic crisis (TAC) in patients with underlying hemolytic anemias, hydrops fetalis and fetal loss in pregnant women, chronic anemia in immunocompromised individuals, and arthropathy (joint pain).
  • Diagnostic Tests: Serology (IgM antibodies for acute infection, IgG for past immunity), PCR for viral DNA (useful in immunocompromised patients or for fetal diagnosis).
  • Differential Diagnosis: Rubella, measles, roseola, enterovirus infections, scarlet fever, drug eruptions, systemic lupus erythematosus.
  • Pathophysiology of Rash: The rash is believed to be an immune-mediated phenomenon, occurring as the body mounts an immune response to the virus.
