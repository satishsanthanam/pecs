Mononucleosis

🏠 Home / Infections and vaccines / Viral infections

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Causative Agent: Mononucleosis, often called 'mono' or 'the kissing disease', is primarily caused by the Epstein-Barr Virus (EBV), a member of the herpes virus family.
  • Transmission: The virus is predominantly spread through saliva, via close contact such as kissing, sharing drinks, or using contaminated utensils.
  • Incubation Period: Symptoms typically appear 4 to 6 weeks after exposure to the virus, though this can vary.
  • Affected Population: While anyone can contract EBV, symptomatic mononucleosis is most common in adolescents and young adults.
  • Self-Limiting Nature: Mono is generally a self-limiting illness, meaning it resolves on its own, but symptoms can be debilitating and prolonged.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Profound Fatigue: Extreme and persistent tiredness that can last for weeks or even months.
  • Fever: Often a high fever, which may fluctuate throughout the day.
  • Sore Throat: Severe pharyngitis, sometimes with exudates (pus) on the tonsils, mimicking strep throat.
  • Swollen Lymph Nodes: Enlarged and tender lymph nodes, particularly in the neck, but also in the armpits and groin.
  • Splenomegaly: Enlargement of the spleen, which can be a serious complication if ruptured.
  • Hepatomegaly: Less commonly, the liver may also become enlarged.
  • Headache and Body Aches: Generalized discomfort and pain.
  • Skin Rash: A maculopapular rash may occur, especially if ampicillin or amoxicillin is administered during the illness.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Avoid Sharing: Refrain from sharing drinks, food, eating utensils, or personal items to prevent viral transmission.
  • Rest and Hydration: Prioritize ample rest to aid recovery and maintain good hydration, especially with fever and sore throat.
  • Contact Sports Restriction: Strictly avoid contact sports, heavy lifting, or strenuous activities for at least 3-4 weeks, or until cleared by a physician, due to the risk of splenic rupture.
  • Hand Hygiene: Practice frequent and thorough hand washing with soap and water, particularly after coughing or sneezing.
  • Medication Caution: Avoid aspirin in children and adolescents due to the risk of Reye's syndrome; use acetaminophen or ibuprofen for fever and pain.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Hydration Focus: Maintain a high fluid intake with water, clear broths, and electrolyte-rich beverages to prevent dehydration, especially with fever.
  • Soft, Bland Foods: Opt for soft, easy-to-swallow foods like soups, mashed potatoes, yogurt, and smoothies to soothe a sore throat.
  • Avoid Irritants: Steer clear of acidic, spicy, or rough-textured foods that can irritate a sore throat.
  • Alcohol Restriction: Absolutely avoid alcohol, particularly if there is any indication of liver involvement (hepatomegaly or elevated liver enzymes), as it can exacerbate liver stress.
  • Nutrient-Dense Choices: Focus on nutrient-dense foods to support the immune system during recovery.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Monitor for Splenic Rupture: Educate patients on symptoms of splenic rupture, such as sudden, severe left upper abdominal pain, left shoulder pain, or dizziness, and advise immediate medical attention.
  • Respiratory Distress: Watch for signs of airway obstruction due to severe tonsillar enlargement, including difficulty breathing or noisy breathing, which requires urgent medical intervention.
  • Prolonged Fatigue: Acknowledge that fatigue can be debilitating and long-lasting; provide reassurance and strategies for managing energy levels.
  • Rash with Antibiotics: Inform patients that a widespread rash is common if ampicillin or amoxicillin is mistakenly prescribed for mono, and it is not a true penicillin allergy.
  • Avoid Steroids: Generally, corticosteroids are not recommended unless there is severe airway obstruction or other life-threatening complications.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Diagnostic Confirmation: Diagnosis is typically based on clinical symptoms, physical examination (e.g., palpable splenomegaly, lymphadenopathy), and confirmed by blood tests such as a Monospot test or EBV-specific serology.
  • Symptomatic Management: Treatment is primarily supportive, focusing on symptom relief with rest, hydration, and analgesics/antipyretics.
  • No Antiviral Cure: There is no specific antiviral treatment for EBV, as the illness is self-limiting. Antivirals are generally not effective or recommended for uncomplicated mono.
  • Follow-up for Complications: Advise regular follow-up, especially to monitor for splenic enlargement and ensure safe return to physical activities.
  • Patient Education: Emphasize the importance of patient education regarding transmission prevention and recognizing signs of complications.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Patient Education: Nurses play a crucial role in educating patients and families about symptom management, activity restrictions (especially regarding splenic rupture), and infection control measures.
  • Symptom Assessment: Meticulous assessment of vital signs, pain levels, throat condition, and abdominal tenderness is essential to monitor for complications.
  • Fluid and Nutrition Support: Assist patients in maintaining adequate hydration and nutrition, offering palatable soft foods and encouraging fluid intake.
  • Psychosocial Support: Acknowledge the significant impact of prolonged fatigue and provide emotional support, encouraging gradual return to activities.
  • Infection Control: Reinforce standard precautions in clinical settings to prevent transmission, especially hand hygiene.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Pathophysiology: EBV primarily infects B lymphocytes and epithelial cells, leading to a robust cytotoxic T-lymphocyte response responsible for many of the clinical symptoms.
  • Diagnostic Markers: Heterophile antibodies (Monospot test) are often positive, especially after the first week of illness. EBV-specific antibody tests (e.g., VCA-IgM, VCA-IgG, EBNA) can confirm diagnosis and stage infection.
  • Differential Diagnosis: Consider other viral infections (e.g., CMV, HIV primary infection, adenovirus), streptococcal pharyngitis, toxoplasmosis, and acute leukemia.
  • Complications: Rare but serious complications include splenic rupture, airway obstruction, hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, hepatitis, myocarditis, encephalitis, and Guillain-Barré syndrome.
  • Chronic EBV: In some cases, EBV can lead to chronic active EBV infection or be associated with certain malignancies (e.g., Burkitt lymphoma, nasopharyngeal carcinoma) in specific populations.
