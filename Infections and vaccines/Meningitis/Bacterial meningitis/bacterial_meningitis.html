Bacterial meningitis

🏠 Home / Infections and vaccines / Meningitis

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Serious Infection: Bacterial meningitis is a severe infection of the meninges, the protective membranes surrounding the brain and spinal cord.
  • Rapid Progression: It can progress rapidly, leading to life-threatening complications if not treated promptly.
  • Common Pathogens: Key causative bacteria include Streptococcus pneumoniae, Neisseria meningitidis, and Haemophilus influenzae type b (Hib).
  • Transmission: Spreads through respiratory droplets or close contact with an infected person's secretions.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Sudden High Fever: Often accompanied by chills and malaise.
  • Severe Headache: Typically intense and persistent, not relieved by over-the-counter pain relievers.
  • Stiff Neck (Nuchal Rigidity): Difficulty or pain when trying to touch the chin to the chest.
  • Altered Mental Status: Confusion, disorientation, difficulty concentrating, or decreased level of consciousness.
  • Photophobia: Increased sensitivity to light.
  • Nausea and Vomiting: Common gastrointestinal symptoms.
  • Seizures: Can occur due to inflammation and pressure on the brain.
  • Rash: A non-blanching petechial or purpuric rash is characteristic of meningococcal meningitis.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Vaccination: Ensure up-to-date vaccinations against Haemophilus influenzae type b (Hib), Streptococcus pneumoniae (pneumococcal), and Neisseria meningitidis (meningococcal) according to national guidelines.
  • Prompt Treatment: Seek immediate medical attention if symptoms of meningitis are suspected to prevent severe outcomes.
  • Contact Precautions: Implement droplet precautions for suspected or confirmed cases in healthcare settings to prevent spread.
  • Avoid Close Contact: Minimize close contact with individuals exhibiting respiratory symptoms, especially in crowded environments.
  • Hand Hygiene: Practice rigorous hand washing, particularly after coughing, sneezing, or contact with respiratory secretions.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Hydration Management: Maintain adequate fluid intake, often intravenously, especially if oral intake is compromised by nausea, vomiting, or altered consciousness.
  • Nutritional Support: Provide easily digestible, soft foods or liquid diets once oral intake is safe and tolerated, to support recovery and energy needs.
  • Aspiration Risk: Carefully assess swallowing function and elevate the head of the bed during feeding for patients with altered mental status to prevent aspiration.
  • Electrolyte Balance: Monitor and manage electrolyte imbalances, which can be affected by fever, vomiting, and fluid shifts.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Emergency Medical Care: Bacterial meningitis is a medical emergency requiring immediate hospitalization and intravenous antibiotic therapy.
  • Isolation Protocols: Patients with suspected meningococcal meningitis require droplet precautions for at least 24 hours after initiating effective antibiotic treatment.
  • Neurological Monitoring: Continuous assessment of neurological status, including Glasgow Coma Scale, pupillary response, and motor function, is crucial.
  • Vital Sign Surveillance: Frequent monitoring of vital signs (temperature, heart rate, blood pressure, respiratory rate) to detect signs of deterioration or complications.
  • Medication Adherence: Strict adherence to the prescribed antibiotic regimen and duration is essential to eradicate the infection and prevent recurrence.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Early Diagnosis is Key: Rapid diagnosis via lumbar puncture and CSF analysis is paramount for initiating appropriate empiric antibiotic therapy.
  • Empiric Antibiotics: Start broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics immediately upon suspicion, even before definitive culture results, to improve outcomes.
  • Corticosteroid Use: Consider adjunctive dexamethasone in specific cases (e.g., S. pneumoniae meningitis) to reduce inflammation and neurological sequelae.
  • Prophylaxis: Administer chemoprophylaxis to close contacts of patients with meningococcal meningitis to prevent secondary cases.
  • Complication Management: Actively monitor for and manage potential complications such as hearing loss, seizures, hydrocephalus, and cognitive impairment.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Neurological Assessment: Perform frequent and thorough neurological assessments to detect subtle changes in mental status, motor function, and cranial nerve function.
  • Antibiotic Administration: Administer prescribed intravenous antibiotics on schedule, ensuring correct dosage and monitoring for adverse reactions.
  • Fluid and Electrolyte Balance: Monitor intake and output, assess hydration status, and manage intravenous fluids to prevent fluid overload or dehydration.
  • Pain Management: Assess and manage headache and neck pain effectively using prescribed analgesics.
  • Infection Control: Educate patients and families on infection control measures, including hand hygiene and the importance of completing antibiotic courses.
  • Post-Procedure Care: Provide care following lumbar puncture, ensuring patient comfort and monitoring for complications like post-dural puncture headache.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Etiology: Predominantly caused by Streptococcus pneumoniae, Neisseria meningitidis, and Haemophilus influenzae type b (in unvaccinated populations).
  • Diagnosis: Confirmed by cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis (elevated white blood cells, low glucose, high protein), Gram stain, and bacterial culture from lumbar puncture.
  • Treatment: Empiric broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics (e.g., ceftriaxone, vancomycin) adjusted based on CSF culture and sensitivity results.
  • Pathophysiology: Bacterial invasion of the subarachnoid space leads to inflammation of the meninges, increased intracranial pressure, and potential cerebral ischemia.
  • Complications: Can include hearing loss, seizures, hydrocephalus, focal neurological deficits, cognitive impairment, and death.
  • Vaccine Efficacy: Vaccines for Hib, pneumococcus, and meningococcus have significantly reduced the incidence of bacterial meningitis.
