Tuberculosis

🏠 Home / Infections and vaccines / Tuberculosis

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Bacterial Infection: Tuberculosis (TB) is caused by the bacterium Mycobacterium tuberculosis, primarily affecting the lungs but capable of impacting other organs.
  • Airborne Transmission: The bacteria spread through the air when an infected person coughs, sneezes, or speaks, releasing tiny droplets.
  • Latent vs. Active TB: Many people have latent TB infection (LTBI), meaning they carry the bacteria but show no symptoms and are not contagious. Active TB disease occurs when the bacteria multiply and cause symptoms, making the person contagious.
  • Global Health Concern: TB remains a leading cause of death worldwide, especially in low-income countries, and is a significant public health challenge.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Persistent Cough: A cough lasting three weeks or longer, often producing sputum, sometimes with blood.
  • Chest Pain: Pain in the chest, which may worsen with breathing or coughing.
  • Fever and Chills: Unexplained fever, often low-grade, accompanied by chills.
  • Night Sweats: Drenching sweats occurring during sleep.
  • Unexplained Weight Loss: Significant loss of body weight without intentional dieting.
  • Fatigue and Weakness: Persistent tiredness and general feeling of malaise.
  • Loss of Appetite: Reduced desire to eat, contributing to weight loss.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Infection Control: Patients with active pulmonary TB should be isolated in negative-pressure rooms or advised to stay home, avoiding public places until deemed non-infectious.
  • Respiratory Hygiene: Cover mouth and nose when coughing or sneezing, preferably with a tissue, and dispose of it immediately.
  • Mask Use: Individuals with active TB should wear a surgical mask when outside their isolation area or when around others. Healthcare workers should use N95 respirators when caring for TB patients.
  • Contact Tracing: Identify and screen close contacts of individuals with active TB to detect and treat new cases or latent infections.
  • Medication Adherence: Emphasize the critical importance of completing the full course of prescribed anti-TB medication to prevent drug resistance and ensure cure.
  • BCG Vaccination: In regions with high TB prevalence, the Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) vaccine is administered, primarily to protect infants and young children from severe forms of TB.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Nutrient-Rich Diet: Encourage a balanced diet rich in protein, vitamins (especially A, C, E, and B-complex), and minerals (iron, zinc) to support immune function and recovery.
  • Adequate Hydration: Maintain good fluid intake to prevent dehydration, especially with fever and night sweats.
  • Small, Frequent Meals: For patients experiencing appetite loss or nausea due to medication, suggest smaller, more frequent meals to ensure adequate caloric and nutrient intake.
  • Avoid Alcohol: Strictly advise against alcohol consumption, as it can exacerbate liver toxicity, a potential side effect of several anti-TB medications (e.g., isoniazid, rifampin).
  • Manage Medication Side Effects: Dietary adjustments may help manage specific drug side effects, such as taking medications with food to reduce gastrointestinal upset.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Strict Medication Adherence: Ensure the patient takes all prescribed medications exactly as directed, without missing doses, for the entire duration of treatment. Directly Observed Therapy (DOT) may be recommended.
  • Monitor for Side Effects: Watch for signs of adverse drug reactions, such as jaundice, dark urine, persistent nausea, vomiting, skin rash, or vision changes, and report them immediately to the healthcare provider.
  • Infection Control at Home: Advise the patient to sleep in a separate room if possible, ensure good ventilation, and avoid close contact with vulnerable individuals (e.g., infants, immunocompromised persons) until deemed non-infectious.
  • Follow-up Appointments: Emphasize the necessity of attending all scheduled follow-up appointments for monitoring treatment efficacy and managing side effects.
  • Educate Family Members: Provide education to household members about TB transmission, symptoms, and the importance of screening if exposed.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Early Diagnosis is Crucial: Prompt identification of active TB cases is vital for initiating treatment, preventing disease progression, and curbing community transmission.
  • Comprehensive Treatment Regimen: TB treatment involves a multi-drug regimen, typically lasting 6-9 months, tailored to the patient's specific strain and drug susceptibility.
  • Directly Observed Therapy (DOT): For many patients, DOT is the gold standard to ensure medication adherence and improve treatment outcomes, especially in preventing drug resistance.
  • Drug-Resistant TB: Be vigilant for signs of drug-resistant TB (MDR-TB, XDR-TB), which requires specialized and longer treatment regimens, often with more toxic drugs.
  • Public Health Reporting: All confirmed cases of active TB must be reported to public health authorities for surveillance, contact investigation, and outbreak control.
  • Screening High-Risk Groups: Regular screening for latent TB infection is recommended for individuals at higher risk, such as close contacts of active cases, immunocompromised patients, and healthcare workers.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Patient Education: Nurses play a critical role in educating patients and families about TB, its transmission, the importance of medication adherence, and infection control measures.
  • Medication Administration & Monitoring: Administer anti-TB drugs, monitor for side effects, and provide patient teaching on how to manage common adverse reactions.
  • Infection Control Protocols: Implement and reinforce strict airborne isolation precautions in healthcare settings for patients with suspected or confirmed active pulmonary TB.
  • Psychosocial Support: Provide emotional support and address social determinants of health that may impact treatment adherence, such as housing, nutrition, and access to care.
  • DOT Implementation: Facilitate and participate in Directly Observed Therapy programs, ensuring patients take their medication consistently.
  • Symptom Assessment: Regularly assess patients for resolution of symptoms and development of new or worsening symptoms that may indicate treatment failure or drug resistance.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Diagnostic Modalities: Include Tuberculin Skin Test (TST) and Interferon-Gamma Release Assays (IGRAs) for LTBI screening; sputum smear microscopy (acid-fast bacilli), sputum culture, nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs), and chest X-ray for active TB.
  • First-Line Treatment Regimen (RIPE): Standard treatment for drug-susceptible TB typically involves Rifampin, Isoniazid, Pyrazinamide, and Ethambutol.
  • Drug Resistance: Multidrug-resistant TB (MDR-TB) is resistant to at least isoniazid and rifampin. Extensively drug-resistant TB (XDR-TB) is resistant to isoniazid and rifampin, plus any fluoroquinolone and at least one of three injectable second-line drugs.
  • Latent TB Treatment: Treatment for LTBI aims to prevent progression to active disease, often involving isoniazid or rifampin monotherapy for several months.
  • Public Health Surveillance: Mandatory reporting of TB cases to local and national health departments is essential for epidemiological tracking and control efforts.
  • Immunocompromised Patients: TB presents unique challenges in immunocompromised individuals (e.g., HIV-positive, transplant recipients), often requiring longer treatment durations and careful monitoring for drug interactions.
