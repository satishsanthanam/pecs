Preventing HIV after a possible exposure

🏠 Home / Infections and vaccines / Hepatitis

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Post-Exposure Prophylaxis (PEP): PEP is a course of antiretroviral medications taken after a potential exposure to HIV to prevent infection. It is highly effective when started promptly.
  • Time-Sensitive Intervention: PEP must be initiated as soon as possible, ideally within hours, and no later than 72 hours (3 days) after a potential exposure. Every hour counts.
  • Mechanism of Action: PEP works by preventing the HIV virus from replicating and establishing a permanent infection in the body.
  • Consultation is Key: Immediate medical evaluation by a healthcare professional is crucial to assess the risk of exposure and determine if PEP is appropriate.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Unprotected Sexual Contact: Exposure involving vaginal, anal, or oral sex without barrier protection with a partner of unknown or positive HIV status.
  • Sharing Injection Equipment: Use of contaminated needles, syringes, or other drug injection paraphernalia.
  • Needlestick or Sharps Injury: Accidental puncture wounds from needles or sharp objects contaminated with blood from an HIV-positive individual.
  • Mucous Membrane or Non-Intact Skin Exposure: Contact of blood, semen, vaginal fluids, or breast milk from an HIV-positive person with eyes, mouth, nose, or broken skin.
  • Sexual Assault: Any non-consensual sexual contact carries a potential risk of HIV transmission, necessitating immediate medical evaluation for PEP.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Strict Adherence to Regimen: Take all prescribed PEP medications exactly as directed for the full 28-day course, without missing doses, to maximize effectiveness.
  • Avoid Further Exposures: Practice safe sex (e.g., consistent condom use) and avoid sharing needles during the PEP course and until follow-up HIV testing confirms status.
  • Inform Healthcare Providers: Disclose all medications, supplements, and pre-existing conditions to your doctor to prevent potential drug interactions or contraindications with PEP.
  • Regular Follow-up Testing: Adhere to the recommended schedule for HIV testing (e.g., at baseline, 4-6 weeks, and 3 months post-exposure) to confirm HIV status.
  • Monitor for Side Effects: Be vigilant for potential side effects of PEP medications, such as nausea, fatigue, or headache, and report any severe or persistent symptoms to your provider.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Take with Food: Many PEP medications are better tolerated when taken with food to reduce gastrointestinal side effects like nausea or stomach upset. Follow specific medication instructions.
  • Maintain Hydration: Drink plenty of fluids, especially water, throughout the PEP course to help manage potential side effects and support overall well-being.
  • Avoid Alcohol and Illicit Drugs: These substances can exacerbate side effects, interfere with medication absorption, or impair judgment, potentially leading to missed doses or further exposures.
  • Manage Nausea: If experiencing nausea, opt for bland, easy-to-digest foods like crackers, toast, rice, or bananas. Avoid greasy, spicy, or highly acidic foods.
  • Consistent Meal Times: Try to take medications at consistent times each day, often with meals, to establish a routine and improve adherence.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Complete the Full Course: Stopping PEP early significantly reduces its effectiveness and increases the risk of HIV infection. Complete the entire 28-day regimen.
  • Potential Drug Interactions: PEP medications can interact with other drugs, including over-the-counter medications, herbal supplements, and recreational drugs. Always consult your doctor or pharmacist.
  • Side Effect Management: While side effects are common, they are usually manageable. Do not discontinue PEP without consulting your healthcare provider; they can offer strategies or adjust the regimen if necessary.
  • Confidentiality and Support: Seek support from trusted individuals or counseling services. Healthcare providers are bound by confidentiality and can offer resources.
  • Post-PEP Testing: Understand that a negative HIV test immediately after exposure does not rule out infection. Follow-up testing is essential to confirm HIV status after completing PEP.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Patient Education on PEP: Nurses play a vital role in educating patients about the importance of PEP, its 72-hour window, the 28-day regimen, potential side effects, and the need for strict adherence.
  • Side Effect Management Support: Provide practical advice for managing common PEP side effects (e.g., nausea, fatigue) and encourage patients to report severe symptoms for medical review.
  • Confidentiality and Non-Judgmental Care: Ensure a supportive, confidential, and non-judgmental environment for patients seeking PEP, recognizing the sensitive nature of potential HIV exposure.
  • Follow-up Schedule Reinforcement: Clearly communicate the schedule for follow-up HIV testing and clinic visits, emphasizing their importance in confirming PEP success and ongoing health.
  • Resource Navigation: Assist patients in accessing resources for ongoing HIV prevention, sexual health counseling, mental health support, and financial assistance for medication if needed.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Post-Exposure Prophylaxis (PEP): A short-term antiretroviral treatment initiated within 72 hours of potential HIV exposure to prevent seroconversion.
  • Antiretroviral Therapy (ART): A combination of medications used to treat HIV infection by inhibiting viral replication at various stages of the HIV life cycle.
  • Seroconversion: The period during which HIV antibodies develop and become detectable in the blood, typically 3-12 weeks post-exposure.
  • Acute Retroviral Syndrome (ARS): A flu-like illness that can occur 2-4 weeks after initial HIV infection, characterized by fever, rash, fatigue, and lymphadenopathy.
  • HIV-1/2 Antigen/Antibody Combination Immunoassay: A common screening test for HIV that detects both HIV antibodies and the p24 antigen, allowing for earlier detection of infection.
  • Nucleoside Reverse Transcriptase Inhibitors (NRTIs): A class of antiretroviral drugs that inhibit the reverse transcriptase enzyme, preventing HIV from converting its RNA into DNA.
  • Integrase Strand Transfer Inhibitors (INSTIs): A class of antiretroviral drugs that block the integrase enzyme, preventing HIV from integrating its DNA into the host cell's genome.
