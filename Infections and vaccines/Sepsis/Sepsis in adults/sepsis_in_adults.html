Sepsis in adults

🏠 Home / Infections and vaccines / Sepsis

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Definition: Sepsis is a life-threatening condition that arises when the body's response to an infection causes injury to its own tissues and organs.
  • Progression: It can lead to septic shock, multi-organ failure, and death if not recognized and treated promptly.
  • Underlying Cause: Sepsis is always triggered by an existing infection, which can originate anywhere in the body (e.g., pneumonia, urinary tract infection, wound infection).
  • Medical Emergency: Sepsis is a medical emergency requiring immediate diagnosis and treatment to prevent severe complications.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Slurred Speech or Confusion: Sudden changes in mental status, disorientation, or difficulty speaking clearly.
  • Extreme Shivering or Muscle Pain: Uncontrolled shaking, severe body aches, or feeling unusually cold.
  • Passing No Urine (in a day): Significant decrease in urine output, indicating kidney dysfunction.
  • Severe Breathlessness: Rapid, shallow breathing, difficulty catching breath, or feeling short of air.
  • It Feels Like You're Going to Die: A profound sense of impending doom or feeling acutely unwell.
  • Skin Mottled or Discolored: Patches of discolored skin, especially on extremities, or a bluish tint to lips/fingernails.
  • Rapid Heart Rate: Tachycardia, often accompanied by low blood pressure (hypotension).

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Early Recognition: Maintain a high index of suspicion for sepsis in patients with known or suspected infections, especially those with risk factors.
  • Infection Prevention: Implement strict hand hygiene, ensure timely vaccinations, and provide meticulous wound care to prevent initial infections.
  • Antibiotic Stewardship: Prescribe antibiotics judiciously and appropriately to minimize the development of antibiotic resistance.
  • Close Monitoring: Regularly assess vital signs, mental status, and organ function in at-risk patients to detect early signs of deterioration.
  • Fluid Management: Carefully manage intravenous fluid administration to maintain adequate perfusion without causing fluid overload.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Nutritional Support: Initiate early enteral nutrition (via the gut) if feasible, as it is preferred over parenteral nutrition to support gut integrity and immune function.
  • Fluid Balance: Maintain strict intake and output monitoring; fluid resuscitation is critical initially, but fluid restriction may be necessary later to prevent overload.
  • Electrolyte Management: Closely monitor and correct electrolyte imbalances (e.g., potassium, sodium, magnesium) which are often managed intravenously due to critical illness.
  • Gastric Motility: Be aware that gastric motility can be impaired in sepsis, potentially requiring prokinetic agents or post-pyloric feeding.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Rapid Antibiotic Administration: Administer broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics within one hour of sepsis recognition after obtaining appropriate cultures.
  • Fluid Resuscitation: Provide an initial bolus of intravenous crystalloids (e.g., 30 mL/kg) for hypotension or elevated lactate, then titrate based on patient response.
  • Source Control: Promptly identify and control the source of infection (e.g., drain abscesses, remove infected catheters, debride necrotic tissue).
  • Vasopressor Therapy: Initiate vasopressors (e.g., norepinephrine) if hypotension persists despite adequate fluid resuscitation to maintain mean arterial pressure.
  • Lactate Monitoring: Perform serial lactate measurements to assess tissue perfusion and guide resuscitation efforts.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • High Index of Suspicion: Always consider sepsis in patients presenting with signs of infection and new organ dysfunction, especially in vulnerable populations.
  • Early Goal-Directed Therapy: Adhere to established sepsis protocols, such as those from the Surviving Sepsis Campaign, to optimize outcomes.
  • Multidisciplinary Approach: Collaborate closely with intensivists, infectious disease specialists, surgeons, and nursing staff for comprehensive patient management.
  • Prognosis Communication: Engage in clear and empathetic communication with patients and their families regarding the severity of sepsis, treatment plans, and potential outcomes.
  • Reassessment: Continuously reassess the patient's response to therapy and adjust interventions as clinical status evolves.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Sepsis Screening Tools: Utilize validated screening tools (e.g., NEWS2, qSOFA) to identify patients at risk for sepsis promptly.
  • Fluid Challenge Monitoring: Closely monitor patient response to fluid boluses, including vital signs, urine output, and signs of fluid overload.
  • Medication Timeliness: Ensure timely administration of antibiotics, vasopressors, and other critical medications as per physician orders and protocols.
  • Comprehensive Assessment: Perform frequent and thorough patient assessments, including neurological status, respiratory effort, skin perfusion, and urine output.
  • Accurate Documentation: Maintain precise and timely documentation of all assessments, interventions, and patient responses to track progress and inform care decisions.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • SOFA Score: Sequential Organ Failure Assessment score, used to quantify organ dysfunction and predict mortality in sepsis.
  • Lactate Levels: A key biomarker for tissue hypoperfusion and anaerobic metabolism, often elevated in sepsis and septic shock.
  • Procalcitonin: A biomarker that can aid in the diagnosis of bacterial infections and guide antibiotic stewardship.
  • Blood Cultures: Essential for identifying the causative pathogen and guiding targeted antibiotic therapy.
  • Vasopressor Agents: Medications like norepinephrine, vasopressin, and dopamine used to increase blood pressure in septic shock.
  • C-Reactive Protein (CRP): An acute phase reactant, often elevated in inflammatory conditions including sepsis.
