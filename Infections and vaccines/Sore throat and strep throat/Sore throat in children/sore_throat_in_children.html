Sore throat in children

🏠 Home / Infections and vaccines / Sore throat and strep throat

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Common Childhood Ailment: Sore throats are very common in children, often caused by viral infections, but can also be bacterial, most notably Group A Streptococcus (Strep throat).
  • Viral vs. Bacterial: Viral sore throats typically resolve on their own, while bacterial infections like Strep throat require antibiotic treatment to prevent complications.
  • Age-Related Incidence: Strep throat is most common in school-aged children (5-15 years old) but can occur in younger children, though less frequently.
  • Contagious Nature: Both viral and bacterial sore throats are contagious and spread through respiratory droplets from coughing, sneezing, or close contact.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Painful Swallowing: Difficulty or pain when swallowing food or liquids, often leading to refusal to eat.
  • Fever: Elevated body temperature, which can range from mild to high, often accompanying the sore throat.
  • Headache: A common complaint, especially in older children, accompanying the general malaise.
  • Stomach Ache & Vomiting: More common in younger children with Strep throat, presenting as abdominal pain or nausea.
  • Red & Swollen Tonsils: Visual inspection may reveal red, inflamed tonsils, sometimes with white patches or streaks of pus.
  • Tiny Red Spots (Petechiae): Small red spots on the soft or hard palate can be indicative of Strep throat.
  • Rash (Scarlet Fever): A fine, sandpaper-like red rash that may accompany Strep throat, particularly on the neck, chest, and groin.
  • Swollen Lymph Nodes: Tender, enlarged lymph nodes in the neck are a frequent finding.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Seek Medical Evaluation: Consult a healthcare provider promptly if a child has a severe sore throat, difficulty breathing, drooling, rash, or refusal to drink fluids.
  • Prevent Spread: Emphasize frequent hand washing, covering coughs and sneezes, and avoiding sharing utensils, cups, and food items.
  • Hydration Monitoring: Closely monitor for signs of dehydration, such as decreased urination, dry mouth, or lethargy, especially if the child is reluctant to drink.
  • Avoid Irritants: Protect the child from exposure to cigarette smoke, strong fumes, or very dry air, which can exacerbate throat irritation.
  • Complete Antibiotic Course: If Strep throat is diagnosed and antibiotics are prescribed, ensure the child completes the entire course, even if symptoms improve, to prevent recurrence and complications.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Soft, Easy-to-Swallow Foods: Offer pureed soups, mashed potatoes, yogurt, applesauce, and cooked cereals to minimize discomfort during swallowing.
  • Cool Liquids & Foods: Provide cool drinks, popsicles, and ice chips to soothe the throat and help with hydration.
  • Ensure Adequate Hydration: Encourage frequent sips of water, diluted juices, or electrolyte solutions to prevent dehydration.
  • Avoid Acidic & Spicy Foods: Restrict citrus juices, tomatoes, spicy dishes, and crunchy foods that can irritate a sore throat.
  • Warm, Soothing Drinks: Offer warm (not hot) decaffeinated tea with honey (for children over 1 year old) or warm water to provide comfort.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Symptom Observation: Continuously monitor the child for worsening symptoms, new symptoms, or signs of complications like difficulty breathing or severe lethargy.
  • Pain Management: Administer age-appropriate doses of acetaminophen or ibuprofen as directed by a healthcare provider for pain and fever relief.
  • Rest & Comfort: Ensure the child gets plenty of rest to aid recovery and provide comfort measures such as humidifiers in their room.
  • Isolation for Contagion: Keep children with suspected or confirmed Strep throat home from school or daycare until they have been on antibiotics for at least 24 hours and are fever-free.
  • Follow-up Care: Adhere to any recommended follow-up appointments or testing as advised by the physician.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Accurate Diagnosis is Key: Differentiating between viral and bacterial pharyngitis is crucial; a rapid strep test or throat culture is often necessary for definitive diagnosis of Strep throat.
  • Antibiotic Stewardship: Prescribe antibiotics only for confirmed bacterial infections to prevent antibiotic resistance and unnecessary side effects. Penicillin or amoxicillin are typically first-line treatments for Strep throat.
  • Preventing Complications: Timely treatment of Strep throat with antibiotics is essential to prevent serious complications such as acute rheumatic fever and post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis.
  • Parental Education: Educate parents on the signs of dehydration, when to return for re-evaluation, and the importance of completing the full course of antibiotics.
  • Symptomatic Relief: Advise on supportive care measures, including pain relief, hydration, and rest, to manage discomfort regardless of the cause.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Clinical Assessment: Focus on a thorough history (onset, associated symptoms, exposure) and physical examination, including inspection of the pharynx, tonsils, and palpation of cervical lymph nodes.
  • Rapid Strep Testing: Understand the procedure for collecting a throat swab for rapid antigen detection tests (RADT) and subsequent throat culture if RADT is negative but suspicion remains high.
  • Patient & Family Education: Provide clear instructions on medication administration, potential side effects, importance of adherence, and infection control measures.
  • Recognizing Red Flags: Be vigilant for signs of airway compromise (stridor, severe drooling, tripod position) or systemic toxicity, which warrant immediate medical intervention.
  • Comfort Interventions: Implement nursing interventions focused on comfort, such as offering cool liquids, popsicles, and administering prescribed analgesics.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Etiology: Predominantly viral (e.g., rhinovirus, adenovirus, influenza, parainfluenza, Epstein-Barr virus) or bacterial (Group A Streptococcus pyogenes).
  • Diagnostic Criteria: Clinical assessment often guided by scoring systems like the Centor or modified McIsaac score, followed by laboratory confirmation (RADT, throat culture).
  • Differential Diagnoses: Include infectious mononucleosis, peritonsillar abscess, epiglottitis, retropharyngeal abscess, and allergic pharyngitis.
  • Pharmacological Management: For Strep throat, first-line antibiotics include penicillin V or amoxicillin; macrolides (e.g., azithromycin) for penicillin-allergic patients. Symptomatic relief with NSAIDs or acetaminophen.
  • Complications of Untreated Strep: Acute Rheumatic Fever (ARF), Post-Streptococcal Glomerulonephritis (PSGN), peritonsillar abscess, cervical lymphadenitis, otitis media, sinusitis.
