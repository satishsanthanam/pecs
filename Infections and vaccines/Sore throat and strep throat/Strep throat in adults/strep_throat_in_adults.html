Strep throat in adults

🏠 Home / Infections and vaccines / Sore throat and strep throat

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Causative Agent: Strep throat is a bacterial infection caused by *Streptococcus pyogenes*, also known as Group A Streptococcus (GAS).
  • Transmission: It is highly contagious, spreading through respiratory droplets when an infected person coughs, sneezes, or talks.
  • Prevalence in Adults: While more common in children, adults can contract strep throat, especially those in close contact with school-aged children.
  • Potential Complications: Untreated strep throat can lead to serious complications such as rheumatic fever, post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis, and peritonsillar abscess.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Sudden Sore Throat: Often severe and rapid in onset, making swallowing painful.
  • Painful Swallowing (Dysphagia): Difficulty and discomfort when attempting to swallow food or liquids.
  • Fever: Typically 101°F (38.3°C) or higher, often accompanied by chills.
  • Red and Swollen Tonsils: May appear bright red, sometimes with white patches of pus or streaks.
  • Tiny Red Spots (Petechiae): Small red spots may be visible on the soft or hard palate at the back of the mouth.
  • Headache: A common accompanying symptom.
  • Nausea, Vomiting, or Abdominal Pain: More frequent in children but can occur in adults.
  • Rash (Scarlet Fever): A fine, sandpaper-like rash can develop, indicating scarlet fever, a complication of strep throat.
  • Absence of Cough or Runny Nose: Unlike viral colds, strep throat typically does not present with these symptoms.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Hand Hygiene: Wash hands frequently and thoroughly with soap and water, especially after coughing, sneezing, or touching contaminated surfaces.
  • Avoid Sharing: Do not share eating utensils, drinking glasses, food, or personal items with others.
  • Cover Coughs and Sneezes: Use a tissue or the crook of your elbow to cover your mouth and nose when coughing or sneezing.
  • Isolation: Stay home from work, school, or public gatherings until you have been fever-free for 24 hours and have completed at least 24 hours of antibiotic treatment.
  • Complete Antibiotic Course: It is critical to finish the entire prescribed course of antibiotics, even if symptoms improve, to prevent recurrence and complications.
  • Monitor for Complications: Be vigilant for signs of rheumatic fever (e.g., joint pain, rash, heart murmurs) or kidney issues (e.g., dark urine, swelling).

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Soft, Easy-to-Swallow Foods: Opt for foods like soups, broths, yogurt, mashed potatoes, scrambled eggs, and smoothies to minimize throat irritation.
  • Adequate Hydration: Drink plenty of fluids such as water, herbal teas, and clear broths to prevent dehydration and soothe the throat.
  • Avoid Irritating Foods: Steer clear of spicy, acidic (e.g., citrus juices), crunchy, or very hot foods and beverages that can exacerbate throat pain.
  • Temperature Preference: Some individuals find warm liquids (e.g., tea with honey) soothing, while others prefer cold items like popsicles or ice chips to numb the throat.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Prompt Medical Evaluation: Seek medical attention immediately if you suspect strep throat for accurate diagnosis and timely treatment.
  • Strict Antibiotic Adherence: Emphasize the importance of completing the full antibiotic regimen as prescribed, even if symptoms resolve quickly, to eradicate the bacteria and prevent serious sequelae.
  • Contagion Period Awareness: Patients remain contagious until they have been on appropriate antibiotic therapy for at least 24 hours and are fever-free.
  • Inform Close Contacts: Advise household members and close contacts to monitor for symptoms and seek testing if they develop a sore throat or fever.
  • Recurrent Infections: If strep throat recurs frequently, discuss with your physician to rule out carrier status or other underlying factors.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Diagnostic Confirmation: A rapid strep antigen detection test (RADT) or a throat culture is essential for confirming the diagnosis of strep throat, as clinical presentation alone can be misleading.
  • First-Line Treatment: Penicillin or amoxicillin are the preferred antibiotics due to their efficacy, narrow spectrum, and low cost. For penicillin-allergic patients, macrolides (e.g., azithromycin) or clindamycin are alternatives.
  • Symptomatic Management: Recommend over-the-counter pain relievers such as acetaminophen or ibuprofen for fever and throat pain. Throat lozenges or sprays can also provide temporary relief.
  • Prevention of Complications: Stress to patients that antibiotic treatment is primarily aimed at preventing acute rheumatic fever and other serious complications, not just symptom relief.
  • Differential Diagnosis: Always consider other causes of pharyngitis, including viral infections (e.g., common cold, influenza, infectious mononucleosis), which do not require antibiotic treatment.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Clinical Assessment: Utilize the Centor criteria (or modified Centor score) to assess the likelihood of strep throat and guide the decision for testing.
  • Specimen Collection: Master the proper technique for obtaining a throat swab for rapid antigen detection tests (RADT) or throat culture, ensuring adequate sampling of the tonsils and posterior pharynx.
  • Patient Education: Provide clear instructions on antibiotic administration, potential side effects, the importance of completing the full course, and infection control measures.
  • Monitoring for Deterioration: Educate patients and caregivers on signs of worsening infection or complications, such as difficulty breathing, severe unilateral throat pain, or new rashes, prompting immediate re-evaluation.
  • Community Health Role: Understand the role of nurses in public health surveillance and education to prevent the spread of Group A Strep in community settings.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Etiology: *Streptococcus pyogenes* (Group A Beta-Hemolytic Streptococcus - GABHS).
  • Pathophysiology: Bacterial adherence to pharyngeal epithelial cells, evasion of host immunity, and production of various virulence factors including streptolysins, pyrogenic exotoxins, and hyaluronidase.
  • Diagnosis: Rapid Antigen Detection Test (RADT) for quick identification; Throat Culture as the gold standard for confirmation, especially if RADT is negative and clinical suspicion remains high.
  • Treatment Protocol: Penicillin V potassium (oral), Amoxicillin (oral), or Benzathine Penicillin G (intramuscular) are first-line. For penicillin allergy, Azithromycin, Clindamycin, or Cephalexin may be used.
  • Key Complications: Acute Rheumatic Fever (ARF), Post-Streptococcal Glomerulonephritis (PSGN), Peritonsillar Abscess, Scarlet Fever, Streptococcal Toxic Shock Syndrome.
  • ICD-10 Codes: J02.0 (Streptococcal pharyngitis).
