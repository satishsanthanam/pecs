Endocarditis

🏠 Home / Infections and vaccines / Endocarditis

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Definition: Endocarditis is an inflammation of the inner lining of the heart chambers and valves (endocardium), typically caused by bacterial or, less commonly, fungal infection.
  • Primary Cause: Most cases are infective endocarditis, resulting from microorganisms entering the bloodstream and attaching to damaged heart valves or endocardial surfaces.
  • Key Risk Factors: Pre-existing heart conditions (e.g., prosthetic heart valves, congenital heart defects, damaged native valves), intravenous drug use, poor dental hygiene, and indwelling catheters.
  • Potential Severity: Can lead to severe heart valve damage, heart failure, systemic embolization (e.g., stroke, pulmonary embolism), and widespread infection if untreated.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • General Symptoms: Persistent fever, chills, fatigue, malaise, night sweats, and unexplained weight loss.
  • Cardiac Manifestations: Development of a new heart murmur or a change in an existing murmur, indicating valve damage.
  • Cutaneous Signs: Petechiae (small red spots), splinter hemorrhages under fingernails, Osler's nodes (painful red nodules on fingers/toes), and Janeway lesions (non-tender macules on palms/soles).
  • Embolic Phenomena: Symptoms related to emboli traveling to other organs, such as stroke (neurological deficits), limb ischemia, splenic infarction (abdominal pain), or renal infarction.
  • Musculoskeletal Pain: Arthralgia (joint pain) and myalgia (muscle pain) are common.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Antibiotic Prophylaxis: Administering prophylactic antibiotics for high-risk patients undergoing certain dental procedures or medical interventions that may cause bacteremia.
  • Oral Hygiene: Maintaining meticulous oral hygiene to prevent bacteremia originating from dental infections.
  • IV Drug Use Avoidance: Strongly advise against intravenous drug use; if unavoidable, emphasize strict sterile injection practices to minimize infection risk.
  • Catheter Care: Implement rigorous sterile techniques for insertion and maintenance of indwelling catheters, and remove them promptly when no longer needed.
  • Early Symptom Recognition: Educate high-risk individuals to recognize and report symptoms like unexplained fever, chills, or new heart murmurs immediately.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Hydration: Ensure adequate fluid intake, especially during febrile episodes, to prevent dehydration and support renal function.
  • Nutrient-Dense Foods: Encourage a diet rich in protein, vitamins, and minerals to support immune function and aid in recovery from infection and potential weight loss.
  • Sodium Restriction: If heart failure develops as a complication, implement sodium restriction to manage fluid retention and reduce cardiac workload.
  • Avoidance of Stimulants: Limit caffeine and alcohol intake, particularly if cardiac function is compromised or arrhythmias are present.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Prompt Diagnosis: Emphasize the critical need for early diagnosis through blood cultures and echocardiography to identify the causative organism and assess valve damage.
  • Adherence to Treatment: Stress the importance of strict adherence to prolonged intravenous antibiotic therapy, often requiring several weeks of treatment.
  • Monitoring for Complications: Closely monitor for signs of heart failure, embolic events (e.g., neurological changes, limb pain), renal dysfunction, and persistent infection.
  • Surgical Consultation: Be prepared for potential surgical intervention for valve repair or replacement in cases of severe valve damage, persistent infection, or recurrent emboli.
  • Infection Control: Maintain strict infection control practices, especially around IV access sites, to prevent secondary infections.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Diagnostic Imperative: A high index of suspicion is crucial, especially in patients with predisposing cardiac conditions or risk factors, necessitating prompt blood cultures (multiple sets) and echocardiography (preferably transesophageal).
  • Tailored Therapy: Treatment involves prolonged, organism-specific intravenous antibiotics, guided by culture and sensitivity results, often requiring a multidisciplinary approach.
  • Surgical Indications: Surgical intervention is considered for severe valvular regurgitation, heart failure, uncontrolled infection, large vegetations with high embolic risk, or prosthetic valve endocarditis.
  • Risk Stratification: Meticulous risk assessment and patient education are vital for prevention, particularly regarding antibiotic prophylaxis guidelines for specific procedures.
  • Long-term Follow-up: Regular follow-up is essential to monitor for recurrence, assess cardiac function, and manage any residual valvular dysfunction.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Patient Education: Educate patients and families on the disease process, the importance of completing the full course of antibiotics, signs of complications, and the need for ongoing oral hygiene.
  • IV Access Management: Meticulous care and maintenance of peripheral or central intravenous access for prolonged antibiotic administration, including site assessment and dressing changes.
  • Vital Sign Monitoring: Frequent assessment of vital signs, including temperature, heart rate, and blood pressure, to detect fever, tachycardia, or signs of hemodynamic instability.
  • Emboli Assessment: Regular neurological assessments, palpation of peripheral pulses, and abdominal assessments to detect signs of systemic embolization.
  • Cardiac Assessment: Auscultate heart sounds regularly to identify new or changing murmurs and monitor for signs of heart failure (e.g., dyspnea, edema).

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Common Pathogens: *Staphylococcus aureus*, Viridans group streptococci, Enterococci, and HACEK organisms (Haemophilus, Aggregatibacter, Cardiobacterium, Eikenella, Kingella).
  • Diagnostic Criteria: Modified Duke Criteria, which include major criteria (e.g., positive blood cultures, evidence of endocardial involvement on echo) and minor criteria (e.g., fever, vascular phenomena, immunological phenomena).
  • Echocardiography Modalities: Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) for initial assessment, and transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) for higher sensitivity in detecting vegetations and assessing valve damage.
  • Major Complications: Heart failure, systemic embolization (e.g., stroke, splenic infarction, renal infarction), mycotic aneurysms, and glomerulonephritis.
  • Prophylaxis Guidelines: American Heart Association (AHA) guidelines specify conditions and procedures warranting antibiotic prophylaxis.
