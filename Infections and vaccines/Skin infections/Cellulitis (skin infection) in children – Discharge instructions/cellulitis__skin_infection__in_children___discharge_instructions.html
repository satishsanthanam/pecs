Cellulitis (skin infection) in children – Discharge instructions

🏠 Home / Infections and vaccines / Skin infections

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Understanding Cellulitis: Cellulitis is a common bacterial infection of the skin and underlying tissues. It often appears as a red, swollen, warm, and tender area.
  • Importance of Treatment: Untreated cellulitis can spread rapidly and lead to serious complications, including bloodstream infections (sepsis) or deeper tissue infections.
  • Antibiotic Therapy: Your child has been prescribed antibiotics to fight the infection. It is crucial to administer these exactly as directed and complete the entire course, even if the skin appears to be improving.
  • Healing Process: Improvement in redness and swelling may take a few days after starting antibiotics. Full resolution can take 1-2 weeks.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Worsening Redness and Swelling: Watch for the infected area becoming larger, redder, or more swollen despite antibiotic treatment.
  • Increased Pain: If your child complains of worsening pain or tenderness at the site of infection.
  • Fever or Chills: A return of fever, new fever, or chills after discharge can indicate the infection is not responding or is spreading.
  • Pus or Drainage: Any new pus, fluid, or foul-smelling discharge from the affected skin area.
  • Red Streaks: The appearance of red streaks extending from the infected area towards the heart, which can indicate lymphangitis.
  • General Malaise: Increased lethargy, poor feeding, irritability, or signs of dehydration (e.g., dry mouth, decreased urination) in infants and young children.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Complete Antibiotic Course: Administer all prescribed antibiotic doses for the full duration, even if symptoms improve. Stopping early can lead to recurrence or antibiotic resistance.
  • Pain Management: Use over-the-counter pain relievers like acetaminophen or ibuprofen (if appropriate for age and without contraindications) as directed by your physician for discomfort.
  • Elevate Affected Limb: If the cellulitis is on an arm or leg, elevate the limb above heart level when resting to help reduce swelling.
  • Avoid Scratching: Prevent your child from scratching the affected area to avoid further skin breakdown and potential spread of bacteria. Keep nails trimmed.
  • Monitor for Allergic Reactions: Watch for signs of an allergic reaction to antibiotics, such as rash, hives, difficulty breathing, or severe swelling. Seek immediate medical attention if these occur.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Maintain Hydration: Encourage your child to drink plenty of fluids (water, clear broths, diluted juices) to prevent dehydration, especially if they have a fever.
  • Nutritional Support: Offer a balanced diet rich in fruits, vegetables, and lean proteins to support the body's healing process and immune function.
  • Medication with Food: If advised by the pharmacist or physician, administer antibiotics with food to minimize stomach upset or nausea.
  • Avoid Irritants: While not directly restricted, ensure your child avoids foods that might cause gastrointestinal distress if they are already feeling unwell from the infection or medication.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Hand Hygiene: Always wash hands thoroughly with soap and water before and after touching the infected area or applying dressings.
  • Wound Care: If there is an open wound or drainage, follow specific instructions for cleaning and dressing changes. Keep the area clean and dry.
  • Medication Administration: Use accurate measuring devices for liquid medications. Do not skip doses or double up if a dose is missed. Store medications as directed.
  • Follow-up Appointment: Ensure you attend all scheduled follow-up appointments with your pediatrician or specialist to monitor healing and assess the need for further treatment.
  • When to Seek Emergency Care: Go to the nearest emergency department if your child develops high fever, severe pain, rapid spread of redness, difficulty breathing, or signs of severe illness.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Adherence to Antibiotics is Key: Emphasize that completing the full course of antibiotics is paramount to eradicate the infection and prevent recurrence or complications.
  • Early Recognition of Worsening: Counsel parents on specific signs of worsening infection or systemic involvement that warrant immediate medical re-evaluation.
  • Pain and Comfort Measures: Advise on appropriate age- and weight-based dosing for analgesics and comfort measures to support the child's recovery.
  • Skin Care and Prevention: Recommend strategies to prevent future skin infections, such as good hygiene, prompt cleaning of cuts/scrapes, and moisturizing dry skin.
  • Follow-up is Non-Negotiable: Stress the importance of the follow-up visit to ensure complete resolution and address any lingering concerns.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Patient Education Reinforcement: Reinforce discharge instructions regarding antibiotic administration, wound care, and signs/symptoms of worsening infection.
  • Demonstration of Care: Provide a clear demonstration of any required wound care or dressing changes, ensuring parental understanding and return demonstration if necessary.
  • Assessment Skills: Educate parents on how to assess the cellulitis site for changes in size, color, warmth, and tenderness, and how to monitor for fever.
  • Medication Reconciliation: Review all prescribed medications with parents, including dosage, frequency, route, and potential side effects.
  • Resource Provision: Provide contact information for the clinic/hospital for questions or concerns, and clarify when to seek emergency care versus calling the pediatrician.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Causative Organisms: Most commonly Staphylococcus aureus (including MRSA) and Streptococcus pyogenes. Less common in children: Haemophilus influenzae (especially pre-Hib vaccine era).
  • Risk Factors: Skin trauma (cuts, scrapes, insect bites), eczema, chickenpox, impetigo, tinea infections, immunosuppression, obesity, poor hygiene.
  • Potential Complications: Abscess formation, necrotizing fasciitis (rare but severe), osteomyelitis, septic arthritis, sepsis, post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis (if S. pyogenes).
  • Antibiotic Choices: Empiric therapy often covers Staphylococcus and Streptococcus. Common choices include cephalexin, clindamycin, amoxicillin/clavulanate, or trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (for suspected MRSA).
  • Diagnostic Considerations: Clinical diagnosis primarily. Blood cultures for systemic signs. Wound cultures if purulent drainage or non-response to initial therapy. Imaging (ultrasound, MRI) for suspected abscess or deeper infection.
