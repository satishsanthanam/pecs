Bacterial folliculitis

🏠 Home / Infections and vaccines / Skin infections

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Definition: Bacterial folliculitis is an inflammation of one or more hair follicles caused by bacterial infection, leading to small, red, pus-filled bumps.
  • Common Causes: Most frequently caused by Staphylococcus aureus, but can also be due to Pseudomonas aeruginosa (often associated with inadequately chlorinated hot tubs).
  • Appearance: Presents as small, red, tender bumps or pustules, often with a hair visible in the center, resembling pimples.
  • Risk Factors: Shaving, tight clothing, excessive sweating, pre-existing skin conditions (e.g., eczema), immunosuppression, and prolonged antibiotic use can increase susceptibility.
  • Progression: Typically superficial and resolves with treatment, but can sometimes lead to deeper infections like furuncles (boils) or carbuncles.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Skin Lesions: Small, red, swollen bumps or pustules centered around hair follicles.
  • Itching and Tenderness: Affected areas may feel itchy, tender, or mildly painful to the touch.
  • Crusting: Lesions may develop a crust as they heal or if they rupture.
  • Location: Commonly found on the scalp, face, neck, armpits, buttocks, thighs, and groin – areas prone to friction or shaving.
  • Mild Discomfort: Generally causes localized discomfort rather than systemic symptoms, unless the infection becomes widespread or deep.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Maintain Hygiene: Regularly cleanse skin with mild soap, especially after sweating or physical activity, to reduce bacterial load.
  • Proper Shaving Techniques: Use a clean, sharp razor, shave in the direction of hair growth, and use a lubricating shave gel or cream. Avoid dry shaving.
  • Wear Loose Clothing: Opt for loose-fitting, breathable fabrics to minimize friction and allow skin to air, especially in humid environments.
  • Hot Tub Safety: Ensure hot tubs and pools are properly chlorinated and maintained; shower immediately after using public facilities.
  • Avoid Picking:1 Refrain from picking, scratching, or squeezing lesions to prevent spread of infection and potential scarring.
  • Moisturize Appropriately: Keep skin hydrated with non-comedogenic moisturizers to maintain skin barrier integrity.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Hydration: Ensure adequate fluid intake to support overall skin health and facilitate immune function.
  • Nutrient-Rich Diet: Consume a balanced diet rich in vitamins (especially A, C, E) and minerals (like zinc) that support skin integrity and immune response.
  • Limit Processed Foods: Minimize intake of highly processed foods and excessive sugars, which can potentially influence inflammatory pathways.
  • Probiotic-Rich Foods: Consider incorporating fermented foods to support a healthy gut microbiome, which can indirectly impact skin health and immunity.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Do Not Self-Treat Deep Infections: Avoid attempting to drain or pop pustules, as this can push bacteria deeper, leading to more severe infections or scarring.
  • Complete Medication Course: If prescribed antibiotics (topical or oral), complete the entire course as directed by your healthcare provider, even if symptoms improve.
  • Avoid Sharing Personal Items: Do not share razors, towels, washcloths, or other personal hygiene items to prevent the spread of bacteria.
  • Monitor for Worsening: Seek medical attention if the infection spreads, becomes more painful, develops into large boils, or is accompanied by fever.
  • Identify Triggers: Work with a healthcare provider to identify and mitigate personal risk factors or triggers for recurrent folliculitis.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Diagnosis: Clinical diagnosis is often made by visual inspection. In recurrent or severe cases, bacterial culture of pus may be performed to identify the causative organism and guide antibiotic selection.
  • Treatment Strategy: Treatment typically involves topical antibiotics (e.g., mupirocin, clindamycin) or antiseptic washes (e.g., chlorhexidine). Oral antibiotics (e.g., cephalexin, dicloxacillin, doxycycline) are reserved for widespread, deep, or persistent infections.
  • Addressing Underlying Factors: It is crucial to identify and manage predisposing conditions such as diabetes, obesity, or immunosuppression to prevent recurrence.
  • Complication Awareness: Physicians must be vigilant for complications like cellulitis, abscess formation, or the development of furuncles and carbuncles, which may require incision and drainage.
  • Patient Education: Emphasize patient education on hygiene practices, proper shaving techniques, and adherence to treatment to ensure effective management and reduce recurrence.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Assessment: Conduct thorough skin assessments, noting the location, size, color, and tenderness of lesions, and inquire about patient's hygiene practices and risk factors.
  • Patient Education: Educate patients on the importance of skin hygiene, proper shaving techniques, avoiding tight clothing, and the correct application of topical medications.
  • Medication Adherence: Instruct patients on the correct dosage, frequency, and duration of prescribed antibiotics, stressing the importance of completing the full course.
  • Infection Control: Advise on measures to prevent spread, such as hand hygiene, not sharing personal items, and cleaning environmental surfaces.
  • Monitoring for Complications: Teach patients to recognize signs of worsening infection (e.g., increased redness, swelling, pain, pus, fever) and when to seek further medical care.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Primary Pathogens: Staphylococcus aureus (most common), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (hot tub folliculitis), occasionally coagulase-negative staphylococci or streptococci.
  • Histopathology: Characterized by a perifollicular inflammatory infiltrate, often neutrophilic, involving the ostium and infundibulum of the hair follicle.
  • Differential Diagnoses: Pseudofolliculitis barbae, fungal folliculitis (tinea barbae), acne vulgaris, keratosis pilaris, drug-induced eruptions, eosinophilic folliculitis.
  • Pharmacological Interventions: Topical: Mupirocin, clindamycin, erythromycin, benzoyl peroxide, chlorhexidine wash. Oral: Cephalexin, dicloxacillin, doxycycline, ciprofloxacin (for Pseudomonas).
  • ICD-10 Codes: L73.8 (Other specified disorders of hair follicles), L01.0 (Impetigo, for superficial staphylococcal infections), L02 (Cutaneous abscess, furuncle and carbuncle, for deeper infections).
