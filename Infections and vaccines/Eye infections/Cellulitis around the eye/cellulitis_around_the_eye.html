Cellulitis around the eye

🏠 Home / Infections and vaccines / Eye infections

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Understanding Cellulitis: Cellulitis around the eye is a bacterial infection of the skin and soft tissues. It can be preseptal (periorbital), affecting the eyelids and skin anterior to the orbital septum, or orbital, affecting tissues posterior to the septum.
  • Severity Distinction: Preseptal cellulitis is generally less severe, while orbital cellulitis is a medical emergency due to its potential for vision loss and intracranial complications.
  • Common Causes: Often results from local trauma, insect bites, skin infections (e.g., impetigo), or spread from adjacent infections like sinusitis.
  • Prompt Medical Attention: Early diagnosis and aggressive treatment are crucial to prevent serious complications, especially with orbital involvement.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Eyelid Swelling & Redness: Marked swelling, erythema (redness), and warmth of the eyelids and periorbital area.
  • Pain & Tenderness: Significant pain and tenderness upon touching the affected area.
  • Fever & Malaise: Systemic symptoms such as fever, chills, and general feeling of unwellness are common.
  • Vision Changes (Orbital): Blurred vision, double vision (diplopia), or decreased visual acuity, indicating potential orbital involvement.
  • Proptosis (Orbital): Bulging of the eyeball (proptosis) is a critical sign of orbital cellulitis.
  • Ocular Motility Restriction (Orbital): Painful or restricted movement of the eye, suggesting inflammation of the extraocular muscles.
  • Chemosis: Swelling of the conjunctiva (the membrane lining the eyelids and covering the white part of the eye).

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Avoid Self-Treatment: Do not attempt to treat cellulitis with home remedies; immediate medical evaluation is essential.
  • Complete Antibiotic Course: Adhere strictly to the prescribed antibiotic regimen, even if symptoms improve, to prevent recurrence and resistance.
  • Monitor for Worsening: Watch for signs of worsening infection, such as increasing pain, swelling, vision changes, or fever, and report them immediately.
  • Maintain Hygiene: Practice good hand hygiene and avoid touching or rubbing the affected eye to prevent further irritation or spread of bacteria.
  • Contact Lens Care: If a contact lens wearer, discontinue use immediately and ensure proper lens hygiene to prevent future infections.
  • Prevent Trauma: Protect the periorbital area from injury, especially if prone to skin breaks or insect bites.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Adequate Hydration: Ensure sufficient fluid intake, especially if fever is present, to support overall recovery and prevent dehydration.
  • Nutrient-Rich Foods: Focus on a balanced diet rich in vitamins and minerals to support immune function during the infection.
  • Avoid Irritants: If there are open wounds or skin breaks, avoid foods or substances that might cause irritation or allergic reactions.
  • Medication Interactions: Be aware of potential food-drug interactions with prescribed antibiotics; consult with a pharmacist or physician regarding specific dietary restrictions.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Emergency Presentation: Any suspicion of orbital cellulitis (e.g., proptosis, vision changes, restricted eye movement) requires immediate emergency medical attention.
  • Intravenous Antibiotics: Orbital cellulitis typically necessitates hospitalization and administration of broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics.
  • Risk of Complications: Untreated or inadequately treated orbital cellulitis can lead to severe complications including permanent vision loss, cavernous sinus thrombosis, meningitis, and brain abscess.
  • Surgical Intervention: Abscess formation within the orbit may require surgical drainage in addition to antibiotic therapy.
  • Pediatric Considerations: Children are particularly susceptible, and prompt diagnosis and treatment are critical to prevent rapid progression.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Thorough Examination: A comprehensive ophthalmic examination is paramount to differentiate preseptal from orbital cellulitis.
  • Imaging Studies: Computed Tomography (CT) scan of the orbits and sinuses is often necessary to assess the extent of infection, identify orbital involvement, and detect abscess formation.
  • Broad-Spectrum Antibiotics: Initial antibiotic therapy should cover common pathogens like Staphylococcus aureus and Streptococcus species, often requiring a combination of agents.
  • Consultation: Early consultation with an ophthalmologist, infectious disease specialist, and possibly an ENT surgeon is recommended, especially for orbital cases.
  • Monitoring Response: Close monitoring of clinical response to antibiotics, vital signs, and visual acuity is essential.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Assessment Skills: Develop proficiency in differentiating preseptal from orbital cellulitis through careful observation of clinical signs (e.g., globe position, extraocular movements, pupillary response).
  • Vital Signs Monitoring: Meticulously monitor vital signs, especially temperature, to track systemic response to infection and treatment.
  • IV Access & Administration: Ensure secure intravenous access for antibiotic administration and monitor for infusion reactions.
  • Pain Management: Assess and manage pain effectively, as cellulitis can be very uncomfortable.
  • Patient Education: Educate patients and caregivers on the importance of medication adherence, recognizing warning signs of worsening infection, and follow-up care.
  • Documentation: Maintain accurate and detailed documentation of assessment findings, interventions, and patient response.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Etiology: Predominantly bacterial, commonly Staphylococcus aureus (including MRSA), Streptococcus pyogenes, and Haemophilus influenzae (less common post-Hib vaccine).
  • Risk Factors: Recent trauma to the periorbital area, insect bites, sinusitis, dental infections, dacryocystitis, recent ocular surgery, foreign bodies, and immunocompromised states.
  • Pathophysiology: Infection spreads from a contiguous source or via hematogenous dissemination, leading to inflammation and edema of the soft tissues.
  • Diagnostic Workup: Clinical assessment, complete blood count (CBC) with differential, blood cultures (especially in febrile patients), and orbital CT scan with contrast.
  • Complications: Vision loss (due to optic nerve compression or ischemia), subperiosteal abscess, orbital abscess, cavernous sinus thrombosis, meningitis, brain abscess, and sepsis.
  • Treatment Modalities: Systemic antibiotics (IV initially for orbital, oral for mild preseptal), surgical drainage for abscesses, and supportive care.
