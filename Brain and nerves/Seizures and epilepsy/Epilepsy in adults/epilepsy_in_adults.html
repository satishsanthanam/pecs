Epilepsy in adults

🏠 Home / Brain and nerves / Seizures and epilepsy

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Definition: Epilepsy is a chronic neurological disorder characterized by recurrent, unprovoked seizures resulting from abnormal electrical activity in the brain.
  • Prevalence in Adults: It can develop at any age, with common causes including genetic factors, brain injury, stroke, tumors, or infections, though often the cause is unknown.
  • Seizure Types: Seizures vary widely, from brief staring spells (absence) to full-body convulsions (tonic-clonic), and can be focal (originating in one part of the brain) or generalized.
  • Impact on Life: Epilepsy can significantly affect daily activities, employment, driving ability, and mental health, necessitating comprehensive management.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Aura (Pre-Seizure): Many individuals experience an 'aura' before a seizure, which can include unusual smells, tastes, visual disturbances, tingling, dizziness, or feelings of fear or déjà vu.
  • Motor Symptoms: These include rhythmic jerking of limbs (clonic), stiffening of muscles (tonic), sudden loss of muscle tone (atonic), or brief, shock-like muscle twitches (myoclonic).
  • Non-Motor Symptoms: Can manifest as staring spells, confusion, unresponsiveness, repetitive non-purposeful movements (automatisms like lip smacking or fumbling), or changes in awareness.
  • Postictal State: Following a seizure, individuals may experience a period of confusion, drowsiness, headache, muscle soreness, or temporary weakness, which can last minutes to hours.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Medication Adherence: Strict and consistent adherence to prescribed antiepileptic drugs (AEDs) is paramount to prevent seizures and maintain therapeutic levels.
  • Trigger Identification & Avoidance: Identify and actively avoid personal seizure triggers such as sleep deprivation, excessive stress, alcohol, certain medications, or flashing lights.
  • Safety Measures: Implement safety precautions like showering instead of bathing, avoiding heights, swimming with supervision, and informing close contacts about your condition and seizure first aid.
  • Driving Restrictions: Be aware of and comply with local driving regulations, which typically require a period of seizure freedom before driving is permitted.
  • Medical Alert: Consider wearing a medical alert bracelet or carrying an identification card detailing your condition and emergency contacts.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Ketogenic Diet: In some cases, particularly for refractory epilepsy, a medically supervised ketogenic diet (high-fat, low-carbohydrate) may be recommended to help control seizures.
  • Hydration: Maintain adequate hydration, as dehydration can sometimes lower the seizure threshold or exacerbate side effects of certain antiepileptic medications.
  • Alcohol & Caffeine: Moderate or avoid alcohol consumption, as it can interfere with AEDs and lower the seizure threshold. Monitor caffeine intake, as it can also be a trigger for some individuals.
  • Medication-Food Interactions: Be aware of potential interactions between your AEDs and certain foods or supplements (e.g., grapefruit juice with carbamazepine), and discuss these with your physician or pharmacist.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Seizure First Aid: If someone has a seizure, stay with them, time the seizure, protect them from injury by clearing the area, loosen tight clothing around the neck, and gently turn them onto their side.
  • Do Not Restrain: Never try to restrain a person having a seizure or put anything in their mouth.
  • When to Call Emergency Services (911/Local Equivalent): Call immediately if a seizure lasts longer than 5 minutes, if another seizure starts soon after the first, if the person is injured, has difficulty breathing after the seizure, is pregnant, or if it's their first seizure.
  • Post-Seizure Care: After the seizure, stay with the person until they are fully aware and oriented, and offer reassurance.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Accurate Diagnosis: Diagnosis involves a detailed medical history, neurological examination, electroencephalogram (EEG) to detect abnormal brain activity, and often brain imaging (MRI) to identify structural causes.
  • Individualized Treatment: Treatment is highly individualized, primarily involving antiepileptic medications chosen based on seizure type, patient age, comorbidities, and potential side effects.
  • Treatment Goals: The primary goal is to achieve seizure freedom with minimal side effects, thereby improving the patient's quality of life and preventing complications.
  • Non-Pharmacological Options: For individuals whose seizures are not controlled by medication, options like vagus nerve stimulation (VNS), responsive neurostimulation (RNS), deep brain stimulation (DBS), or epilepsy surgery may be considered.
  • Comorbidity Management: Addressing common comorbidities such as depression, anxiety, and cognitive issues is crucial for holistic patient care.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Comprehensive Assessment: Conduct a thorough nursing assessment including detailed seizure history (onset, duration, characteristics, triggers, postictal state), medication adherence, and psychosocial impact.
  • Patient Education: Educate patients and families on medication regimen, potential side effects, seizure first aid, trigger identification, safety precautions, and lifestyle modifications.
  • Monitoring & Documentation: Observe and accurately document seizure events, including pre-seizure symptoms, seizure characteristics, duration, and postictal recovery.
  • Care Plan Development: Collaborate with the healthcare team to develop individualized care plans focusing on seizure control, safety, medication management, and psychosocial support.
  • Emergency Preparedness: Ensure patients and caregivers understand when to seek emergency medical attention for prolonged or serial seizures.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • ICD-10 Codes: G40.0-G40.9 (Epilepsy and recurrent epileptic seizures, various types).
  • Diagnostic Modalities: Electroencephalogram (EEG), Video-EEG monitoring, Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) of the brain, Positron Emission Tomography (PET), Single-Photon Emission Computed Tomography (SPECT).
  • Common Antiepileptic Drugs (AEDs): Levetiracetam, Lamotrigine, Valproate, Carbamazepine, Phenytoin, Topiramate, Gabapentin, Oxcarbazepine.
  • Status Epilepticus Management: Acute treatment typically involves intravenous benzodiazepines (e.g., lorazepam, midazolam) followed by non-benzodiazepine AEDs (e.g., fosphenytoin, levetiracetam, valproate).
  • Epilepsy Syndromes: Examples include Juvenile Myoclonic Epilepsy, Lennox-Gastaut Syndrome, Temporal Lobe Epilepsy.
