Meningitis in children

🏠 Home / Brain and nerves / Meningitis

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Definition: Meningitis is an inflammation of the meninges, the protective membranes surrounding the brain and spinal cord.
  • Types: It can be caused by bacteria (bacterial meningitis) or viruses (viral meningitis), with bacterial forms being more severe and potentially life-threatening.
  • Vulnerability in Children: Infants and young children are particularly susceptible due to their developing immune systems.
  • Transmission: Often spread through respiratory droplets from close contact with an infected person.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Fever: High fever, often sudden onset.
  • Irritability & Lethargy: Unusual fussiness, excessive sleepiness, or difficulty waking up.
  • Poor Feeding: Refusal to eat or drink, or decreased appetite in infants and young children.
  • Stiff Neck: Difficulty or pain when moving the head, more common in older children.
  • Headache: Severe headache, especially in older children who can verbalize it.
  • Bulging Fontanelle: A soft spot on an infant's head that bulges outwards, indicating increased intracranial pressure.
  • Seizures: Convulsions or uncontrolled body movements.
  • Rash: A non-blanching rash (petechiae or purpura) can indicate meningococcal meningitis, a medical emergency.
  • Photophobia: Sensitivity to light.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Vaccination: Ensure children receive recommended vaccinations, including Hib, pneumococcal conjugate (PCV), meningococcal ACWY, and meningococcal B vaccines, as per national guidelines.
  • Hand Hygiene: Practice frequent and thorough handwashing, especially after coughing, sneezing, or using the restroom.
  • Avoid Close Contact: Limit exposure to individuals who are sick, particularly those with respiratory infections.
  • Respiratory Etiquette: Teach children to cover their mouth and nose when coughing or sneezing.
  • Early Medical Attention: Seek immediate medical evaluation for any suspected symptoms of meningitis in a child.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Hydration Management: Ensure adequate fluid intake to prevent dehydration, offering small, frequent sips of water, oral rehydration solutions, or breast milk/formula.
  • Feeding Support: Provide easily digestible, soft foods if the child is able to tolerate oral intake; avoid forcing food.
  • Intravenous Fluids: Be prepared for the possibility of intravenous fluid administration if oral intake is insufficient or contraindicated due to severe illness.
  • Monitoring Intake: Closely monitor fluid and food intake and output, reporting any significant decrease to the medical team.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Medical Emergency: Meningitis, especially bacterial, is a medical emergency requiring immediate hospitalization and treatment.
  • Rapid Progression: Symptoms can worsen rapidly; do not delay seeking medical care.
  • Isolation Precautions: Be aware that children with suspected or confirmed bacterial meningitis may require droplet isolation precautions in a healthcare setting.
  • Post-Exposure Prophylaxis: Close contacts of individuals with certain types of bacterial meningitis (e.g., meningococcal) may require prophylactic antibiotics.
  • Neurological Monitoring: Continuous monitoring for changes in neurological status, vital signs, and seizure activity is critical.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Prompt Diagnosis: Early diagnosis through lumbar puncture and CSF analysis is paramount for effective treatment.
  • Empiric Antibiotics: Initiate empiric broad-spectrum antibiotics immediately upon suspicion of bacterial meningitis, even before definitive culture results are available.
  • Supportive Care: Provide aggressive supportive care, including fluid management, fever control, seizure management, and respiratory support as needed.
  • Corticosteroids: Consider adjunctive corticosteroids (e.g., dexamethasone) in specific cases of bacterial meningitis to reduce inflammatory complications.
  • Long-Term Follow-up: Emphasize the importance of follow-up care to monitor for potential long-term complications such as hearing loss, developmental delays, or neurological deficits.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Neurological Assessment: Perform frequent neurological assessments, including level of consciousness, pupillary response, motor function, and fontanelle assessment in infants.
  • Vital Sign Monitoring: Closely monitor vital signs, including temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate, and blood pressure, for signs of deterioration or shock.
  • Fluid & Electrolyte Balance: Monitor intake and output, urine specific gravity, and serum electrolytes to prevent fluid overload or dehydration.
  • Medication Administration: Administer prescribed antibiotics or antiviral medications on time and monitor for adverse effects.
  • Infection Control: Implement strict infection control measures, including appropriate isolation precautions, hand hygiene, and safe handling of body fluids.
  • Parental Support: Provide emotional support and clear education to parents regarding the child's condition, treatment plan, and potential outcomes.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Etiology: Common bacterial causes include Streptococcus pneumoniae, Neisseria meningitidis, and Haemophilus influenzae type b (Hib). Viral causes often include enteroviruses.
  • Diagnosis: Confirmed by lumbar puncture with cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis (cell count, protein, glucose, Gram stain, culture), blood cultures, and PCR testing.
  • Complications: Potential complications include hearing loss, seizures, hydrocephalus, developmental delay, cerebral palsy, and even death.
  • Treatment: Bacterial meningitis is treated with intravenous antibiotics; viral meningitis is typically supportive care, though antivirals may be used for specific viral etiologies (e.g., HSV).
  • Prognosis: Varies significantly based on the causative agent, age of the child, and timeliness of treatment. Bacterial meningitis carries higher morbidity and mortality.
