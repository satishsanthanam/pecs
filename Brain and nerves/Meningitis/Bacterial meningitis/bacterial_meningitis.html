Bacterial meningitis

🏠 Home / Brain and nerves / Meningitis

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Serious Infection: Bacterial meningitis is an acute inflammation of the protective membranes surrounding the brain and spinal cord (meninges).
  • Medical Emergency: It is a life-threatening condition requiring immediate medical attention and prompt antibiotic treatment.
  • Common Causes: Caused by various bacteria, including *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, *Neisseria meningitidis*, and *Haemophilus influenzae* type b.
  • Transmission: Spreads through respiratory droplets and close contact with an infected person.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Classic Triad: Characterized by sudden onset of fever, severe headache, and nuchal rigidity (stiff neck).
  • Neurological Changes: May include altered mental status, confusion, irritability, and difficulty concentrating.
  • Other Manifestations: Photophobia (sensitivity to light), nausea, vomiting, and seizures can occur.
  • Specific Signs: A non-blanching rash (petechiae or purpura) is a hallmark of meningococcal meningitis.
  • Infant Symptoms: In infants, symptoms may be non-specific, including poor feeding, lethargy, irritability, and a bulging fontanelle.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Vaccination: Ensure up-to-date vaccinations against *Haemophilus influenzae* type b (Hib), pneumococcal disease, and meningococcal disease.
  • Hygiene Practices: Practice frequent handwashing, especially after coughing, sneezing, or before eating.
  • Avoid Close Contact: Minimize close contact with individuals who are sick, particularly those with respiratory symptoms.
  • Prophylaxis: Close contacts of individuals with certain types of bacterial meningitis (e.g., meningococcal) may require antibiotic prophylaxis.
  • Early Intervention: Seek immediate medical evaluation for any suspected symptoms to prevent severe complications.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Hydration Focus: Maintain adequate fluid intake, often requiring intravenous fluids during the acute phase due to fever, vomiting, and altered consciousness.
  • Easily Digestible Foods: Offer soft, bland, and easily digestible foods as tolerated once nausea subsides and consciousness improves.
  • Nutritional Support: In severe or prolonged cases, enteral or parenteral nutrition may be necessary to meet caloric and nutritional requirements.
  • Avoid Irritants: During recovery, avoid foods that may exacerbate nausea or gastrointestinal discomfort.
  • Gradual Reintroduction: Gradually reintroduce a balanced diet as the patient's condition stabilizes and appetite returns.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Isolation Precautions: Implement droplet precautions for suspected or confirmed cases, especially for meningococcal meningitis, to prevent transmission.
  • Neurological Monitoring: Perform frequent neurological assessments, including level of consciousness, pupillary response, and motor function.
  • Vital Sign Surveillance: Closely monitor vital signs, including temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate, and blood pressure, for signs of deterioration or complications.
  • Medication Adherence: Ensure timely and accurate administration of prescribed antibiotics and adjunctive therapies (e.g., corticosteroids).
  • Family Education: Educate family members about the disease, its transmission, and the importance of post-exposure prophylaxis for eligible contacts.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Prompt Diagnosis: Lumbar puncture for cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis is crucial for definitive diagnosis and identification of the causative pathogen.
  • Empiric Antibiotics: Initiate broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics immediately upon suspicion, prior to culture results, to improve outcomes.
  • Adjunctive Therapy: Consider adjunctive dexamethasone in specific cases (e.g., pneumococcal meningitis) to reduce inflammation and neurological sequelae.
  • Complication Management: Be vigilant for complications such as cerebral edema, hydrocephalus, seizures, hearing loss, and septic shock.
  • Long-term Follow-up: Arrange for neurological and audiological follow-up to assess for and manage potential long-term deficits.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Assessment Skills: Master the assessment of meningeal signs (e.g., Brudzinski's sign, Kernig's sign) and neurological status.
  • Fluid Balance Management: Accurately monitor intake and output, and assess for signs of dehydration or fluid overload, especially with SIADH risk.
  • Medication Administration: Understand the pharmacokinetics and potential side effects of common antibiotics used in meningitis treatment.
  • Infection Control: Adhere strictly to infection control protocols, including proper donning and doffing of personal protective equipment for droplet precautions.
  • Patient and Family Support: Provide emotional support and clear, concise information to patients and their families regarding the illness and treatment plan.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Key Pathogens: *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, *Neisseria meningitidis*, *Haemophilus influenzae* type b, Group B *Streptococcus* (neonates), *Listeria monocytogenes* (immunocompromised, elderly).
  • Diagnostic Modalities: CSF analysis (cell count, protein, glucose, Gram stain, culture, PCR), blood cultures, neuroimaging (CT/MRI) to rule out other pathologies or complications.
  • First-Line Treatment: Empiric antibiotics typically include a third-generation cephalosporin (e.g., ceftriaxone) often combined with vancomycin; ampicillin added for *Listeria* risk.
  • Potential Complications: Cerebral edema, hydrocephalus, seizures, hearing loss, cranial nerve palsies, intellectual disability, septic shock, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC).
  • Prevention Strategies: Routine childhood immunizations (PCV, Hib, MenACWY, MenB), chemoprophylaxis for close contacts.
