Glioblastoma

🏠 Home / Brain and nerves / Brain tumors

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Aggressive Brain Tumor: Glioblastoma (GBM) is the most common and aggressive primary malignant brain tumor in adults, originating from astrocytes.
  • Rapid Progression: Characterized by rapid, invasive growth and significant resistance to conventional therapies.
  • Infiltrative Nature: GBM cells often spread into surrounding brain tissue, making complete surgical removal challenging.
  • Prognosis: Despite aggressive treatment, the prognosis for glioblastoma remains poor, emphasizing the need for ongoing research and novel therapies.
  • Location: Can occur in any lobe of the brain, but most commonly found in the cerebral hemispheres.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Persistent Headaches: Often severe, worsening over time, and not relieved by typical pain medication, sometimes worse in the morning.
  • New-Onset Seizures: Can manifest as focal (affecting one part of the body) or generalized (affecting the whole body) seizures.
  • Progressive Neurological Deficits: Including weakness or numbness on one side of the body, difficulty with balance, or coordination issues.
  • Cognitive and Personality Changes: Memory loss, confusion, difficulty concentrating, changes in mood, or altered personality.
  • Speech and Language Difficulties: Aphasia (trouble speaking or understanding language) or dysarthria (slurred speech).
  • Vision Problems: Blurred vision, double vision, or loss of peripheral vision due to pressure on optic pathways.
  • Nausea and Vomiting: Often indicative of increased intracranial pressure, especially when accompanied by headaches.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Symptom Monitoring: Vigilant observation for new or worsening neurological symptoms is critical for early intervention and management.
  • Medication Adherence: Strict compliance with prescribed anticonvulsants, corticosteroids, and chemotherapy regimens to manage symptoms and disease progression.
  • Fall Prevention: Implement safety measures in the home environment for patients experiencing motor weakness, balance issues, or seizures.
  • Driving Restrictions: Patients with seizures, significant cognitive impairment, or visual field defects should refrain from driving to ensure public safety.
  • Cognitive Support: Provide a structured, predictable environment and use memory aids for patients experiencing cognitive decline.
  • Infection Control: Post-surgical patients require meticulous wound care and monitoring for signs of infection, especially in immunocompromised states.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Dysphagia Management: For patients with swallowing difficulties, modify food textures (e.g., pureed, soft) and liquid consistency (e.g., thickened fluids) to prevent aspiration.
  • Steroid-Induced Appetite Changes: Monitor for increased appetite and potential weight gain due to corticosteroid use; encourage balanced, portion-controlled meals.
  • Nausea and Vomiting Control: Offer small, frequent, bland meals; avoid strong-smelling or greasy foods, especially during chemotherapy or radiation.
  • Hydration Status: Ensure adequate fluid intake, unless medically contraindicated, to prevent dehydration, particularly during treatment.
  • Nutritional Support: Consider nutritional supplements or enteral feeding if oral intake is insufficient to meet caloric and protein needs, to maintain strength and immune function.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Caregiver Education: Provide comprehensive education to caregivers on symptom recognition, medication schedules, and emergency protocols for seizures or acute neurological changes.
  • Emotional and Psychological Support: Offer resources for psychological counseling, support groups, and palliative care services for both patients and their families.
  • Home Safety Modifications: Assess and adapt the home environment to enhance safety and accessibility, such as removing trip hazards and installing grab bars.
  • Communication Strategies: Develop effective communication techniques for patients with aphasia or cognitive deficits, using visual aids or simplified language.
  • Advance Care Planning: Facilitate discussions about advance directives, end-of-life wishes, and goals of care with the patient and family early in the disease course.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Multidisciplinary Approach: Glioblastoma management necessitates a collaborative team including neurosurgeons, radiation oncologists, medical oncologists, neurologists, and palliative care specialists.
  • Standard of Care: Typically involves maximal safe surgical resection, followed by concurrent radiation therapy and temozolomide chemotherapy, then adjuvant temozolomide.
  • Clinical Trials: Patients should be evaluated for eligibility in clinical trials exploring novel therapies, given the aggressive nature and limited treatment options for GBM.
  • Symptom Management: Aggressive management of symptoms such as seizures, cerebral edema, and pain is paramount to optimize patient quality of life.
  • Prognosis Discussion: Open and empathetic communication regarding the disease prognosis and realistic treatment expectations is crucial for shared decision-making.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Neurological Assessments: Perform frequent and thorough neurological assessments, including Glasgow Coma Scale, cranial nerve function, and motor/sensory evaluations, to detect subtle changes.
  • Medication Management: Administer complex medication regimens, including chemotherapy, corticosteroids, and anticonvulsants, with a deep understanding of their indications, side effects, and interactions.
  • Patient and Family Education: Educate on disease progression, treatment side effects, self-care strategies, and when to seek urgent medical attention.
  • Post-Operative Care: Provide meticulous post-neurosurgical care, including monitoring for intracranial pressure changes, CSF leaks, and infection at the surgical site.
  • Psychosocial Support: Offer empathetic support and connect patients and families with appropriate psychosocial resources, including social workers, chaplains, and support groups.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Histopathology: Characterized by high cellularity, nuclear pleomorphism, mitotic activity, microvascular proliferation, and necrosis, defining its WHO Grade IV status.
  • Molecular Markers: Key markers include IDH mutation status (IDH-wildtype is more common and aggressive) and MGMT promoter methylation (predicts response to temozolomide).
  • Imaging Characteristics: MRI with contrast typically reveals an irregularly enhancing ring lesion with central necrosis and surrounding vasogenic edema.
  • Treatment Modalities: Primary treatments include surgery, radiation therapy, chemotherapy (temozolomide), and increasingly, tumor treating fields (TTFields).
  • Recurrence Patterns: Despite aggressive treatment, recurrence is common, often at the site of the original tumor or in adjacent brain tissue.
