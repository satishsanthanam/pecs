Head injury in adults

🏠 Home / Brain and nerves / Head injury

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Definition: A head injury refers to any trauma to the scalp, skull, or brain, ranging from minor bumps to severe brain damage.
  • Severity Spectrum: Injuries can be mild (e.g., concussion), moderate, or severe, with varying impacts on brain function and recovery.
  • Immediate Evaluation: All head injuries, especially those involving loss of consciousness, confusion, or persistent symptoms, require prompt medical assessment.
  • Potential for Delayed Symptoms: Symptoms may not appear immediately and can worsen over hours or days, necessitating continuous monitoring.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Mild Injury Symptoms: Headache, dizziness, confusion, nausea, temporary memory loss, sensitivity to light or sound.
  • Moderate to Severe Symptoms: Persistent or worsening headache, repeated vomiting, slurred speech, weakness or numbness in limbs, loss of coordination, seizures, dilated or unequal pupils.
  • Altered Consciousness: Drowsiness, difficulty waking up, loss of consciousness (even brief), or changes in behavior or personality.
  • Fluid Leakage: Clear fluid (CSF) or blood draining from the ears or nose, indicating a potential skull fracture.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Do Not Move: If a spinal injury is suspected (e.g., from a fall or accident), do not move the person; stabilize the head and neck until medical help arrives.
  • Avoid Certain Substances: Post-injury, avoid alcohol, recreational drugs, and certain medications (e.g., blood thinners, unless prescribed) without medical advice.
  • Monitor Closely: A responsible adult should monitor the injured person for at least 24-48 hours for any worsening symptoms.
  • Gradual Return to Activity: Follow medical advice for a gradual return to work, school, or physical activities to prevent re-injury and aid recovery.
  • Preventative Measures: Wear helmets during sports or cycling, use seatbelts in vehicles, and ensure home safety to prevent falls, especially in older adults.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Hydration: Maintain adequate fluid intake, primarily water, to support brain function and overall recovery.
  • Light, Bland Meals: If experiencing nausea or vomiting, opt for small, frequent, bland meals (e.g., toast, crackers, clear broths) to prevent dehydration and provide energy.
  • Avoid Stimulants: Limit or avoid caffeine and sugary drinks, which can exacerbate headaches or disrupt sleep patterns during recovery.
  • Alcohol Restriction: Strictly avoid alcohol, as it can impair cognitive function, mask symptoms, and interfere with healing.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Emergency Contact: Know when to call emergency services (e.g., severe headache, repeated vomiting, seizures, difficulty waking, changes in pupil size).
  • Observation Period: Keep the injured person under continuous observation for at least 24 hours, checking on them frequently, even if they are sleeping.
  • Quiet Environment: Provide a calm, quiet environment with minimal stimulation (e.g., bright lights, loud noises, screens) to aid brain rest.
  • Medication Management: Administer prescribed pain relief as directed, avoiding over-the-counter medications that may thin blood (e.g., aspirin) unless approved by a doctor.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Thorough Assessment: A comprehensive neurological examination is crucial to determine the extent and type of head injury.
  • Imaging Studies: CT scans or MRI may be ordered to identify skull fractures, bleeding, swelling, or other brain damage.
  • Individualized Management: Treatment plans are tailored to the injury's severity, ranging from rest and symptom management for concussions to surgical intervention for severe hematomas.
  • Rehabilitation: For moderate to severe injuries, rehabilitation services (e.g., physical, occupational, speech therapy) are vital for optimal recovery.
  • Long-Term Monitoring: Patients should be advised on potential long-term effects, such as post-concussion syndrome, and the importance of follow-up care.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Neurological Assessment: Proficiently perform and document serial neurological assessments, including Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), pupil reaction, and motor function.
  • Vital Signs Monitoring: Closely monitor and trend vital signs, recognizing signs of increased intracranial pressure (ICP) such as Cushing's triad.
  • Patient Education: Educate patients and caregivers on post-concussion symptoms, 'red flag' signs requiring immediate medical attention, and activity restrictions.
  • Pain Management: Implement effective pain management strategies while being mindful of potential masking of neurological changes.
  • Interdisciplinary Collaboration: Collaborate with physicians, therapists, and social workers to ensure holistic patient care and discharge planning.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Types of Injury: Concussion (mild traumatic brain injury), Contusion, Epidural Hematoma, Subdural Hematoma, Subarachnoid Hemorrhage, Diffuse Axonal Injury.
  • Diagnostic Modalities: Computed Tomography (CT) scan, Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI), X-ray (for skull fractures).
  • Assessment Tools: Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), Post-Concussion Symptom Scale (PCSS), SCAT5 (Sport Concussion Assessment Tool).
  • Management Principles: Intracranial Pressure (ICP) monitoring, Craniotomy, Decompressive Craniectomy, Neuroprotection strategies.
  • Complications: Post-concussion syndrome (PCS), Epilepsy, Cognitive deficits, Chronic Traumatic Encephalopathy (CTE).
