Concussion in adults

🏠 Home / Brain and nerves / Head injury

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Definition: A concussion is a mild traumatic brain injury (mTBI) caused by a direct blow to the head or an indirect force that causes the brain to move rapidly within the skull.
  • Nature of Injury: It results in temporary disruption of normal brain function, affecting cognitive, physical, and emotional processes.
  • Causes: Common causes include falls, sports injuries, motor vehicle accidents, and assaults.
  • Recovery: While most concussions resolve within days to weeks, some individuals may experience prolonged symptoms, known as post-concussion syndrome.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Headache: Often described as pressure or throbbing, which may worsen with activity.
  • Dizziness & Vertigo: Feelings of lightheadedness, unsteadiness, or a spinning sensation.
  • Nausea & Vomiting: Especially common in the initial hours or days post-injury.
  • Confusion & Disorientation: Difficulty thinking clearly, feeling foggy, or being slow to respond.
  • Memory Problems: Trouble remembering events before or after the injury.
  • Sensitivity: Increased sensitivity to light (photophobia) and sound (phonophobia).
  • Mood Changes: Irritability, anxiety, sadness, or emotional lability.
  • Sleep Disturbances: Difficulty falling asleep, sleeping more than usual, or restless sleep.
  • Fatigue: Persistent tiredness and lack of energy.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Immediate Medical Evaluation: Seek prompt medical attention after any head injury, even if symptoms seem mild, to rule out more serious conditions.
  • Avoid Re-injury: Refrain from activities that put you at risk for another head impact until cleared by a healthcare professional, as a second concussion before full recovery can be severe (Second Impact Syndrome).
  • Cognitive & Physical Rest: Limit activities that require significant brain effort (e.g., reading, screen time, complex tasks) and physical exertion during the initial recovery phase.
  • Medication Management: Avoid aspirin, NSAIDs, and alcohol in the acute phase, as they can increase bleeding risk or worsen symptoms; consult a doctor for pain relief.
  • Monitor for Worsening Symptoms: Be vigilant for red flag symptoms such as severe headache, seizures, slurred speech, weakness, or persistent vomiting, and seek emergency care immediately.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Hydration: Maintain adequate fluid intake, primarily water, to support brain function and prevent dehydration, which can exacerbate headaches.
  • Avoid Alcohol & Caffeine: Restrict alcohol consumption as it can impair cognitive function and delay recovery; limit caffeine, which can interfere with sleep and increase headache severity.
  • Nutrient-Rich Foods: Focus on a balanced diet rich in omega-3 fatty acids (e.g., fish, flaxseed), antioxidants (e.g., berries, leafy greens), and lean proteins to support brain healing and reduce inflammation.
  • Small, Frequent Meals: If experiencing nausea or appetite changes, opt for smaller, more frequent meals of bland, easily digestible foods.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Do Not Leave Alone: An adult with a suspected concussion should not be left alone for the first 24-48 hours to monitor for any worsening symptoms.
  • Regular Checks: Periodically wake the individual during sleep to check for responsiveness and cognitive status, especially in the first night.
  • Emergency Signs: Be aware of and immediately report any signs of neurological deterioration, such as increasing confusion, severe headache, repeated vomiting, seizures, or difficulty walking.
  • Quiet Environment: Ensure the individual rests in a quiet, dimly lit environment to minimize sensory stimulation that can worsen symptoms.
  • Medication Administration: Assist with medication as prescribed and ensure adherence to rest protocols.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Early Diagnosis & Management: Prompt and accurate diagnosis is crucial for initiating appropriate management and preventing prolonged recovery.
  • Individualized Recovery Plan: Treatment involves a tailored approach focusing on cognitive and physical rest, followed by a gradual return to activity (GRTA) or return to sport (GRTS) protocol.
  • Symptom Management: Address specific symptoms such as headaches, dizziness, and sleep disturbances with targeted interventions, including medication or therapy.
  • Referral Considerations: Consider referrals to specialists (e.g., neurology, neuropsychology, physical therapy) for persistent symptoms or complex cases.
  • Patient Education: Educate patients and their families about concussion symptoms, recovery expectations, and red flag signs requiring immediate medical attention.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Assessment Tools: Utilize standardized assessment tools like the SCAT5 (Sport Concussion Assessment Tool – 5th Edition) or GCS (Glasgow Coma Scale) for initial evaluation and ongoing monitoring.
  • Neurological Monitoring: Perform frequent neurological assessments, including vital signs, pupillary response, and level of consciousness, especially in the acute phase.
  • Patient Education: Provide comprehensive education on concussion symptoms, the importance of rest, gradual return to activities, and when to seek further medical care.
  • Symptom Management: Implement nursing interventions to manage symptoms such as pain, nausea, and sleep disturbances, and create a supportive environment for recovery.
  • Interdisciplinary Collaboration: Work collaboratively with physicians, physical therapists, occupational therapists, and neuropsychologists to ensure holistic patient care.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Pathophysiology: Involves a complex neurometabolic cascade following biomechanical forces, leading to temporary neuronal dysfunction, energy crisis, and altered neurotransmission.
  • Diagnostic Criteria: Primarily clinical, based on a history of head trauma and the presence of acute neurological symptoms, often without structural abnormalities on conventional imaging (CT/MRI).
  • ICD-10 Codes: Common codes include S06.0X0A (Concussion with no loss of consciousness, initial encounter) or S06.0X1A (Concussion with brief loss of consciousness, initial encounter).
  • Prognosis & Risk Factors: Most adults recover within 2-4 weeks; risk factors for prolonged recovery include prior concussions, pre-existing neurological or psychiatric conditions, and severe initial symptoms.
  • Post-Concussion Syndrome (PCS): Diagnosis for symptoms persisting beyond the expected recovery period (typically >3 months), characterized by a constellation of physical, cognitive, and emotional symptoms.
