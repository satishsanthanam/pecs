Guillain-Barré syndrome

🏠 Home / Brain and nerves / Neuromuscular disorders

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Acute Autoimmune Disorder: Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS) is a rare, rapid-onset autoimmune disorder where the body's immune system attacks its own peripheral nerves.
  • Demyelination and Axonal Damage: This attack typically damages the myelin sheath, which insulates nerve fibers, or sometimes the nerve axons themselves, leading to impaired signal transmission.
  • Ascending Weakness: Characterized by rapidly progressive muscle weakness, often starting in the legs and ascending to the arms and facial muscles.
  • Infection Trigger: Frequently preceded by an infection, such as a respiratory illness, gastrointestinal infection (e.g., Campylobacter jejuni), or viral infections like Zika or influenza.
  • Medical Emergency: GBS can be life-threatening, especially if respiratory muscles are affected, requiring immediate medical attention and often intensive care.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Initial Paresthesias: Tingling, numbness, or prickling sensations (paresthesias) often begin in the fingers, toes, ankles, or wrists.
  • Progressive Muscle Weakness: Weakness typically starts in the lower limbs and progresses upwards, affecting both sides of the body.
  • Difficulty Walking and Standing: Severe weakness can lead to an inability to walk or stand independently.
  • Facial and Bulbar Weakness: Weakness can affect facial muscles, causing difficulty with speaking, chewing, swallowing (dysphagia), and facial expressions.
  • Respiratory Distress: Weakness of the diaphragm and intercostal muscles can lead to severe breathing difficulties, necessitating mechanical ventilation.
  • Autonomic Dysfunction: Fluctuations in blood pressure, abnormal heart rhythms, bowel and bladder dysfunction, and excessive sweating can occur.
  • Severe Pain: Neuropathic pain, muscle aches, and cramping are common and can be debilitating.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Respiratory Monitoring: Continuous assessment of respiratory function, including vital capacity and oxygen saturation, is paramount due to the risk of respiratory failure.
  • Aspiration Prevention: Meticulous attention to swallowing safety, including thickened liquids or enteral feeding, to prevent aspiration pneumonia.
  • Deep Vein Thrombosis (DVT) Prophylaxis: Implement DVT prevention strategies, such as anticoagulants and sequential compression devices, due to prolonged immobility.
  • Skin Integrity: Regular repositioning, pressure-relieving mattresses, and meticulous skin care are essential to prevent pressure ulcers.
  • Autonomic Instability Management: Close monitoring of vital signs for blood pressure fluctuations, cardiac arrhythmias, and other signs of autonomic dysregulation.
  • Fall Prevention: Implement strict fall precautions for patients with any degree of motor weakness, even during transfers.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Dysphagia Assessment: Thorough swallowing evaluations are critical to determine the appropriate diet consistency (e.g., pureed foods, thickened liquids) or need for non-oral feeding.
  • Nutritional Support: If oral intake is unsafe or insufficient, initiate enteral (nasogastric, gastrostomy tube) or parenteral nutrition to maintain adequate caloric and protein intake.
  • Hydration Management: Ensure adequate hydration, either orally with thickened liquids or via intravenous fluids/feeding tubes, especially with autonomic dysfunction.
  • Bowel Regularity: Implement a bowel regimen, including high-fiber options (if tolerated orally) and stool softeners, to prevent constipation associated with immobility and medications.
  • Electrolyte Balance: Monitor and manage electrolyte levels, particularly with significant fluid shifts or autonomic instability.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Recognize Rapid Deterioration: Attendants must be vigilant for any sudden worsening of weakness, difficulty breathing, or changes in consciousness and seek immediate medical help.
  • Assist with Mobility: Provide comprehensive assistance with all transfers, ambulation, and repositioning to ensure patient safety and prevent injury.
  • Communication Support: Facilitate communication for patients with speech difficulties, using whiteboards, communication boards, or assistive devices.
  • Emotional Support: Offer psychological support and reassurance, as the sudden onset of paralysis can be highly distressing for patients and their families.
  • Educate on Rehabilitation: Understand that recovery is a long process and support adherence to physical, occupational, and speech therapy regimens.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Early Diagnosis and Treatment: Prompt recognition of GBS symptoms and initiation of immunomodulatory therapies (IVIg or plasma exchange) are crucial to mitigate disease progression and improve outcomes.
  • Multidisciplinary Care: A collaborative approach involving neurologists, intensivists, pulmonologists, physical therapists, occupational therapists, and speech-language pathologists is essential for comprehensive management.
  • Vigilant Monitoring: Close observation for respiratory compromise, autonomic instability, and other complications is critical, often requiring intensive care unit admission.
  • Prognosis and Rehabilitation: Discuss realistic expectations regarding recovery, which can be prolonged and may involve residual weakness; emphasize the importance of intensive rehabilitation.
  • Long-term Follow-up: Regular neurological follow-up is necessary to monitor for recovery, manage persistent symptoms, and address potential long-term complications.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Comprehensive Neurological Assessments: Perform frequent and detailed neurological assessments, including motor strength (using a standardized scale), sensory function, cranial nerves, and deep tendon reflexes.
  • Respiratory Management: Monitor respiratory rate, depth, effort, and oxygen saturation; be prepared for intubation and mechanical ventilation, and provide meticulous ventilator care.
  • Pain Management Strategies: Assess and manage neuropathic and musculoskeletal pain effectively using pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions.
  • Mobility and Positioning: Implement passive range of motion exercises, assist with repositioning every two hours, and ensure proper body alignment to prevent contractures and pressure injuries.
  • Patient and Family Education: Educate patients and families about the disease process, treatment plan, potential complications, and the importance of rehabilitation.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Pathophysiology: Autoimmune response targeting gangliosides (e.g., GM1, GD1b) on peripheral nerve myelin or axons, leading to demyelination or axonal degeneration.
  • Diagnostic Criteria: Clinical presentation of progressive, symmetrical weakness, areflexia, and exclusion of other causes; supported by CSF analysis (albuminocytologic dissociation) and nerve conduction studies (NCS)/electromyography (EMG).
  • Treatment Modalities: Intravenous Immunoglobulin (IVIg) or Plasma Exchange (PLEX) are the primary treatments, aimed at reducing the autoimmune attack. Corticosteroids are generally not effective.
  • Variants: Includes Acute Inflammatory Demyelinating Polyneuropathy (AIDP, most common), Miller Fisher Syndrome (MFS, ophthalmoplegia, ataxia, areflexia), Acute Motor Axonal Neuropathy (AMAN), and Acute Motor Sensory Axonal Neuropathy (AMSAN).
  • Complications: Respiratory failure, autonomic dysregulation (cardiac arrhythmias, labile blood pressure), deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, pressure ulcers, persistent weakness, and chronic pain.
