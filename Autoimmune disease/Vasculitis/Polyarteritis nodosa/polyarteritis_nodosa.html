Polyarteritis nodosa

🏠 Home / Autoimmune disease / Vasculitis

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Systemic Vasculitis: Polyarteritis nodosa (PAN) is a rare, severe form of necrotizing vasculitis primarily affecting medium-sized muscular arteries.
  • Organ Involvement: It can damage multiple organ systems, including the kidneys, gastrointestinal tract, peripheral nerves, skin, and heart. The lungs are typically spared.
  • Pathology: Characterized by segmental inflammation and necrosis of arterial walls, leading to microaneurysms, thrombosis, and subsequent ischemia or infarction of affected tissues.
  • Etiology: The cause is often unknown (idiopathic), but a significant subset of cases is associated with chronic Hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection.
  • Prognosis: Untreated PAN carries a high mortality rate, but with timely diagnosis and aggressive immunosuppressive therapy, remission is often achievable.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Constitutional Symptoms: Persistent fever, profound fatigue, unexplained weight loss, and general malaise are common initial presentations.
  • Renal Manifestations: New-onset or worsening hypertension (due to renal artery involvement), impaired kidney function, proteinuria, and hematuria.
  • Gastrointestinal Issues: Severe abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, gastrointestinal bleeding, and potentially bowel infarction.
  • Neurological Symptoms: Mononeuritis multiplex (foot drop, wrist drop), other peripheral neuropathies, and less commonly, central nervous system events like stroke or seizures.
  • Dermatological Signs: Livedo reticularis (mottled skin pattern), subcutaneous nodules, skin ulcers, purpura, and digital gangrene.
  • Musculoskeletal Pain: Generalized myalgia (muscle pain) and arthralgia (joint pain) without significant arthritis.
  • Cardiovascular Complications: Angina, myocardial infarction, or heart failure dueating to coronary artery involvement.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Prompt Diagnosis: Early recognition and diagnosis are critical to prevent irreversible organ damage and improve patient outcomes.
  • Tissue Biopsy: A definitive diagnosis often requires a biopsy of an affected organ (e.g., nerve, muscle, skin, kidney) to demonstrate characteristic vasculitic changes.
  • Hepatitis B Screening: All patients suspected of PAN must be screened for Hepatitis B virus infection, as its presence dictates specific treatment strategies.
  • Immunosuppression Management: Close monitoring for side effects of corticosteroids and other immunosuppressants (e.g., cyclophosphamide), including infection risk and bone density loss.
  • Blood Pressure Control: Aggressive management of hypertension is vital to protect renal function and prevent cardiovascular complications.
  • Avoid NSAIDs: Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs should generally be avoided, especially in patients with renal involvement, as they can worsen kidney function.
  • Regular Monitoring: Consistent follow-up with blood tests (ESR, CRP, creatinine, liver function) and imaging to assess disease activity and treatment response.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Corticosteroid-Related Adjustments: Patients on corticosteroids may need a low-sodium diet to manage fluid retention and hypertension, and increased intake of potassium-rich foods to counteract potassium depletion.
  • Bone Health Support: Adequate calcium and Vitamin D intake, often through supplementation, is crucial to mitigate the risk of osteoporosis induced by long-term corticosteroid use.
  • Blood Glucose Management: Dietary modifications to help control blood sugar levels may be necessary for patients experiencing steroid-induced hyperglycemia.
  • Renal Impairment Considerations: If kidney function is significantly impaired, protein intake may need to be restricted, and fluid intake carefully monitored as advised by a nephrologist.
  • Gastrointestinal Symptom Relief: During periods of active gastrointestinal inflammation or ulceration, a bland, low-residue diet may be recommended to minimize irritation.
  • Hydration: Maintain adequate hydration unless fluid restriction is specifically indicated due to renal or cardiac issues.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Medication Adherence: Strict adherence to the prescribed immunosuppressive regimen is paramount for controlling disease activity and preventing relapses.
  • Report New Symptoms: Immediately report any new or worsening symptoms, such as fever, severe pain, changes in sensation, or skin lesions, to your healthcare provider.
  • Infection Prevention: Be vigilant about infection prevention, including meticulous hand hygiene and avoiding exposure to sick individuals, due to immunosuppression.
  • Vaccination Status: Discuss your vaccination schedule with your physician, as some live vaccines may be contraindicated while on immunosuppressive therapy.
  • Avoid Smoking: Smoking can exacerbate vascular damage and should be strictly avoided.
  • Regular Dental Care: Maintain good oral hygiene and regular dental check-ups to prevent infections that could be problematic with immunosuppression.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Multidisciplinary Care: Effective management of PAN often requires a collaborative approach involving rheumatologists, nephrologists, gastroenterologists, neurologists, and dermatologists.
  • Biopsy Confirmation: Emphasize the critical role of tissue biopsy for definitive diagnosis, as clinical presentation can mimic other conditions.
  • HBV-Associated PAN: For HBV-associated PAN, antiviral therapy is as crucial as immunosuppression and should be initiated promptly.
  • Individualized Treatment: Treatment protocols must be tailored to the individual patient, considering disease severity, organ involvement, and presence of HBV infection.
  • Long-term Monitoring: Stress the importance of long-term follow-up to monitor disease activity, manage treatment side effects, and detect potential relapses.
  • Patient Education: Empower patients with comprehensive knowledge about their condition, treatment plan, and potential complications to foster adherence and self-management.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Comprehensive Assessment: Conduct thorough assessments focusing on vital signs, pain levels, neurological status, skin integrity, and signs of organ ischemia or dysfunction.
  • Medication Management: Understand the indications, dosages, administration routes, and potential side effects of corticosteroids and cytotoxic agents used in PAN treatment.
  • Infection Control: Implement strict infection control measures and educate patients on strategies to minimize infection risk due to immunosuppression.
  • Fluid & Electrolyte Balance: Monitor intake and output, serum electrolytes, and signs of fluid overload or dehydration, especially in patients with renal involvement.
  • Patient Education: Provide clear, concise education on medication adherence, symptom recognition, dietary modifications, and the importance of follow-up appointments.
  • Psychosocial Support: Offer emotional support and resources to help patients cope with the challenges of a chronic, severe autoimmune disease.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Diagnostic Criteria: While no single set of criteria is universally accepted, the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) criteria for PAN are often used, though biopsy remains the gold standard.
  • Histopathology: Characterized by transmural inflammation of medium-sized arteries, fibrinoid necrosis, and often microaneurysm formation, with varying stages of inflammation and healing.
  • Angiography: Mesenteric or renal angiography may reveal characteristic microaneurysms, stenoses, and occlusions in affected arteries.
  • Laboratory Markers: Common findings include elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), C-reactive protein (CRP), leukocytosis, anemia, and elevated creatinine. Anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA) are typically negative.
  • Treatment Modalities: High-dose glucocorticoids (e.g., prednisone) are the cornerstone of therapy, often combined with cyclophosphamide for severe disease. Antiviral therapy is crucial for HBV-associated PAN.
  • Differential Diagnosis: Important to differentiate from other vasculitides (e.g., GPA, MPA, EGPA), other causes of secondary vasculitis, and conditions mimicking PAN.
