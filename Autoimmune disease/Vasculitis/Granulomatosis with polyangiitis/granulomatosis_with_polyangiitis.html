Granulomatosis with polyangiitis

🏠 Home / Autoimmune disease / Vasculitis

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Systemic Autoimmune Disease: Granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA), formerly Wegener's granulomatosis, is a rare, serious autoimmune disease causing inflammation of small and medium-sized blood vessels (vasculitis).
  • Organ Involvement: Primarily affects the upper respiratory tract (sinuses, nose, ears), lungs, and kidneys, but can impact almost any organ system, including eyes, skin, and nerves.
  • Granulomatous Inflammation: Characterized by necrotizing granulomatous inflammation, meaning destructive inflammatory lesions form in affected tissues.
  • ANCA Association: Often associated with the presence of anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), specifically proteinase 3 (PR3-ANCA).
  • Chronic and Relapsing: GPA is a chronic condition that requires long-term management and can have periods of remission and relapse.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Upper Respiratory Tract: Chronic sinusitis, nasal crusting, epistaxis (nosebleeds), saddle nose deformity, ear pain, hearing loss, and persistent cough.
  • Pulmonary Manifestations: Cough, shortness of breath (dyspnea), hemoptysis (coughing up blood), pleuritic chest pain, and pulmonary infiltrates visible on imaging.
  • Renal Involvement: Hematuria (blood in urine), proteinuria (protein in urine), rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis, and signs of kidney failure.
  • Systemic Symptoms: Unexplained fever, profound fatigue, unintentional weight loss, night sweats, and general malaise.
  • Musculoskeletal Pain: Arthralgia (joint pain) and myalgia (muscle pain) are common.
  • Ocular Symptoms: Red eyes (scleritis, episcleritis), proptosis (bulging eyes), and vision changes.
  • Skin Lesions: Purpura, nodules, ulcers, and other skin rashes.
  • Neurological Symptoms: Peripheral neuropathy, such as mononeuritis multiplex, causing weakness, numbness, or tingling in extremities.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Early Diagnosis and Treatment: Prompt recognition and initiation of immunosuppressive therapy are critical to prevent irreversible organ damage, especially to the kidneys and lungs.
  • Infection Risk: Patients on immunosuppressants are at high risk for infections; monitor closely for fever, chills, or any signs of infection, and ensure appropriate prophylaxis (e.g., for Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia).
  • Renal Function Monitoring: Regular and meticulous monitoring of kidney function (serum creatinine, GFR, urinalysis) is essential due to the high risk of rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis.
  • Medication Side Effects: Be vigilant for side effects of immunosuppressive drugs, including corticosteroids (e.g., hyperglycemia, osteoporosis), cyclophosphamide (e.g., hemorrhagic cystitis, myelosuppression), and rituximab (e.g., infusion reactions, infections).
  • Vaccination Status: Ensure patients receive appropriate vaccinations, particularly inactivated vaccines, before or during immunosuppressive therapy, consulting with the healthcare team.
  • Avoidance of Triggers: While specific triggers are not fully understood, patients should avoid known irritants or allergens that may exacerbate respiratory symptoms.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Corticosteroid Management: If on corticosteroids, manage potential side effects with a low-sodium diet to reduce fluid retention, a diet rich in calcium and vitamin D to prevent osteoporosis, and careful carbohydrate intake to manage blood glucose levels.
  • Kidney Health Support: For patients with significant renal involvement, a dietitian-guided diet may be necessary, potentially involving restrictions on protein, potassium, and phosphorus to protect kidney function.
  • Hydration with Cyclophosphamide: Maintain excellent hydration, especially when receiving cyclophosphamide, to help prevent hemorrhagic cystitis.
  • Food Safety: Due to immunosuppression, emphasize strict food hygiene practices, including thorough cooking of meats, avoiding raw or undercooked foods, and careful washing of fruits and vegetables to minimize infection risk.
  • Nutrient-Dense Foods: Focus on a balanced intake of nutrient-dense foods to support overall health and immune function, avoiding highly processed foods.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Strict Adherence to Therapy: Emphasize the critical importance of strict adherence to prescribed immunosuppressive regimens to control disease activity and prevent relapses.
  • Prompt Symptom Reporting: Instruct patients to immediately report any new or worsening symptoms, especially fever, difficulty breathing, blood in urine, or severe pain, to their healthcare provider.
  • Regular Clinical Follow-ups: Stress the necessity of consistent follow-up appointments with rheumatologists, nephrologists, pulmonologists, and other specialists for ongoing monitoring and treatment adjustments.
  • Infection Prevention: Educate patients on rigorous hand hygiene, avoiding sick contacts, and recognizing early signs of infection due to their immunocompromised state.
  • Medication Interactions: Advise patients to inform all healthcare providers about their GPA diagnosis and current medications to prevent adverse drug interactions.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Multidisciplinary Management: GPA requires a collaborative approach involving rheumatology, nephrology, pulmonology, ENT, and ophthalmology to address multi-organ involvement effectively.
  • Induction and Maintenance Therapy: Treatment typically involves an aggressive induction phase (e.g., cyclophosphamide or rituximab with high-dose corticosteroids) followed by a long-term maintenance phase to sustain remission.
  • Biopsy Confirmation: While ANCA serology is highly suggestive, tissue biopsy (e.g., kidney, lung, sinus) demonstrating necrotizing granulomatous inflammation and vasculitis is often crucial for definitive diagnosis.
  • Relapse Recognition: Physicians must be vigilant for signs of disease relapse, which can occur even during maintenance therapy, and be prepared to escalate treatment as needed.
  • Long-term Surveillance: Ongoing monitoring for disease activity, treatment toxicities, and long-term complications (e.g., chronic kidney disease, subglottic stenosis) is paramount.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Patient Education: Nurses are vital in educating patients about their disease, medication regimens, potential side effects, and the importance of infection prevention and adherence.
  • Symptom Assessment: Conduct thorough and regular assessments of respiratory status, renal function, skin integrity, and neurological symptoms to identify disease activity or complications.
  • Medication Administration: Safely administer complex immunosuppressive agents, including intravenous infusions, and monitor for immediate and delayed adverse reactions.
  • Laboratory Monitoring: Closely monitor laboratory results such as complete blood counts, renal function tests, liver function tests, and ANCA titers to guide treatment and detect complications.
  • Psychosocial Support: Provide emotional support and connect patients with resources to cope with the challenges of living with a chronic, serious autoimmune disease.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Pathophysiology: Systemic necrotizing vasculitis affecting small-to-medium vessels, characterized by granulomatous inflammation, often associated with PR3-ANCA.
  • Diagnostic Markers: Positive PR3-ANCA (c-ANCA pattern), MPO-ANCA (p-ANCA pattern in some cases), elevated ESR/CRP, tissue biopsy showing vasculitis and granulomas.
  • Classification Criteria: American College of Rheumatology (ACR) 1990 criteria, and more recently, the 2012 Chapel Hill Consensus Conference (CHCC) definitions and EULAR/ACR 2022 classification criteria.
  • Treatment Guidelines: EULAR/ERA-EDTA recommendations for the management of ANCA-associated vasculitis, typically involving induction with cyclophosphamide/rituximab and corticosteroids, followed by maintenance with azathioprine/methotrexate/rituximab.
  • Prognosis: Historically fatal, but modern immunosuppressive therapy has significantly improved prognosis, though chronic disease and potential for relapse remain. Five-year survival rates exceed 80%.
  • Complications: End-stage renal disease, respiratory failure, subglottic stenosis, permanent organ damage, and treatment-related toxicities (e.g., infection, malignancy).
