Whooping cough in teens and adults – ED discharge instructions

🏠 Home / Emergency medicine / Emergency department discharge instructions

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Understanding Pertussis: Whooping cough (pertussis) is a highly contagious respiratory infection caused by the bacterium Bordetella pertussis, characterized by severe coughing fits.
  • Contagious Period: You are contagious from the onset of symptoms and typically for at least 21 days, or until 5 days after starting appropriate antibiotic treatment.
  • Vaccination Importance: Even if vaccinated, adults and teens can still contract pertussis, though symptoms are often milder. Vaccination helps reduce severity and transmission.
  • Prolonged Cough: The characteristic 'whooping' cough and post-tussive vomiting can persist for weeks to months, even after the bacteria have been cleared by antibiotics.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Worsening Cough: Increasing frequency or severity of coughing fits, especially those leading to breathlessness or vomiting.
  • Difficulty Breathing: New or worsening shortness of breath, rapid breathing, or visible effort to breathe.
  • Cyanosis: Bluish discoloration around the lips or fingertips, indicating low oxygen levels.
  • Dehydration Signs: Decreased urination, dry mouth, or extreme thirst, particularly if vomiting after coughing.
  • Fever Persistence: High or persistent fever, which may indicate a secondary bacterial infection like pneumonia.
  • Altered Mental Status: Confusion, excessive drowsiness, or unresponsiveness, which can be a sign of severe oxygen deprivation or complications.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Complete Antibiotic Course: It is vital to take all prescribed antibiotics exactly as directed, even if you start feeling better, to eradicate the bacteria and prevent further spread.
  • Infection Control: Practice meticulous hand hygiene, cover coughs and sneezes, and avoid close contact with infants, pregnant individuals, and immunocompromised persons until no longer contagious.
  • Avoid Irritants: Steer clear of smoke, dust, and chemical fumes, which can trigger coughing fits and worsen respiratory irritation.
  • Hydration and Nutrition: Maintain adequate fluid intake to thin secretions and prevent dehydration. Eat small, frequent meals to minimize post-tussive vomiting.
  • Vaccinate Close Contacts: Encourage family members and close contacts, especially those with infants or young children, to ensure their pertussis vaccinations are up-to-date.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Frequent Small Meals: Consume smaller, more frequent meals throughout the day to reduce the risk of vomiting after coughing episodes.
  • Hydration Focus: Drink plenty of fluids such as water, clear broths, and electrolyte solutions to prevent dehydration and help thin respiratory secretions.
  • Avoid Trigger Foods: Identify and avoid foods or beverages that seem to trigger or worsen your cough, such as very cold drinks or highly acidic foods.
  • Soft, Easy-to-Swallow Foods: Opt for soft, bland foods that are easy to swallow, especially if your throat is sore from coughing.
  • Post-Vomiting Rehydration: If vomiting occurs, slowly reintroduce fluids and bland foods once the coughing fit subsides.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Return to ED Criteria: Seek immediate medical attention if you experience severe difficulty breathing, bluish discoloration of the lips or face, seizures, or extreme lethargy.
  • Isolation Period: Remain isolated from others, especially vulnerable populations, for at least 5 days after starting antibiotics to prevent transmission.
  • Monitor for Complications: Be vigilant for signs of pneumonia (persistent fever, worsening cough with colored sputum) or dehydration.
  • Medication Side Effects: Report any severe side effects from your antibiotics to your primary care provider.
  • Follow-up Care: Schedule a follow-up appointment with your primary care physician within the next few days to monitor your recovery and address any ongoing concerns.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Antibiotic Adherence is Key: Completing the full course of antibiotics is paramount, not only for your recovery but also to prevent the spread of this highly contagious disease.
  • Expect a Lingering Cough: Understand that the cough associated with pertussis can persist for many weeks, even after the infection has been cleared. This is due to airway irritation.
  • Protect Vulnerable Individuals: Your primary responsibility is to prevent transmission to infants and unvaccinated individuals, for whom pertussis can be life-threatening.
  • Vaccination for Contacts: Advise all close contacts to consult their physician regarding Tdap vaccination, especially if they are around infants or young children.
  • When to Re-evaluate: If your respiratory symptoms worsen significantly, or if you develop new symptoms such as high fever or chest pain, do not hesitate to seek further medical evaluation.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Assessment Focus: Prioritize respiratory assessment, including rate, effort, oxygen saturation, and auscultation for adventitious sounds. Monitor for signs of respiratory distress.
  • Patient Education: Provide clear, concise instructions on antibiotic regimen, infection control measures (hand hygiene, cough etiquette), and signs/symptoms warranting ED return.
  • Hydration Monitoring: Assess hydration status, especially in patients with post-tussive vomiting, and educate on fluid intake strategies.
  • Vaccination Counseling: Discuss the importance of Tdap vaccination for the patient and their close contacts, emphasizing booster schedules.
  • Psychosocial Support: Acknowledge the distress caused by prolonged coughing fits and offer strategies for coping, such as rest and humidified air.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Causative Agent: Bordetella pertussis, a Gram-negative coccobacillus.
  • Diagnosis: Primarily via nasopharyngeal swab for PCR (polymerase chain reaction) testing. Culture may also be performed.
  • Treatment: Macrolide antibiotics (e.g., azithromycin, erythromycin, clarithromycin) are the first-line treatment. Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole is an alternative.
  • Complications: Pneumonia, otitis media, seizures, encephalopathy, rib fractures, syncope, weight loss, and urinary incontinence due to severe coughing.
  • Public Health Reporting: Pertussis is a nationally notifiable disease, requiring reporting to local public health authorities.
  • Vaccine: Tdap (tetanus, diphtheria, acellular pertussis) vaccine is recommended for adolescents and adults.
