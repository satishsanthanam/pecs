Cellulitis (skin infection) in children – ED discharge instructions

🏠 Home / Emergency medicine / Emergency department discharge instructions

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Understanding Cellulitis: Cellulitis is a common bacterial infection of the skin and the tissues beneath it, often appearing as a red, swollen, warm, and tender area.
  • Causes: It usually occurs when bacteria enter the skin through a cut, scratch, insect bite, or other break in the skin barrier.
  • Treatment Goal: The primary goal is to eliminate the infection with antibiotics and prevent its spread or recurrence.
  • Expected Recovery: With proper treatment, symptoms should begin to improve within 24-48 hours, though the redness may take longer to fully resolve.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Redness and Swelling: The affected skin area will appear red, swollen, and may expand over time.
  • Warmth and Tenderness: The infected area will feel warm to the touch and be painful or tender when pressed.
  • Fever and Chills: Children may develop a fever, chills, or generally feel unwell (malaise).
  • Streaking: Red streaks extending from the infected area towards the lymph nodes (lymphangitis) indicate spreading infection.
  • Blisters or Pus: In some cases, blisters or pus-filled bumps may form on the skin.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Complete Antibiotic Course: Administer all prescribed antibiotic doses exactly as directed, even if the child feels better, to prevent recurrence and antibiotic resistance.
  • Elevate Affected Limb: If the cellulitis is on an arm or leg, keep the limb elevated above heart level as much as possible to reduce swelling.
  • Pain Management: Administer over-the-counter pain relievers like acetaminophen or ibuprofen as needed for pain and fever, following dosage instructions carefully.
  • Keep Area Clean: Gently clean any open wounds or skin breaks daily with mild soap and water, then cover with a clean dressing if advised by the physician.
  • Monitor for Worsening: Regularly check the affected area for signs of spreading redness, increased pain, new blisters, or pus formation.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Maintain Hydration: Encourage your child to drink plenty of fluids (water, clear broths, diluted juices) to stay well-hydrated, especially if they have a fever.
  • Antibiotics with Food: If the prescribed antibiotic causes stomach upset (nausea, vomiting), try giving it with a small meal or snack, unless otherwise instructed.
  • Avoid Irritants: While not a direct restriction, avoid giving foods that might further upset a sensitive stomach if the child is already feeling unwell from the infection or medication.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Spreading Redness: Return to the ED if the redness spreads rapidly, especially if it crosses previous markings made by the doctor.
  • High Fever or Chills: Seek immediate medical attention for a persistent high fever (above 102°F or 39°C) or uncontrollable shivering/chills.
  • Increased Pain or Swelling: Return if pain becomes severe and unmanageable, or if swelling significantly worsens.
  • New Blisters or Pus: Immediate evaluation is needed if new blisters, pus, or open sores develop in the affected area.
  • Lethargy or Confusion: Any signs of extreme tiredness, difficulty waking, or confusion require urgent medical assessment.
  • Allergic Reaction: Seek emergency care for signs of an allergic reaction to antibiotics, such as hives, difficulty breathing, or facial swelling.
  • No Improvement: If there is no noticeable improvement in symptoms after 24-48 hours of starting antibiotics, return for re-evaluation.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Adherence to Treatment: Emphasize the critical importance of completing the entire course of antibiotics to fully eradicate the infection and prevent complications.
  • Follow-up Care: Schedule a follow-up appointment with your child's primary care physician within 24-48 hours or as advised to monitor progress.
  • Skin Integrity: Advise on identifying and addressing any underlying skin conditions (e.g., eczema, athlete's foot) that may predispose to future cellulitis.
  • Marking the Area: Explain that the ED team may have marked the border of the cellulitis to help monitor for spread; advise parents to observe this line.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Patient Education: Provide clear, concise discharge instructions to parents/guardians regarding medication administration, wound care, and signs of worsening infection.
  • Pain Assessment: Educate parents on how to assess their child's pain level and appropriate use of analgesics.
  • Infection Control: Reinforce hand hygiene and proper dressing changes (if applicable) to prevent transmission and secondary infections.
  • Monitoring for Complications: Instruct on recognizing signs of potential complications such as abscess formation, lymphangitis, or systemic infection.
  • Resource Provision: Ensure parents have contact information for follow-up care and know when to return to the ED.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Etiology: Commonly caused by Streptococcus pyogenes (Group A Strep) and Staphylococcus aureus, including Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) in some cases.
  • Diagnosis: Primarily a clinical diagnosis based on characteristic skin findings; cultures are often not necessary unless severe or unresponsive to initial treatment.
  • Treatment: Oral antibiotics (e.g., cephalexin, clindamycin, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole) are typically prescribed for 5-10 days, depending on severity and pathogen.
  • Complications: Potential complications include abscess formation, necrotizing fasciitis (rare but severe), bacteremia, and sepsis.
  • Risk Factors: Skin breaks (cuts, insect bites, eczema), tinea pedis, obesity, and immunocompromised states.
