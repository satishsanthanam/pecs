Gout – ED discharge instructions

🏠 Home / Emergency medicine / Emergency department discharge instructions

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Gout is a form of inflammatory arthritis: It's caused by the buildup of uric acid crystals in a joint, leading to sudden, severe pain, swelling, redness, and tenderness.
  • Acute flare management: Emergency department treatment focuses on rapidly reducing pain and inflammation.
  • Importance of follow-up: This ED visit addresses the acute attack; long-term management with your primary care provider or a rheumatologist is crucial to prevent future flares.
  • Medication adherence is key: Take all prescribed medications as directed to control symptoms and prevent recurrence.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Sudden, intense joint pain: Often affecting the big toe, but can occur in ankles, knees, wrists, and fingers.
  • Swelling and redness: The affected joint will appear swollen, red, and inflamed.
  • Warmth and tenderness: The joint will feel warm to the touch and be extremely sensitive, even to light touch (e.g., a bedsheet).
  • Limited range of motion: Difficulty moving the affected joint due to pain and swelling.
  • Fever and chills: While less common in gout, these symptoms warrant immediate medical re-evaluation to rule out infection.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Medication compliance: Take all prescribed anti-inflammatory medications (NSAIDs, colchicine, or corticosteroids) exactly as instructed to manage pain and inflammation. Do not stop early unless advised by a doctor.
  • Avoid triggers: Identify and avoid personal triggers such as certain foods, alcohol, dehydration, or stress that can precipitate a flare.
  • Rest and elevation: Rest the affected joint and elevate it to reduce swelling and pain. Avoid putting weight on the joint if possible.
  • Hydration: Drink plenty of water throughout the day to help flush uric acid from your system.
  • When to seek immediate care: Return to the ED or seek urgent medical attention if pain worsens significantly, you develop a high fever, the joint becomes hot and red with pus-like discharge, or you experience new, concerning symptoms.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Limit high-purine foods: Reduce intake of red meat, organ meats (liver, kidney), shellfish (shrimp, scallops, mussels), and certain fish (sardines, anchovies).
  • Avoid alcohol, especially beer: Alcohol, particularly beer, can increase uric acid levels and trigger flares.
  • Steer clear of sugary drinks and high-fructose corn syrup: These can also elevate uric acid levels.
  • Increase water intake: Aim for 8-12 glasses of water daily to help excrete uric acid.
  • Incorporate low-fat dairy: Some studies suggest low-fat dairy products may help reduce gout risk.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Medication instructions: Ensure you understand the dosage, frequency, and potential side effects of all prescribed medications. Take with food if advised to prevent stomach upset.
  • Follow-up appointment: Schedule a follow-up with your primary care physician or a rheumatologist within 1-2 weeks to discuss long-term management and prevention strategies.
  • Activity modification: Avoid strenuous activity or prolonged standing/walking that puts stress on the affected joint until symptoms subside.
  • Pain management: Use ice packs for 15-20 minutes several times a day to help reduce pain and swelling, in addition to prescribed medications.
  • Recognize worsening symptoms: Be vigilant for signs of infection (increasing redness, warmth, pus, fever) or uncontrolled pain, and seek medical advice promptly.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Long-term management is crucial: While acute flares are painful, the focus should shift to preventing future attacks and complications like joint damage or kidney stones.
  • Uric acid lowering therapy (ULT): Once the acute flare resolves, discuss initiating or adjusting ULT (e.g., allopurinol, febuxostat) with your doctor to maintain uric acid levels below 6 mg/dL.
  • Lifestyle modifications are foundational: Dietary changes, weight management, and adequate hydration are essential adjuncts to medication.
  • Avoid self-medication: Do not take over-the-counter pain relievers without consulting your doctor, especially if you have other medical conditions or are on other medications.
  • Regular monitoring: Periodic blood tests to check uric acid levels and kidney function are important for effective long-term management.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Pathophysiology review: Gout results from hyperuricemia leading to monosodium urate crystal deposition in joints, triggering an inflammatory response.
  • Acute vs. chronic management: Acute flares are managed with anti-inflammatory agents; chronic gout requires uric acid-lowering therapy.
  • Patient education priorities: Emphasize medication adherence, dietary restrictions (purines, alcohol, fructose), hydration, and the importance of follow-up for ULT initiation/adjustment.
  • Pain assessment and management: Utilize appropriate pain scales and ensure effective pain relief strategies are in place, including pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions.
  • Complication awareness: Educate patients on potential complications of uncontrolled gout, such as tophi formation, joint destruction, and renal calculi.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Hyperuricemia: Elevated serum uric acid levels, typically >6.8 mg/dL, a prerequisite for gout but not always symptomatic.
  • First-line acute treatment: NSAIDs (e.g., indomethacin, naproxen), colchicine, or corticosteroids (oral or intra-articular).
  • Uric acid lowering agents (ULT): Xanthine oxidase inhibitors (allopurinol, febuxostat) are first-line; uricosurics (probenecid) are alternatives.
  • Differential diagnoses: Septic arthritis (requires urgent aspiration and antibiotics), pseudogout (calcium pyrophosphate deposition disease), cellulitis.
  • Definitive diagnosis: Aspiration of synovial fluid from the affected joint showing negatively birefringent, needle-shaped monosodium urate crystals under polarized light microscopy.
