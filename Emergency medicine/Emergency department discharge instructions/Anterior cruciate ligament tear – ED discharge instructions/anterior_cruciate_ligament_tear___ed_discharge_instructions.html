Anterior cruciate ligament tear – ED discharge instructions

🏠 Home / Emergency medicine / Emergency department discharge instructions

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Understanding an ACL Tear: The anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) is a major ligament in your knee that connects your thigh bone (femur) to your shin bone (tibia). A tear means this ligament has been stretched or ruptured, often due to sudden stops, changes in direction, or direct impact.
  • Common Injury Mechanism: ACL tears frequently occur during sports activities involving pivoting, jumping, or landing, such as basketball, soccer, and skiing.
  • Immediate Impact: An ACL tear can lead to significant knee pain, swelling, and instability, making it difficult to bear weight or move the knee normally.
  • Initial Management in ED: In the Emergency Department, the focus is on pain control, reducing swelling, and immobilizing the knee to prevent further injury, along with initial assessment and referral.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Audible Pop or Snap: Many individuals report hearing or feeling a distinct 'pop' or 'snap' at the time of injury.
  • Rapid Swelling: Significant knee swelling typically develops within a few hours due to bleeding within the joint.
  • Severe Pain: Intense pain, especially when attempting to move the knee or bear weight.
  • Knee Instability: A feeling that the knee is 'giving way' or is unstable, particularly when trying to pivot or change direction.
  • Limited Range of Motion: Difficulty fully straightening or bending the knee due to pain and swelling.
  • Inability to Bear Weight: Often, patients are unable to put full weight on the injured leg immediately after the injury.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • RICE Protocol Adherence: Continue to apply the RICE method – Rest the knee, Ice the affected area for 15-20 minutes every 2-3 hours, Compress with an elastic bandage (not too tight), and Elevate the leg above heart level to reduce swelling.
  • Crutch Use: Use crutches as instructed to avoid putting weight on the injured leg. Ensure proper crutch height and technique to prevent falls and secondary injuries.
  • Pain Management: Take prescribed pain medication as directed. Do not exceed the recommended dose. Over-the-counter anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) may also be recommended if not contraindicated.
  • Immobilization: Keep the knee immobilized in the brace or splint provided by the ED, removing it only for icing or as instructed by your physician.
  • Avoid Weight-Bearing: Strictly avoid putting full weight on the injured leg until cleared by an orthopedic specialist.
  • Watch for Complications: Monitor for signs of infection (fever, redness, warmth, pus from any skin breaks), deep vein thrombosis (DVT) (calf pain, swelling, warmth), or worsening numbness/tingling in the foot.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Hydration: Maintain adequate fluid intake, especially if taking pain medications that can cause constipation. Water and clear broths are good choices.
  • Medication-Related Considerations: If prescribed opioid pain relievers, be aware they can cause constipation; consider increasing fiber intake (fruits, vegetables, whole grains) and using stool softeners as advised.
  • Avoid Alcohol with Medications: Do not consume alcohol while taking pain medications, especially opioids or NSAIDs, as this can increase side effects and liver toxicity.
  • Nutrient-Rich Diet: Focus on a balanced diet rich in protein, vitamins (especially Vitamin C for collagen synthesis), and minerals (like zinc) to support tissue healing and recovery.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Recognize Worsening Symptoms: Seek immediate medical attention if the pain becomes unmanageable, swelling significantly increases, you develop a fever, experience numbness or tingling in the foot, or notice any signs of a blood clot (e.g., sudden calf pain, swelling, redness).
  • Medication Adherence: Ensure the patient takes all prescribed medications as directed and understands potential side effects.
  • Assistance with Mobility: Help the patient with safe ambulation using crutches, especially on stairs or uneven surfaces. Clear pathways at home to prevent falls.
  • Follow-up Appointments: Confirm the patient has scheduled and understands the importance of their follow-up appointment with an orthopedic surgeon.
  • Emotional Support: Provide emotional support, as an ACL injury can be frustrating and impact daily activities and mental well-being.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Orthopedic Follow-up is Crucial: An ACL tear requires specialized orthopedic evaluation. You must follow up with an orthopedic surgeon within 3-7 days for definitive diagnosis and treatment planning.
  • Diagnostic Confirmation: While clinical examination in the ED suggests an ACL tear, an MRI is typically required to confirm the diagnosis and assess for other associated injuries (e.g., meniscal tears, collateral ligament injuries).
  • Treatment Options: Treatment may range from non-surgical management with extensive physical therapy to surgical reconstruction, depending on the patient's age, activity level, and extent of instability.
  • Rehabilitation is Key: Regardless of surgical intervention, a comprehensive physical therapy program is essential for restoring knee function, strength, and stability.
  • Long-term Implications: Untreated or inadequately rehabilitated ACL tears can lead to chronic knee instability, recurrent 'giving way' episodes, and an increased risk of developing early-onset osteoarthritis.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Neurovascular Assessment: Regularly assess distal pulses, capillary refill, sensation, and motor function in the affected extremity to rule out neurovascular compromise.
  • Pain Management Education: Educate patients on the proper use of analgesics, potential side effects, and non-pharmacological pain relief strategies (e.g., RICE).
  • Crutch Training: Provide thorough instruction and demonstration on safe crutch ambulation, including ascending/descending stairs, emphasizing partial or non-weight-bearing as ordered.
  • Splint/Brace Application: Ensure proper application and fit of knee immobilizers or braces, educating the patient on when and how to remove them (e.g., for icing).
  • DVT Prophylaxis Education: Discuss the risk of deep vein thrombosis and instruct patients on signs/symptoms to watch for, especially with prolonged immobilization.
  • Discharge Planning: Facilitate timely orthopedic referral and ensure the patient understands all discharge instructions, including activity restrictions and follow-up care.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Mechanism of Injury: Often non-contact deceleration with valgus collapse and internal tibial rotation, or hyperextension.
  • Clinical Tests: Lachman test (most sensitive), Anterior Drawer test, Pivot Shift test.
  • Imaging Modalities: X-rays to rule out fractures; MRI is the gold standard for diagnosing ligamentous and meniscal injuries.
  • Associated Injuries: Often co-occurs with meniscal tears (especially lateral meniscus), medial collateral ligament (MCL) tears (O'Donoghue's unhappy triad), and bone bruises.
  • Surgical Reconstruction: Typically involves arthroscopic surgery using autograft (patellar tendon, hamstring, quadriceps tendon) or allograft tissue.
  • Rehabilitation Phases: Involves pre-operative strengthening, immediate post-operative protection, early motion, progressive strengthening, and return-to-sport specific training.
