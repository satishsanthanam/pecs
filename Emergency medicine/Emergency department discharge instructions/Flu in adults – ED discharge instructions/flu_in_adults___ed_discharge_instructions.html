Flu in adults – ED discharge instructions

🏠 Home / Emergency medicine / Emergency department discharge instructions

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Influenza (Flu): You have been diagnosed with influenza, a contagious respiratory illness caused by a virus.
  • Transmission: The flu spreads easily through respiratory droplets when an infected person coughs, sneezes, or talks.
  • Contagious Period: You can spread the flu to others starting 1 day before symptoms develop and up to 5-7 days after becoming sick.
  • Recovery Expectation: Most adults recover within 1-2 weeks, but symptoms can be severe and lead to complications, especially in high-risk individuals.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Common Symptoms: Expect to experience fever, cough, sore throat, body aches, headache, chills, and fatigue.
  • Worsening Cough/Shortness of Breath: Persistent or worsening cough, difficulty breathing, or shortness of breath are signs of potential complications.
  • Persistent High Fever: A fever that remains very high or returns after improving could indicate a secondary infection.
  • Signs of Dehydration: Decreased urination, dry mouth, lightheadedness, or dizziness warrant attention.
  • Chest Pain/Pressure: Any new or worsening chest pain or pressure requires immediate medical evaluation.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Medication Adherence: Take all prescribed antiviral medications exactly as directed. Complete the full course, even if you start to feel better.
  • Symptom Management: Use over-the-counter medications like acetaminophen or ibuprofen for fever and body aches as advised by your physician.
  • Rest and Hydration: Get plenty of rest to aid recovery and drink ample fluids (water, clear broths, electrolyte solutions) to prevent dehydration.
  • Infection Control: Cover coughs and sneezes with a tissue or your elbow. Wash hands frequently with soap and water for at least 20 seconds or use an alcohol-based hand sanitizer.
  • Limit Contact: Stay home from work or school and avoid contact with others, especially vulnerable individuals, until you are fever-free for at least 24 hours without the use of fever-reducing medication.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Prioritize Hydration: Focus on clear fluids such as water, herbal teas, clear broths, and electrolyte-rich drinks (e.g., sports drinks, oral rehydration solutions) to prevent dehydration.
  • Easily Digestible Foods: If you have an appetite, opt for bland, easy-to-digest foods like toast, crackers, rice, applesauce, and bananas.
  • Avoid Irritants: Limit caffeine, alcohol, and sugary drinks, which can contribute to dehydration or irritate the throat.
  • Small, Frequent Meals: If nausea is present, try consuming small, frequent meals rather than large ones to maintain nutrition.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Immediate ED Return: Return to the Emergency Department immediately if you experience severe difficulty breathing, persistent chest pain or pressure, sudden dizziness, confusion, severe or persistent vomiting, or seizures.
  • Worsening After Improvement: If your flu-like symptoms improve but then return with a fever and worse cough, this could indicate a secondary bacterial infection requiring prompt medical evaluation.
  • High-Risk Individuals: If you have chronic medical conditions (e.g., asthma, diabetes, heart disease), are pregnant, or are over 65 years old, you are at higher risk for complications and should be closely monitored for any worsening symptoms.
  • Dehydration Signs: Watch for signs of severe dehydration such as extreme thirst, infrequent urination, or inability to keep fluids down.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Antiviral Timing: Antiviral medications are most effective when started within 48 hours of symptom onset. Adherence is crucial for reducing symptom severity and duration.
  • Supportive Care: Emphasize the importance of supportive care, including adequate rest, consistent hydration, and appropriate use of fever and pain relief.
  • Complication Vigilance: Counsel patients to be vigilant for signs of complications such as pneumonia, bronchitis, or exacerbation of underlying chronic conditions.
  • Follow-up Plan: Advise patients to follow up with their primary care provider within a few days, especially if symptoms persist or worsen, or if they belong to a high-risk group.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Patient Education Priority: Ensure clear, concise discharge instructions are provided, covering medication administration, symptom monitoring, and clear criteria for returning to the ED.
  • Infection Prevention Strategies: Reinforce proper hand hygiene, respiratory etiquette, and environmental cleaning to prevent further transmission within the household and community.
  • Fluid Balance Assessment: Educate patients on recognizing signs of dehydration and emphasize the importance of consistent fluid intake.
  • Vaccination Advocacy: Use this opportunity to educate patients on the importance of annual influenza vaccination for future prevention and reducing severity.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Influenza Virus Types: Seasonal epidemics are primarily caused by Influenza A and B viruses, with varying strains each year.
  • Antiviral Agents: Common antiviral medications include Oseltamivir (Tamiflu), Zanamivir (Relenza), Peramivir (Rapivab), and Baloxavir marboxil (Xofluza).
  • Potential Complications: Key complications include primary viral pneumonia, secondary bacterial pneumonia, bronchitis, sinusitis, otitis media, and exacerbation of chronic medical conditions.
  • Differential Diagnosis: Consider other respiratory pathogens such as SARS-CoV-2, RSV, and other common cold viruses when evaluating flu-like symptoms.
