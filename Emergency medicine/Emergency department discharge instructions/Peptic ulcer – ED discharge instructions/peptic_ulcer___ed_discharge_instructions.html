Peptic ulcer – ED discharge instructions

🏠 Home / Emergency medicine / Emergency department discharge instructions

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Understanding Peptic Ulcers: A peptic ulcer is an open sore that develops on the lining of the stomach, small intestine (duodenum), or esophagus, often causing burning stomach pain.
  • Common Causes: The most frequent causes are infection with Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) bacteria and long-term use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) like ibuprofen or naproxen.
  • ED Management: Your visit to the Emergency Department addressed acute symptoms and ruled out immediate life-threatening complications. Definitive diagnosis and long-term management require follow-up.
  • Importance of Follow-Up: It is crucial to schedule and attend your follow-up appointment with a gastroenterologist or primary care provider to determine the exact cause of your ulcer and establish a treatment plan.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Epigastric Pain: A burning or gnawing pain in the upper abdomen, often worse when the stomach is empty and relieved by food or antacids.
  • Nausea and Vomiting: Feeling sick to your stomach, sometimes accompanied by vomiting, which may include blood (hematemesis) appearing as bright red or 'coffee grounds'.
  • Bloating and Fullness: A sensation of discomfort or swelling in the abdomen, even after eating small amounts.
  • Dark, Tarry Stools (Melena): This indicates bleeding in the upper gastrointestinal tract. Seek immediate medical attention if you notice this.
  • Unexplained Weight Loss: Chronic ulcers can lead to appetite loss and subsequent weight reduction.
  • Fatigue and Weakness: Symptoms of anemia due to chronic blood loss from the ulcer.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Medication Adherence: Take all prescribed medications (e.g., proton pump inhibitors, H2 blockers, antibiotics for H. pylori) exactly as directed to promote healing and prevent recurrence.
  • Avoid NSAIDs and Aspirin: Strictly avoid all nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and aspirin unless specifically instructed by your doctor, as they can worsen ulcers or cause new ones.
  • Smoking Cessation: Smoking significantly impairs ulcer healing and increases the risk of recurrence. Seek support to quit.
  • Alcohol Restriction: Limit or avoid alcohol consumption, as it can irritate the stomach lining and delay healing.
  • Recognize Warning Signs: Be vigilant for signs of worsening condition such as severe, sudden abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, black or bloody stools, or dizziness/fainting. These warrant immediate ED re-evaluation.
  • Stress Management: While not a direct cause, stress can exacerbate ulcer symptoms. Practice stress-reducing techniques.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Avoid Trigger Foods: Limit or avoid foods that commonly irritate the stomach, such as spicy foods, acidic foods (citrus, tomatoes), fatty foods, and caffeine.
  • Small, Frequent Meals: Eat smaller meals more frequently throughout the day rather than large, heavy meals to reduce stomach acid surges.
  • Hydration: Maintain adequate hydration with water, avoiding carbonated beverages and excessive caffeine.
  • Identify Personal Triggers: Pay attention to foods that specifically worsen your symptoms and avoid them. Keep a food diary if helpful.
  • Avoid Eating Before Bed: Try to avoid eating for at least 2-3 hours before lying down to sleep to minimize acid reflux.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Monitor for Worsening Symptoms: Caregivers should be aware of and monitor for any signs of complications, such as increased pain, vomiting blood, or dark stools, and ensure prompt medical attention.
  • Medication Assistance: Help the patient adhere to their medication schedule, especially if multiple medications are prescribed.
  • Ensure Follow-Up: Assist the patient in scheduling and attending all follow-up appointments with specialists.
  • Emergency Contact: Know when to call emergency services (911 or local equivalent) for severe symptoms like sudden, sharp abdominal pain, fainting, or significant bleeding.
  • Support Dietary Changes: Help the patient maintain dietary restrictions and prepare appropriate meals.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Definitive Diagnosis: An endoscopy is typically required to confirm the presence, location, and cause of a peptic ulcer, and to rule out malignancy.
  • H. pylori Eradication: If H. pylori infection is confirmed, a course of antibiotics combined with acid-suppressing medication is essential for successful treatment and prevention of recurrence.
  • Long-Term Management: Patients with a history of peptic ulcers, especially those requiring chronic NSAID use, may need long-term acid suppression therapy.
  • Complication Awareness: Untreated ulcers can lead to serious complications such as bleeding, perforation (a hole in the stomach/intestinal wall), or obstruction.
  • Lifestyle Modification: Emphasize the critical role of smoking cessation, alcohol moderation, and NSAID avoidance in preventing future ulcer episodes.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Pathophysiology Review: Peptic ulcers result from an imbalance between aggressive factors (acid, pepsin, H. pylori, NSAIDs) and defensive factors (mucus, bicarbonate, prostaglandins, blood flow) in the gastroduodenal mucosa.
  • Patient Education Priority: Nurses play a vital role in educating patients on medication adherence, dietary modifications, symptom recognition, and the importance of follow-up care.
  • Monitoring for GI Bleeding: Assess for signs of gastrointestinal bleeding, including vital sign changes (tachycardia, hypotension), pallor, and changes in stool color/consistency.
  • Pain Management: Implement non-pharmacological and pharmacological strategies for pain relief, ensuring NSAIDs are avoided.
  • Discharge Planning: Ensure comprehensive discharge instructions are provided verbally and in writing, confirming patient understanding and addressing potential barriers to care.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • H. pylori: Helicobacter pylori, a gram-negative bacterium, a primary cause of peptic ulcers and gastritis.
  • NSAIDs: Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, a class of medications that can cause peptic ulcers by inhibiting prostaglandin synthesis.
  • PPIs: Proton Pump Inhibitors, a class of drugs that reduce gastric acid production, commonly used in ulcer treatment.
  • Endoscopy: A diagnostic procedure involving a flexible tube with a camera to visualize the upper GI tract, used to diagnose ulcers and take biopsies.
  • Melena: Dark, tarry stools indicative of upper gastrointestinal bleeding.
  • Hematemesis: Vomiting of blood, which can be bright red or resemble 'coffee grounds'.
  • Perforation: A serious complication where an ulcer erodes through the entire wall of the stomach or intestine, leading to peritonitis.
  • ACG Guidelines: American College of Gastroenterology guidelines for the diagnosis and management of peptic ulcer disease.
