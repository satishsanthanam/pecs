Anxiety in children and teens – ED discharge instructions

🏠 Home / Emergency medicine / Emergency department discharge instructions

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Understanding Anxiety: Anxiety is a normal human emotion, but when it's intense, persistent, and interferes with daily life, it becomes a disorder.
  • ED Visit Context: Your child presented to the Emergency Department due to acute anxiety symptoms, which may include panic attacks, severe worry, or physical manifestations like chest pain or hyperventilation.
  • Goal of ED Care: The ED's role is to rule out urgent medical causes for symptoms and stabilize acute distress. Long-term management requires follow-up.
  • Common Triggers: Stressors like school pressure, social issues, family changes, or underlying medical conditions can exacerbate anxiety.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Physical Manifestations: Rapid heart rate, shortness of breath, chest tightness, dizziness, nausea, stomach aches, headaches, sweating, trembling.
  • Emotional & Cognitive Signs: Excessive worry, fear, irritability, difficulty concentrating, feeling overwhelmed, panic attacks, fear of losing control.
  • Behavioral Changes: Avoidance of school or social situations, sleep disturbances (insomnia or nightmares), restlessness, clinginess, tantrums (in younger children).
  • Acute Distress: Crying spells, hyperventilation, feeling detached from reality, or expressing thoughts of self-harm (requiring immediate re-evaluation).

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Safety Plan: Discuss and establish a safety plan with your child, including who to contact during an anxiety crisis.
  • Medication Adherence: If medication was prescribed (e.g., short-term anxiolytics), administer exactly as directed and understand potential side effects. Do not stop abruptly.
  • Monitoring for Worsening: Watch for increased frequency or intensity of anxiety symptoms, new onset of self-harm ideation, or significant functional impairment.
  • Substance Use: Be aware that teens may attempt to self-medicate anxiety with substances; monitor for signs of drug or alcohol use.
  • Environmental Triggers: Identify and, where possible, reduce exposure to known stressors or triggers in the home or school environment.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Caffeine Restriction: Limit or eliminate caffeine intake (soda, energy drinks, coffee) as it can exacerbate anxiety symptoms and disrupt sleep.
  • Hydration: Ensure adequate fluid intake, preferably water, to support overall physiological function and prevent dehydration-related symptoms that can mimic anxiety.
  • Regular Meals: Encourage regular, balanced meals to maintain stable blood sugar levels, as significant fluctuations can sometimes trigger or worsen anxiety.
  • Sugar Intake: Minimize excessive intake of highly processed foods and sugary snacks, which can lead to energy crashes and irritability.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Follow-Up Appointments: Ensure immediate scheduling and attendance of all recommended follow-up appointments with mental health professionals (therapist, psychiatrist) or primary care provider.
  • Medication Management: Keep all medications out of reach of children and teens. Understand the specific instructions for any prescribed anxiolytics or antidepressants.
  • Crisis Resources: Have contact information readily available for local mental health crisis lines, emergency services, and the child's therapist/psychiatrist.
  • Open Communication: Maintain open and supportive communication with your child, validating their feelings without dismissing their distress.
  • School Communication: Inform the school about your child's anxiety and any accommodations that may be needed, with appropriate consent.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Comprehensive Evaluation: An ED visit addresses acute crisis; a thorough outpatient evaluation by a child psychiatrist or psychologist is crucial for diagnosis and long-term management.
  • Treatment Modalities: Evidence-based treatments include cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT), dialectical behavior therapy (DBT), and sometimes pharmacotherapy.
  • Rule Out Medical Causes: Always consider and rule out underlying medical conditions (e.g., thyroid dysfunction, asthma, cardiac issues) that can present with anxiety-like symptoms.
  • Early Intervention: Early identification and intervention are key to preventing chronic anxiety and improving long-term outcomes for children and teens.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Patient Education: Nurses play a vital role in educating families on anxiety symptoms, coping strategies, medication administration, and the importance of follow-up care.
  • Safety Assessment: Ongoing assessment for suicidality, self-harm, and escalating distress is paramount, especially during ED presentation and discharge planning.
  • Therapeutic Communication: Utilize active listening, empathetic responses, and age-appropriate language to build rapport and de-escalate acute anxiety in pediatric patients.
  • Resource Navigation: Assist families in navigating mental health resources, providing clear instructions for accessing outpatient therapy, crisis lines, and support groups.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD): Persistent and excessive worry about various aspects of life.
  • Panic Disorder: Recurrent, unexpected panic attacks with intense fear and physical symptoms.
  • Social Anxiety Disorder: Intense fear of social situations and scrutiny by others.
  • Separation Anxiety Disorder: Excessive fear or anxiety concerning separation from home or attachment figures.
  • Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT): A psychotherapeutic approach that helps identify and change negative thought patterns and behaviors.
  • Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors (SSRIs): A class of antidepressants often used off-label for anxiety disorders in children and teens under strict medical supervision.
