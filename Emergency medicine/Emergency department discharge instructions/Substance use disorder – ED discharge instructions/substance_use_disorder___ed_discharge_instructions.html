Substance use disorder – ED discharge instructions

🏠 Home / Emergency medicine / Emergency department discharge instructions

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Understanding SUD: Substance Use Disorder (SUD) is a chronic, relapsing brain disease characterized by compulsive substance seeking and use despite harmful consequences.
  • ED's Role: The Emergency Department provides acute stabilization, manages immediate withdrawal symptoms, and addresses overdose, but is not a long-term treatment facility.
  • Discharge Focus: Discharge instructions aim to ensure your safety post-ED visit and facilitate your connection to ongoing specialized treatment and support services.
  • Relapse Risk: The period immediately following acute care is often high-risk for relapse; consistent engagement with treatment is crucial.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Withdrawal Syndrome: Symptoms can include tremors, nausea, vomiting, sweating, anxiety, agitation, seizures, or hallucinations, varying by substance.
  • Overdose Manifestations: May present as altered mental status, respiratory depression, pinpoint pupils (opioids), chest pain, or severe agitation.
  • Medical Complications: Infections (e.g., skin, heart valves), trauma, gastrointestinal bleeding, or organ damage related to substance use.
  • Behavioral & Psychiatric: Impaired judgment, paranoia, aggression, severe depression, or suicidal ideation can be associated with SUD.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Naloxone Access: If opioid use is a concern, ensure you or a trusted individual has naloxone and knows how to administer it in case of overdose.
  • Safe Environment: Remove all illicit substances, alcohol, and any means of self-harm (e.g., firearms) from your immediate environment.
  • Medication Safety: Take all prescribed medications exactly as directed; do not share, sell, or misuse them. Store securely.
  • Avoid Driving: Do not drive or operate heavy machinery if you are impaired by substances, experiencing withdrawal, or under the influence of sedating medications.
  • Overdose Awareness: Be aware that tolerance to substances decreases rapidly after a period of abstinence, significantly increasing the risk of overdose if use resumes.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Hydration: Maintain adequate fluid intake, especially if experiencing nausea, vomiting, or sweating from withdrawal.
  • Nutritional Support: Focus on consuming balanced, nutritious meals to support physical recovery and restore energy levels.
  • Avoid Alcohol & Caffeine: Strictly avoid alcohol, which can trigger relapse or interact dangerously with medications. Limit caffeine, as it can exacerbate anxiety and sleep disturbances.
  • Small, Frequent Meals: If experiencing nausea or poor appetite, try eating small, bland, frequent meals throughout the day.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Monitor for Worsening: Watch closely for signs of worsening withdrawal, severe confusion, seizures, respiratory distress, or unresponsiveness.
  • Medication Adherence: Assist the patient in understanding and adhering to their prescribed medication regimen and follow-up appointments.
  • Emergency Protocol: Know when to call emergency services (911) for severe symptoms like loss of consciousness, difficulty breathing, or prolonged seizures.
  • Supportive Environment: Provide a calm, non-judgmental, and supportive environment to encourage recovery and treatment engagement.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Follow-up is Paramount: Emphasize that attending all scheduled follow-up appointments with addiction specialists, therapists, or primary care is critical for long-term recovery.
  • Medication-Assisted Treatment (MAT): Discuss the benefits and availability of MAT options (e.g., buprenorphine, naltrexone, acamprosate) as an evidence-based approach to reduce cravings and prevent relapse.
  • Relapse Prevention: Reiterate that relapse is a common part of the recovery process, not a failure. Encourage immediate re-engagement with treatment if relapse occurs.
  • Harm Reduction Strategies: Advise on harm reduction techniques, such as never using alone, using clean needles, and knowing the signs of overdose.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Comprehensive Assessment: Conduct thorough substance use history, withdrawal assessment using validated tools (e.g., CIWA-Ar for alcohol, COWS for opioids), and mental health screening.
  • Patient Education: Provide clear, concise education on withdrawal symptom management, overdose recognition, naloxone administration, and the importance of follow-up care.
  • Resource Navigation: Facilitate warm handoffs or provide detailed information on local SUD treatment programs, support groups (e.g., AA, NA), and mental health services.
  • Therapeutic Communication: Utilize non-judgmental, empathetic, and motivational interviewing techniques to build rapport and encourage patient engagement in their recovery journey.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • DSM-5 Criteria: Diagnosis of Substance Use Disorder is based on specific criteria outlined in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 5th Edition.
  • Pharmacotherapy: Medications used in SUD treatment include opioid agonists/antagonists (e.g., methadone, buprenorphine, naltrexone), benzodiazepines for alcohol withdrawal, and disulfiram.
  • Co-occurring Disorders: High prevalence of co-occurring mental health disorders (e.g., depression, anxiety, PTSD) requiring integrated treatment approaches.
  • Social Determinants of Health: Recognize the significant impact of housing instability, unemployment, lack of social support, and trauma on SUD development and recovery outcomes.
