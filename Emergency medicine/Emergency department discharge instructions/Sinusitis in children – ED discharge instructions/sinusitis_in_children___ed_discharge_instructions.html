Sinusitis in children – ED discharge instructions

🏠 Home / Emergency medicine / Emergency department discharge instructions

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Understanding Sinusitis: Sinusitis in children is an inflammation of the sinuses, often following a viral upper respiratory infection. It can be viral or bacterial.
  • ED Evaluation: Your child was evaluated in the Emergency Department for symptoms such as persistent nasal discharge, cough, or facial discomfort.
  • Discharge Plan: The treatment plan involves managing symptoms at home and, if prescribed, completing a course of antibiotics for suspected bacterial infection.
  • Importance of Follow-up: It is crucial to follow up with your child's primary care provider or pediatrician as instructed to monitor recovery and address any ongoing concerns.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Persistent Nasal Discharge: Thick, colored (yellow or green) nasal discharge that lasts for more than 10 days or worsens.
  • Cough: Daytime and nighttime cough, which may be worse at night.
  • Facial Pain/Pressure: Discomfort or tenderness around the eyes, cheeks, or forehead, especially in older children.
  • Fever: Recurrent or sustained fever above 100.4°F (38°C) after initial improvement, or high fever with purulent nasal discharge for at least 3 consecutive days.
  • Headache: Worsening or severe headache, particularly if accompanied by other concerning symptoms.
  • Eye Swelling/Redness: Swelling, redness, or pain around the eyes, or any changes in vision.
  • Lethargy/Irritability: Increased drowsiness, difficulty waking, or unusual irritability or confusion.
  • New or Worsening Symptoms: Any new symptoms or a significant worsening of existing symptoms after discharge.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Medication Adherence: Administer all prescribed medications, including antibiotics, pain relievers, or nasal sprays, exactly as directed by the physician. Complete the full course of antibiotics even if symptoms improve.
  • Adequate Hydration: Ensure your child drinks plenty of fluids (water, clear broths, diluted juices) to help thin mucus and prevent dehydration.
  • Humidification: Use a cool-mist humidifier in your child's room, especially at night, to help soothe irritated airways and loosen secretions.
  • Avoid Irritants: Protect your child from exposure to cigarette smoke, strong chemical fumes, and known allergens that can exacerbate sinus inflammation.
  • Nasal Hygiene: Gentle saline nasal rinses or sprays can help clear nasal passages and reduce congestion. Follow product instructions for age-appropriate use.
  • When to Seek Immediate Care: Return to the Emergency Department immediately if your child develops severe headache, stiff neck, vision changes, swelling around the eyes, altered mental status, or persistent high fever.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Encourage Fluids: Offer frequent small sips of water, clear broths, diluted fruit juices, or oral rehydration solutions to maintain hydration and thin mucus.
  • Soft, Bland Foods: Provide soft, easy-to-swallow foods like soups, yogurt, mashed potatoes, or applesauce if your child's appetite is reduced.
  • Avoid Dehydrating Beverages: Limit sugary drinks and caffeinated beverages, which can contribute to dehydration.
  • No Specific Food Restrictions: Generally, there are no specific dietary restrictions for sinusitis unless your child has other underlying conditions or allergies.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Close Monitoring: Continuously observe your child for any signs of worsening condition or the development of new, concerning symptoms.
  • Medication Safety: Store all medications safely out of reach of children and administer only as prescribed.
  • Emergency Contacts: Keep a list of emergency contact numbers, including your pediatrician and the ED, readily accessible.
  • Trust Your Instincts: If you have any concerns about your child's health or feel their condition is deteriorating, do not hesitate to seek immediate medical attention.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Viral vs. Bacterial Distinction: Most cases of acute sinusitis in children are viral and resolve with supportive care. Antibiotics are reserved for cases strongly suggestive of bacterial infection.
  • Watchful Waiting: For uncomplicated viral sinusitis, a period of watchful waiting with symptomatic treatment is often appropriate before considering antibiotics.
  • Antibiotic Compliance: If antibiotics are prescribed, emphasize the critical importance of completing the entire course to prevent recurrence or resistance, even if symptoms improve quickly.
  • Complication Awareness: While rare, be vigilant for signs of potential complications such as orbital cellulitis (infection around the eye) or intracranial spread, which require urgent intervention.
  • Follow-up for Resolution: Stress the necessity of follow-up with the primary care provider to ensure complete resolution of symptoms and to reassess if symptoms persist or worsen.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Discharge Education: Provide clear, concise, and culturally sensitive discharge instructions to parents/guardians, ensuring understanding of medication administration, symptom monitoring, and red flag signs.
  • Assessment for Red Flags: Educate parents on specific signs of worsening infection or complications, such as periorbital edema, severe localized pain, vision changes, or neurological symptoms, which necessitate immediate re-evaluation.
  • Comfort Measures Teaching: Instruct on effective non-pharmacological comfort measures, including proper saline nasal irrigation techniques, appropriate use of humidifiers, and ensuring adequate fluid intake.
  • Documentation: Meticulously document all discharge teaching provided, including medications prescribed, follow-up instructions, and the parent/guardian's demonstrated understanding.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Acute Bacterial Rhinosinusitis (ABRS) Criteria: Diagnosed clinically by persistent symptoms (nasal discharge, cough) lasting >10 days without improvement, or severe onset (high fever >39°C and purulent nasal discharge for >3 consecutive days), or worsening symptoms after initial improvement ('double sickening').
  • Diagnostic Imaging: Routine imaging (e.g., CT scan) is generally not recommended for uncomplicated ABRS due to radiation exposure and typically does not alter management. It is reserved for suspected complications or atypical presentations.
  • Antibiotic Selection: First-line antibiotic for ABRS in children is typically high-dose amoxicillin or amoxicillin-clavulanate, with consideration for local resistance patterns and penicillin allergy.
  • Potential Complications: Rare but serious complications include orbital cellulitis, subperiosteal abscess, intracranial complications (e.g., meningitis, epidural abscess), and Pott's puffy tumor (osteomyelitis of the frontal bone).
