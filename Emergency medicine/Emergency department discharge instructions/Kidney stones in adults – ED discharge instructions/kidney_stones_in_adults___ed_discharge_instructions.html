Kidney stones in adults – ED discharge instructions

🏠 Home / Emergency medicine / Emergency department discharge instructions

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Stone Passage: Most kidney stones 5mm or less will pass spontaneously within 4-6 weeks. Larger stones may require further intervention.
  • Pain Management: You have been prescribed medication to manage pain. Take as directed and do not exceed the recommended dose.
  • Hydration is Key: Drink plenty of fluids (2-3 liters per day) to help flush the stone through your urinary system. Water is best.
  • Strain Your Urine: You have been provided with a urine strainer. Use it to catch any passed stones for analysis, which helps prevent future stones.
  • Follow-Up: It is crucial to schedule a follow-up appointment with a urologist or your primary care physician as instructed before discharge.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Severe or Worsening Pain: Uncontrolled pain despite medication, or pain that shifts to a new location and becomes more intense.
  • Fever and Chills: These can indicate a urinary tract infection (UTI) or a more serious kidney infection, especially if accompanied by flank pain.
  • Inability to Urinate: Complete blockage of urine flow is an emergency and requires immediate medical attention.
  • Persistent Nausea or Vomiting: If you cannot keep fluids or medications down, you risk dehydration and inadequate pain control.
  • New or Worsening Blood in Urine: While some blood is expected, a significant increase or new onset of gross hematuria should be reported.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Medication Adherence: Take all prescribed medications (pain relievers, alpha-blockers) exactly as directed to facilitate stone passage and manage symptoms.
  • Avoid Strenuous Activity: Limit heavy lifting or intense exercise that could exacerbate pain or dislodge the stone abruptly in a painful manner.
  • Monitor Urine Output: Ensure you are urinating regularly and that the volume is adequate. Report any significant decrease in urine output.
  • Return to ED Criteria: Seek immediate medical attention if you experience uncontrolled pain, fever/chills, inability to urinate, or persistent vomiting.
  • Avoid Driving Under Influence: Do not drive or operate heavy machinery while taking opioid pain medications due to potential drowsiness and impaired judgment.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Increase Fluid Intake: Aim for 2.5 to 3 liters of fluid daily, primarily water, to dilute urine and prevent stone formation.
  • Limit Sodium: Reduce intake of high-sodium foods (processed foods, canned goods) as high sodium can increase calcium in urine.
  • Moderate Animal Protein: Excessive animal protein can increase uric acid and calcium levels in urine. Consume in moderation.
  • Calcium Intake: Do not restrict calcium unless specifically advised by your doctor. Adequate dietary calcium can actually help prevent certain stone types.
  • Oxalate-Rich Foods (if applicable): If your stone type is calcium oxalate, your doctor may advise limiting foods high in oxalate like spinach, rhubarb, nuts, and chocolate.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Observe for Warning Signs: Be vigilant for any signs of worsening pain, fever, chills, or difficulty urinating in the patient.
  • Medication Assistance: Help the patient adhere to their medication schedule, especially for pain relief, and ensure they do not overdose.
  • Hydration Support: Encourage and assist the patient in maintaining a high fluid intake throughout the day.
  • Transportation Readiness: Be prepared to transport the patient back to the Emergency Department if any of the urgent warning signs develop.
  • Strain Urine: Remind and assist the patient in straining all urine to catch the stone for analysis.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Importance of Follow-Up: Adhering to your scheduled urology follow-up is critical for stone analysis, imaging, and long-term prevention strategies.
  • Stone Analysis: Identifying the composition of your stone is paramount for tailoring specific dietary and medical preventative measures.
  • Lifestyle Modifications: Beyond immediate management, sustained changes in diet and hydration are key to reducing recurrence risk.
  • Recurrence Risk: Be aware that having one kidney stone significantly increases your risk of developing another. Proactive prevention is vital.
  • Medication Review: Discuss all current medications with your urologist, as some can contribute to stone formation.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Patient Education Reinforcement: Emphasize the importance of pain management, adequate hydration, and urine straining techniques.
  • Discharge Planning: Ensure the patient understands all discharge instructions, including medication schedules, follow-up appointments, and red flag symptoms.
  • Pain Assessment: Educate the patient on using a pain scale to effectively communicate their pain level and the effectiveness of analgesics.
  • Fluid Balance Monitoring: Instruct patients to monitor their urine output and color, reporting any significant changes.
  • Medication Side Effects: Review potential side effects of prescribed medications (e.g., dizziness with alpha-blockers, GI upset with NSAIDs) and when to report them.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Common Stone Types: Calcium oxalate (most common), calcium phosphate, uric acid, struvite, cystine. Analysis guides prevention.
  • Alpha-Blockers: Medications like tamsulosin (Flomax) relax ureteral smooth muscle, facilitating stone passage, particularly for distal ureteral stones.
  • NSAIDs vs. Opioids: NSAIDs (e.g., ketorolac, ibuprofen) are often first-line for renal colic due to anti-inflammatory effects; opioids for severe, refractory pain.
  • Imaging Follow-up: KUB (Kidney, Ureter, Bladder X-ray) or low-dose CT scan may be used post-discharge to monitor stone progression or confirm passage.
  • Metabolic Workup: For recurrent stone formers, a 24-hour urine collection and blood tests are often performed to identify metabolic abnormalities contributing to stone formation.
