Shoulder pain

🏠 Home / Emergency medicine / Injuries, first aid, and CPR for children

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Acute Injury Focus: Shoulder pain in children often stems from acute trauma like falls, sports injuries, or direct impact, necessitating prompt evaluation.
  • Growth Plate Vulnerability: Children's bones have growth plates (epiphyseal plates) which are weaker than ligaments and tendons, making them susceptible to specific fractures.
  • Common Mechanisms: Injuries can result from a direct blow, a fall onto an outstretched arm, or a traction injury (e.g., 'nursemaid's elbow' can sometimes present with referred shoulder pain, though less common for primary shoulder injury).
  • Emergency Presentation: Severe pain, inability to move the arm, visible deformity, or signs of neurovascular compromise require immediate emergency medical attention.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Localized Pain: Sharp or dull pain in the shoulder area, often worsening with movement.
  • Swelling and Bruising: Visible inflammation or discoloration around the shoulder joint or clavicle.
  • Limited Range of Motion: Difficulty or inability to lift, rotate, or move the affected arm.
  • Deformity: A visible change in the contour or alignment of the shoulder or collarbone.
  • Tenderness to Palpation: Pain upon touching specific areas of the shoulder or surrounding structures.
  • Clicking or Popping: Sounds heard or felt during movement, potentially indicating a dislocation or fracture.
  • Numbness or Tingling: Sensation changes in the arm or hand, suggesting nerve involvement.
  • Child's Distress: Crying, guarding the arm, refusing to use the arm, or general irritability.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Immobilization: Gently support the injured arm in a comfortable position, ideally using a sling or by holding it against the body, to prevent further movement.
  • Avoid Manipulation: Do not attempt to straighten, push, or pull on the child's arm or shoulder, as this can worsen the injury.
  • Ice Application: Apply a cold pack (wrapped in cloth) to the injured area for 15-20 minutes at a time to reduce swelling and pain.
  • Neurovascular Checks: Regularly assess the child's hand and fingers for warmth, color, sensation, and ability to move, indicating adequate blood flow and nerve function.
  • Pain Management: Administer age-appropriate over-the-counter pain relief (e.g., acetaminophen or ibuprofen) if safe and available, but avoid excessive dosing.
  • Prevent Further Injury: Keep the child calm and still to minimize movement of the injured shoulder until medical professionals can assess it.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • NPO Status Consideration: If surgical intervention or sedation for imaging (e.g., MRI) is anticipated, the child may need to be NPO (nothing by mouth) for a specified period.
  • Hydration Maintenance: Ensure the child remains adequately hydrated with clear fluids, especially if in pain or distressed, unless NPO orders are in place.
  • Light Diet Post-Analgesia: If pain medication causes nausea, offer small, bland, easily digestible foods like crackers or toast once cleared by medical staff.
  • Avoidance of Heavy Meals: Refrain from offering large or fatty meals, particularly if the child is experiencing pain or has received pain medication that might induce nausea.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Immediate Emergency Care: Seek urgent medical attention for any visible deformity, severe pain, open wounds, inability to move the arm, or signs of nerve/blood vessel compromise.
  • Accurate History: Provide medical staff with a detailed account of how the injury occurred, including the time, mechanism, and any initial symptoms observed.
  • Medication Disclosure: Inform healthcare providers of any medications the child has received, including over-the-counter pain relievers, and any known allergies.
  • Child Abuse Screening: Be aware that inconsistent injury mechanisms or unexplained injuries may prompt a medical evaluation for non-accidental trauma.
  • Do Not Self-Diagnose: Avoid attempting to diagnose the injury yourself; professional medical assessment is crucial for proper management.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Comprehensive Assessment: A thorough physical examination, including inspection, palpation, range of motion, and neurovascular assessment, is paramount.
  • Imaging Modalities: Radiographs (X-rays) are typically the first-line imaging to identify fractures or dislocations; ultrasound or MRI may be used for soft tissue injuries or complex cases.
  • Pain Management Strategy: Implement an effective pain control regimen, often starting with NSAIDs or acetaminophen, potentially escalating to stronger analgesics if needed.
  • Orthopedic Consultation: Referrals to pediatric orthopedics are indicated for displaced fractures, dislocations requiring reduction, or complex soft tissue injuries.
  • Growth Plate Injury Awareness: Always consider the potential for Salter-Harris fractures involving the growth plates in pediatric patients.
  • Non-Accidental Trauma Consideration: Maintain a high index of suspicion for non-accidental trauma in cases where the history is inconsistent with the injury or in very young children.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Pediatric Pain Scales: Utilize age-appropriate pain assessment tools such as FLACC (Face, Legs, Activity, Cry, Consolability) for non-verbal children or Wong-Baker FACES for verbal children.
  • Neurovascular Monitoring: Perform frequent neurovascular checks (the 5 P's: Pain, Pallor, Pulselessness, Paresthesia, Paralysis) on the affected extremity.
  • Immobilization Techniques: Demonstrate and educate parents on the correct application and care of slings, splints, or other immobilization devices.
  • Parental Education: Provide clear instructions on home care, medication administration, signs of worsening condition, and when to return for follow-up.
  • Documentation Accuracy: Meticulously document the injury mechanism, assessment findings, interventions, pain scores, and patient/parent education provided.
  • Child-Friendly Environment: Create a calm and reassuring environment to minimize anxiety for both the child and their caregivers during assessment and treatment.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Common Pediatric Shoulder Injuries: Clavicle fractures, proximal humerus fractures, shoulder dislocations (less common in young children, more in adolescents), growth plate injuries (Salter-Harris classification).
  • Differential Diagnoses: Septic arthritis, osteomyelitis, referred pain from cervical spine, brachial plexus injury, soft tissue contusion, sprain.
  • Diagnostic Modalities: Standard radiographs (AP, lateral, Y-view shoulder), ultrasound (for soft tissue, rotator cuff, effusions), MRI (for complex soft tissue, labral tears, occult fractures).
  • Management Principles: RICE (Rest, Ice, Compression, Elevation), immobilization, analgesia, closed reduction (for dislocations), surgical fixation (for unstable or displaced fractures).
  • Anatomical Considerations: Understanding the unique anatomy of the pediatric shoulder girdle, including the unfused epiphyses and apophyses.
  • Complications: Malunion, nonunion, growth arrest, avascular necrosis, recurrent dislocation, nerve damage.
