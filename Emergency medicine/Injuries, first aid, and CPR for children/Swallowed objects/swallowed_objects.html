Topic

Swallowed objects

🏠 Home / Emergency medicine / Injuries, first aid, and CPR for children

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Emergency Situation: Swallowing a foreign object, especially in children, is a medical emergency requiring immediate attention due to potential airway obstruction or damage to the gastrointestinal tract.
  • Common Culprits: Small toys, coins, button batteries, magnets, small food items (e.g., grapes, hot dogs), and sharp objects are frequently ingested by curious children.
  • Airway vs. Esophagus: Objects lodged in the airway cause immediate choking and respiratory distress, while those in the esophagus may present with difficulty swallowing, drooling, or chest pain.
  • Hidden Dangers: Some objects, like button batteries, can cause severe chemical burns and tissue necrosis within hours, even if they pass the airway.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Choking & Gagging: Sudden onset of coughing, gagging, difficulty breathing, or inability to speak, indicating potential airway obstruction.
  • Dysphagia & Drooling: Difficulty or pain when swallowing, excessive drooling, refusal to eat, or vomiting after attempts to eat or drink.
  • Chest or Abdominal Pain: Localized pain in the chest or abdomen, which may suggest the object is lodged in the esophagus or has reached the stomach.
  • Respiratory Distress: Wheezing, stridor, cyanosis (bluish discoloration), or rapid, shallow breathing if the object is in the airway or causing tracheal compression.
  • Irritability & Lethargy: Non-specific signs in infants or young children who cannot verbalize discomfort, often accompanied by changes in feeding patterns.
  • Bloody Vomit or Stool: Later signs indicating potential mucosal injury, ulceration, or perforation, especially with sharp objects or corrosive batteries.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Childproofing Homes: Regularly inspect play areas for small objects, loose parts from toys, and household items that could be ingested.
  • Supervision: Maintain constant, vigilant supervision of infants and young children, especially during playtime and meals.
  • Age-Appropriate Toys: Ensure all toys are suitable for the child's age, checking for small, detachable components that pose a choking hazard.
  • Food Preparation: Cut food into small, manageable pieces for young children, avoiding whole grapes, nuts, hot dogs, or large chunks of meat.
  • Battery & Magnet Safety: Store all button batteries and small magnets securely out of reach. Be aware of products containing these items (e.g., remote controls, small toys).
  • Secure Storage: Keep medications, cleaning supplies, and other potentially harmful small items in locked cabinets.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Immediate NPO Status: If a foreign body ingestion is suspected or confirmed, the patient should be kept NPO (nil per os - nothing by mouth) immediately to prevent further aspiration or movement of the object.
  • Pre-Procedure Fasting: Adhere strictly to NPO guidelines (typically 6-8 hours for solids, 2 hours for clear liquids) if endoscopic removal or surgical intervention is anticipated.
  • Post-Procedure Reintroduction: Following successful removal, clear liquids may be gradually introduced, progressing to soft foods as tolerated and per physician's orders, monitoring for any signs of discomfort or complications.
  • Preventative Food Modification: For young children, avoid hard candies, chewing gum, whole nuts, popcorn, and large, round foods that can easily become lodged in the airway.
  • Hydration for Passage:1 In select cases where a small, blunt object is expected to pass spontaneously, clear liquids might be permitted under strict medical supervision to aid passage, but never without physician approval.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Immediate Emergency Call: Call emergency services (e.g., 911) immediately if a child is choking, has difficulty breathing, or if a button battery or magnet is suspected to be swallowed.
  • First Aid for Choking: For conscious infants, administer 5 back blows followed by 5 chest thrusts. For conscious children/adults, perform the Heimlich maneuver.
  • Do NOT Induce Vomiting: Never attempt to induce vomiting, as this can cause the object to become lodged in the airway or cause further injury to the esophagus.
  • Observe & Monitor: If the child is not choking but an object is suspected, monitor closely for symptoms like drooling, difficulty swallowing, vomiting, or abdominal pain, and seek urgent medical evaluation.
  • Provide Object Details: If possible, identify the type, size, and number of objects swallowed, and the approximate time of ingestion, to assist medical professionals.
  • Avoid Blind Sweeps: Do not perform blind finger sweeps in the mouth of a choking child, as this can push the object further down the airway.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Prompt Diagnosis is Key: Rapid assessment, including a detailed history and appropriate imaging (e.g., X-ray, CT scan), is crucial for localizing the object and determining its nature.
  • High-Risk Objects: Button batteries and multiple magnets are considered medical emergencies due to their potential for severe tissue damage (corrosion, fistula formation) and should be removed urgently.
  • Endoscopic Intervention: Most esophageal foreign bodies require endoscopic removal. The choice of endoscope (flexible vs. rigid) depends on the object and patient's age.
  • Observation vs. Removal: Small, blunt objects (e.g., coins) that have passed into the stomach may be observed for spontaneous passage, but close follow-up is essential.
  • Complication Awareness: Be vigilant for complications such as esophageal perforation, stricture formation, aspiration pneumonia, or mediastinitis, which can arise from delayed diagnosis or difficult removal.
  • Prevention Counseling: Educate parents and caregivers on effective childproofing strategies and the dangers of common household items.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Thorough Assessment: Conduct a comprehensive nursing assessment, including vital signs, respiratory status, hydration, and pain level, along with a detailed history from caregivers.
  • Patient & Family Education: Provide clear, empathetic education to parents regarding the condition, planned interventions, potential risks, and post-procedure care instructions.
  • Preparation for Procedures: Ensure the patient is NPO, obtain informed consent, prepare the patient for imaging (e.g., X-ray, CT), and assist with pre-endoscopy preparations.
  • Post-Procedure Monitoring: Closely monitor for signs of complications such as bleeding, perforation (e.g., fever, increasing pain, subcutaneous emphysema), respiratory distress, or difficulty swallowing.
  • Pain Management: Administer appropriate pain relief as ordered, especially after endoscopic removal, and assess its effectiveness.
  • Documentation: Meticulously document all assessments, interventions, patient responses, and communications with the medical team and family.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Diagnostic Modalities: Plain radiography (AP and lateral views), fluoroscopy, CT scan (for complex cases or suspected complications), and direct endoscopy (diagnostic and therapeutic).
  • Foreign Body Types: Coins, button batteries (lithium disc batteries), magnets, sharp objects (pins, needles), food bolus, small toys, dental appliances.
  • Management Algorithms: Specific protocols for different types of ingested foreign bodies (e.g., urgent removal for batteries/magnets, observation for small blunt objects in stomach).
  • Complications: Esophageal perforation, stricture, fistula formation (tracheoesophageal, aortoesophageal), aspiration pneumonia, mediastinitis, intestinal obstruction, peritonitis.
  • ICD-10 Codes: T18.1 (Foreign body in esophagus), T18.2 (Foreign body in stomach), T18.3 (Foreign body in small intestine), T18.4 (Foreign body in large intestine), T17.x (Foreign body in respiratory tract).
  • Endoscopic Techniques: Grasping forceps, retrieval baskets, snare loops, overtube placement, magnet-tipped catheters.
