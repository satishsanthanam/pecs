Moderate to severe traumatic brain injury

🏠 Home / Emergency medicine / Head injury

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Definition: Moderate to severe traumatic brain injury (TBI) involves significant brain damage, often resulting in prolonged unconsciousness, altered mental status, and potential long-term neurological deficits.
  • Causes: Commonly caused by high-impact events such as motor vehicle accidents, falls from significant heights, assaults, and sports-related injuries.
  • Urgency: Requires immediate emergency medical attention and often critical care management due to the risk of secondary brain injury and life-threatening complications.
  • Prognosis: Outcomes vary widely, ranging from full recovery to permanent disability or death, depending on the severity and location of the injury.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Loss of Consciousness: Prolonged period of unconsciousness, typically lasting more than 30 minutes.
  • Altered Mental Status: Profound confusion, disorientation, agitation, or inability to follow commands.
  • Persistent Headache: Severe headache that worsens over time and is not relieved by standard pain medication.
  • Nausea and Vomiting: Recurrent or projectile vomiting, often indicative of increased intracranial pressure.
  • Seizures: New-onset seizures occurring after the injury.
  • Pupil Changes: Unequal pupil size (anisocoria), pupils unresponsive to light, or dilated pupils.
  • Neurological Deficits: Weakness or numbness on one side of the body, difficulty with balance, slurred speech, or vision changes.
  • Cerebrospinal Fluid Leak: Clear fluid draining from the nose or ears, potentially indicating a skull base fracture.
  • Coma: A state of profound unconsciousness from which the patient cannot be aroused.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Spinal Immobilization: Assume concomitant cervical spine injury until cleared; maintain strict C-spine precautions.
  • Airway Management: Ensure a patent airway; intubation may be necessary to protect the airway and facilitate ventilation.
  • Circulatory Support: Maintain adequate cerebral perfusion pressure by preventing hypotension and optimizing fluid status.
  • Oxygenation: Prevent hypoxia; administer supplemental oxygen to maintain SpO2 > 90%.
  • Neurological Monitoring: Frequent and meticulous neurological assessments, including Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) scoring.
  • Intracranial Pressure (ICP) Management: Implement strategies to prevent and manage elevated ICP, such as head elevation and osmotic agents.
  • Avoid Sedatives: Limit or avoid sedating medications that can mask neurological changes, unless medically necessary for ICP control.
  • Temperature Control: Prevent hyperthermia, which can worsen secondary brain injury.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Initial NPO Status: Patients with moderate to severe TBI are typically kept Nil Per Os (NPO) initially due to altered consciousness, risk of aspiration, and potential for surgical intervention.
  • Early Enteral Nutrition: Initiate enteral nutrition (tube feeding) as soon as feasible (within 24-72 hours) to meet high metabolic demands, prevent catabolism, and support recovery.
  • Fluid Management: Careful fluid balance is critical to prevent cerebral edema while maintaining adequate systemic perfusion; avoid hypotonic fluids.
  • Dysphagia Assessment: Prior to any oral intake, a comprehensive swallowing assessment by a speech-language pathologist is mandatory once the patient is more alert and stable.
  • Diet Progression: If oral intake is deemed safe, progress diet slowly from thickened liquids to pureed, then soft foods, monitoring closely for aspiration.
  • Nutritional Requirements: TBI patients have increased caloric and protein needs; ensure adequate macro and micronutrient delivery via enteral or parenteral routes.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Call Emergency Services: Immediately dial emergency services (e.g., 911) and clearly state the nature of the injury.
  • Do Not Move Patient: Unless absolutely necessary for safety (e.g., immediate danger), do not move the injured person to prevent further spinal or brain damage.
  • Stabilize Head and Neck: Gently hold the head and neck in a neutral position to minimize movement until paramedics arrive.
  • Monitor Breathing: Check for breathing and pulse; if absent and trained, begin CPR.
  • Control Bleeding: Apply direct pressure to any bleeding wounds on the head, avoiding direct pressure if a skull fracture is suspected.
  • Observe for Changes: Note any changes in consciousness, pupil size, or seizure activity to report to medical personnel.
  • Do Not Offer Food/Drink: Do not give the injured person anything to eat or drink.
  • Stay with Patient: Remain with the patient until emergency medical personnel take over.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Rapid Assessment: Prioritize a rapid and systematic assessment of airway, breathing, circulation, and neurological status (ABCDE approach).
  • Imaging: Urgent CT scan of the head is the cornerstone for diagnosing intracranial lesions and guiding immediate management.
  • ICP Management: Aggressive management of intracranial pressure (ICP) is paramount to prevent secondary brain injury.
  • Neurosurgical Consultation: Early consultation with neurosurgery is essential for patients with mass lesions requiring surgical evacuation or depressed skull fractures.
  • Prognosis Discussion: Engage in sensitive and realistic discussions with families regarding the severity of injury and potential long-term outcomes.
  • Rehabilitation Planning: Initiate early rehabilitation planning, as multidisciplinary rehabilitation is crucial for optimizing functional recovery.
  • Prevent Secondary Insults: Vigilantly monitor and manage factors like hypoxia, hypotension, hyperthermia, and hyperglycemia, which can worsen brain injury.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • GCS Assessment: Master accurate and consistent Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) scoring and trending to monitor neurological status.
  • Neurological Checks: Perform frequent and thorough neurological assessments, including pupil reactivity, motor response, and level of consciousness.
  • ICP Monitoring: Understand the principles of intracranial pressure (ICP) monitoring, waveform interpretation, and nursing interventions to manage elevated ICP.
  • Ventilator Management: Collaborate with respiratory therapy on ventilator settings to optimize oxygenation and CO2 levels, impacting cerebral blood flow.
  • Aspiration Precautions: Implement strict aspiration precautions, especially during feeding or medication administration, for patients with impaired swallow reflexes.
  • Family Support: Provide empathetic support and clear communication to families, explaining complex medical information and involving them in care decisions.
  • Medication Administration: Administer neuroprotective medications, osmotic agents, and seizure prophylaxis as prescribed, understanding their mechanisms and side effects.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Pathophysiology: Primary injury (contusions, lacerations, diffuse axonal injury, hematomas) vs. secondary injury (ischemia, edema, inflammation, excitotoxicity).
  • Diagnostic Criteria: GCS score of 3-12 (moderate: 9-12, severe: 3-8) within 48 hours of injury, coupled with imaging findings.
  • Imaging Modalities: Initial non-contrast CT head; MRI for detecting subtle lesions, diffuse axonal injury, and for prognostic assessment.
  • Management Guidelines: Adherence to Brain Trauma Foundation (BTF) guidelines for severe TBI management, including ICP targets and CPP goals.
  • Pharmacological Interventions: Osmotic agents (mannitol, hypertonic saline), sedatives/analgesics, neuromuscular blockers, antiepileptics.
  • Surgical Interventions: Craniotomy for evacuation of epidural/subdural hematomas, decompressive craniectomy for refractory ICP.
  • Complications: Post-traumatic epilepsy, hydrocephalus, neuroendocrine dysfunction, cognitive and behavioral deficits, spasticity.
  • Rehabilitation Phases: Acute rehabilitation, post-acute rehabilitation, and long-term community reintegration.
