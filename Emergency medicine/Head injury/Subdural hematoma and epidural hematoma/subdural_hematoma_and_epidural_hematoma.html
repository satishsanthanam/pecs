Subdural hematoma and epidural hematoma

🏠 Home / Emergency medicine / Head injury

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Subdural Hematoma (SDH): Bleeding between the dura mater and arachnoid mater, typically venous in origin, often with a slower onset of symptoms.
  • Epidural Hematoma (EDH): Bleeding between the dura mater and the skull, commonly arterial (e.g., middle meningeal artery), characterized by rapid symptom progression.
  • Traumatic Origin: Both types are usually a consequence of head trauma, leading to blood accumulation that compresses brain tissue.
  • Clinical Urgency: Both conditions are medical emergencies requiring prompt diagnosis and intervention to prevent severe neurological damage or death.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Severe Headache: Often persistent and worsening.
  • Altered Mental Status: Ranging from confusion and disorientation to lethargy, stupor, or coma.
  • Nausea and Vomiting: Common signs of increased intracranial pressure.
  • Neurological Deficits: Weakness or numbness on one side of the body (hemiparesis), speech difficulties (aphasia), or visual disturbances.
  • Pupillary Changes: Unequal pupil size (anisocoria) or a fixed and dilated pupil, often on the side of the hematoma.
  • Seizures: Can occur due to irritation of the brain cortex.
  • Lucid Interval (EDH): A temporary period of consciousness after initial trauma, followed by rapid neurological decline, characteristic of epidural hematomas.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Immediate Medical Evaluation: Any head injury, especially with loss of consciousness, confusion, or persistent symptoms, necessitates urgent emergency medical assessment.
  • Avoid Anticoagulants Post-Injury: If a head injury is suspected, avoid administering blood-thinning medications unless medically directed, as they can exacerbate intracranial bleeding.
  • Neurological Monitoring: Close observation for changes in consciousness, pupil size, motor function, and speech is critical following any head trauma.
  • Fall Prevention Strategies: Implement measures to prevent head injuries, particularly in individuals at high risk such as the elderly or those on anticoagulant therapy.
  • Protective Headgear: Always use appropriate helmets during activities that pose a risk of head injury, such as cycling, motorcycling, or contact sports.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • NPO Status Pre-Procedure: Patients with suspected or confirmed intracranial hematomas are typically kept NPO (nothing by mouth) in anticipation of potential emergency surgical intervention.
  • Intravenous Hydration: Intravenous fluids are administered to maintain hydration and electrolyte balance, especially when oral intake is restricted.
  • Gradual Diet Progression Post-Surgery: Following surgical evacuation and neurological stabilization, diet is gradually advanced from clear liquids to full liquids and then soft foods as tolerated, ensuring a patent gag reflex.
  • Avoid Substances Masking Symptoms: Post-head injury, avoid alcohol or sedatives that can obscure neurological changes or impair cognitive assessment.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Minimize Patient Movement: In cases of severe head injury, ensure the patient's head and neck are stabilized until emergency medical personnel arrive.
  • Diligent Observation and Reporting: Attendants must meticulously observe and immediately report any changes in the patient's level of consciousness, speech, motor function, or pupil size to medical staff.
  • Maintain a Calm Environment: Reduce external stimuli to prevent agitation, which can potentially increase intracranial pressure.
  • Strict Adherence to Instructions: Follow all medical instructions precisely regarding patient positioning, medication administration, and ongoing monitoring.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Rapid Diagnostic Imaging: An emergent non-contrast CT scan of the head is the cornerstone for differentiating subdural from epidural hematomas and assessing their size, location, and mass effect.
  • Surgical Intervention Criteria: Many acute SDH and EDH cases necessitate urgent surgical evacuation (e.g., craniotomy) to decompress the brain and prevent herniation.
  • Intensive Neurological Monitoring: Continuous assessment of Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), pupillary responses, and motor function is paramount for guiding management.
  • Intracranial Pressure Management: Strategies include head elevation, judicious use of osmotic agents (e.g., mannitol), and controlled ventilation to maintain cerebral perfusion pressure.
  • Prognostic Factors: Outcomes depend significantly on the hematoma's size, location, patient's age, pre-existing comorbidities, and the timeliness of intervention.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Mastering Neurological Assessment: Develop proficiency in performing frequent and systematic neurological assessments, including the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS).
  • Pupillary Assessment Significance: Understand the clinical implications of anisocoria, sluggish, or fixed pupils in the context of head injury.
  • ICP Monitoring Principles: Familiarize with the principles of intracranial pressure monitoring, waveform interpretation, and nursing interventions to manage elevated ICP.
  • Post-Craniotomy Care: Focus on meticulous wound care, pain management, seizure precautions, and vigilant monitoring for post-operative complications such as infection or re-bleeding.
  • Patient and Family Education: Educate patients and their families on recognizing signs of neurological deterioration and the importance of adherence to follow-up care plans.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Pathophysiology Distinction: SDH typically results from tearing of bridging veins, while EDH commonly involves arterial bleeding, often from the middle meningeal artery.
  • Imaging Characteristics: On CT, SDH appears as a crescent-shaped collection, conforming to the brain's surface, whereas EDH presents as a lenticular (lens-shaped) collection.
  • Management Modalities: Treatment ranges from conservative management for small, stable hematomas to emergent surgical evacuation (craniotomy, burr holes) for significant mass effect or neurological decline.
  • Potential Complications: Include brain herniation, seizures, hydrocephalus, long-term neurological deficits, and mortality.
  • Anticoagulation Reversal: For patients on anticoagulants, rapid reversal of their effects (e.g., with Vitamin K, prothrombin complex concentrates) is often critical.
