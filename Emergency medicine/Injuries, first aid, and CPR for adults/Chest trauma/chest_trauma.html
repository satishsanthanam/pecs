Chest trauma

🏠 Home / Emergency medicine / Injuries, first aid, and CPR for adults

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Definition: Chest trauma refers to any injury to the chest wall, lungs, heart, great vessels, esophagus, or diaphragm.
  • Causes: Can be blunt (e.g., motor vehicle accidents, falls, direct impact) or penetrating (e.g., stab wounds, gunshot wounds).
  • Severity: Ranges from minor contusions to life-threatening conditions like tension pneumothorax, massive hemothorax, cardiac tamponade, or aortic rupture.
  • Urgency: Requires immediate medical evaluation due to the potential for rapid deterioration and high mortality if not promptly managed.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Chest Pain: Often severe, localized, and may worsen with breathing or movement (pleuritic pain).
  • Dyspnea: Shortness of breath, difficulty breathing, or rapid, shallow breathing (tachypnea).
  • Bruising/Deformity: Visible contusions, abrasions, or paradoxical chest wall movement (flail chest).
  • Crepitus: A crackling sensation under the skin due to subcutaneous emphysema (air trapped in tissues).
  • Hemoptysis: Coughing up blood, indicating lung injury.
  • Hypotension/Tachycardia: Signs of shock, suggesting significant blood loss or cardiac compromise.
  • Tracheal Deviation: Shift of the trachea away from the injured side, a critical sign of tension pneumothorax.
  • Distended Neck Veins: May indicate increased intrathoracic pressure or cardiac tamponade.
  • Diminished Breath Sounds: On the affected side, suggesting pneumothorax or hemothorax.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Scene Safety: Ensure the environment is safe for both the patient and first responders before approaching.
  • Do Not Remove Impaled Objects: Stabilize any penetrating objects in place to prevent further damage or hemorrhage.
  • Airway Management: Prioritize securing a patent airway, especially if the patient has facial or neck trauma.
  • Spinal Immobilization: Assume concomitant spinal injury until ruled out, especially in high-impact blunt trauma.
  • Monitor for Tension Pneumothorax: Watch for sudden worsening of respiratory distress, hypotension, and tracheal deviation.
  • Open Pneumothorax Management: Apply a three-sided occlusive dressing to any sucking chest wound to prevent tension pneumothorax.
  • Rapid Transport: Expedite transfer to a trauma center for definitive care.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • NPO Status: Maintain nothing by mouth (NPO) immediately upon presentation due to the high likelihood of requiring emergency surgery or intubation.
  • Intravenous Fluids: Administer IV fluids as prescribed to maintain hydration and support hemodynamic stability, especially if there is significant blood loss.
  • Post-Stabilization Diet: Once stable and surgical intervention is ruled out or completed, gradually reintroduce clear liquids, then a soft diet, progressing as tolerated and based on the patient's overall condition and bowel function.
  • Nutritional Support: For prolonged recovery or severe injuries, consider enteral or parenteral nutrition to support healing and prevent malnutrition.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Call Emergency Services: Immediately dial emergency medical services (e.g., 911) and provide clear details of the incident and patient's condition.
  • Maintain Airway: If the patient is unconscious, position them to maintain an open airway, if safe to do so without moving the spine.
  • Control External Bleeding: Apply direct, firm pressure to any visible external bleeding points on the chest with a clean cloth.
  • Monitor Vital Signs: Continuously observe the patient for changes in breathing, level of consciousness, and skin color until professional help arrives.
  • Provide Reassurance: Keep the patient calm and warm, offering comfort and support while awaiting medical personnel.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Primary Survey (ABCDE): Follow ATLS guidelines for rapid assessment and management of life-threatening injuries.
  • Imaging: Utilize chest X-ray, focused assessment with sonography for trauma (FAST) exam, and CT scan of the chest to identify specific injuries.
  • Interventions: Be prepared for emergent procedures such as chest tube insertion for pneumothorax/hemothorax, pericardiocentesis for cardiac tamponade, or thoracotomy for massive hemorrhage.
  • Pain Management: Aggressively manage pain to improve respiratory mechanics and prevent complications like atelectasis and pneumonia.
  • Prophylaxis: Administer tetanus prophylaxis and consider antibiotics for penetrating injuries or open fractures.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Comprehensive Assessment: Perform frequent respiratory assessments, including rate, depth, effort, symmetry, and auscultation of breath sounds.
  • Chest Tube Management: Understand the principles of chest tube insertion, maintenance, and troubleshooting, including monitoring drainage and air leaks.
  • Pain Assessment: Utilize appropriate pain scales and administer analgesia effectively to facilitate deep breathing and coughing.
  • Monitoring for Complications: Vigilantly watch for signs of acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), pneumonia, sepsis, and cardiac arrhythmias.
  • Patient Education: Educate patients on incentive spirometry, deep breathing exercises, and early ambulation to prevent pulmonary complications.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Pneumothorax: Air in the pleural space.
  • Hemothorax: Blood in the pleural space.
  • Flail Chest: Paradoxical movement of a segment of the chest wall due to multiple rib fractures.
  • Cardiac Tamponade: Compression of the heart due to fluid accumulation in the pericardial sac.
  • Pulmonary Contusion: Bruising of the lung tissue.
  • Aortic Disruption: Tear in the aorta, often life-threatening.
  • Thoracostomy: Surgical creation of an opening into the chest cavity, typically for chest tube insertion.
  • Pericardiocentesis: Procedure to remove fluid from the pericardial sac.
  • ATLS: Advanced Trauma Life Support protocol.
