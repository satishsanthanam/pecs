Flank pain

🏠 Home / Emergency medicine / Injuries, first aid, and CPR for adults

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Definition: Flank pain refers to discomfort or pain in the side of the body, typically between the lower ribs and the top of the hip.
  • Common Causes: It can stem from various sources, including kidney stones, urinary tract infections (UTIs), pyelonephritis (kidney infection), muscle strain, or more serious conditions like an abdominal aortic aneurysm.
  • Emergency Indicators: Sudden, severe, or worsening flank pain, especially when accompanied by fever, chills, blood in urine, nausea, vomiting, or dizziness, warrants immediate medical evaluation.
  • Unilateral vs. Bilateral: Pain on one side often points to a unilateral issue (e.g., kidney stone), while bilateral pain might suggest systemic causes or musculoskeletal strain.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Kidney Stones: Intense, colicky pain that may radiate to the groin, nausea, vomiting, hematuria (blood in urine), and dysuria (painful urination).
  • Pyelonephritis: High fever, chills, fatigue, frequent and painful urination, and tenderness in the costovertebral angle (CVA).
  • Muscle Strain/Injury: Localized tenderness, pain exacerbated by movement or specific postures, and a history of recent physical exertion or trauma.
  • Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm (AAA): Sudden, severe, tearing pain in the flank or back, often accompanied by hypotension, tachycardia, and a pulsatile abdominal mass (in cases of rupture).
  • Shingles (Herpes Zoster): Burning, tingling pain followed by a characteristic vesicular rash in a dermatomal pattern, typically unilateral.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Do Not Ignore Severe Pain: Seek immediate emergency medical attention for sudden, excruciating flank pain, especially if accompanied by fever, chills, or signs of shock.
  • Avoid Self-Diagnosis: The causes of flank pain are diverse; self-diagnosing or delaying professional medical evaluation can lead to serious complications.
  • Hydration Status: Maintain adequate hydration, especially if kidney stones are suspected, but avoid excessive fluid intake if there is a known urinary obstruction.
  • Medication Caution: Do not take over-the-counter pain relievers without consulting a healthcare provider, as some can mask symptoms or be contraindicated in certain conditions (e.g., NSAIDs with kidney issues).
  • Monitor for Worsening: Pay close attention to any changes in pain intensity, new symptoms, or signs of infection (e.g., increasing fever, chills).

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Hydration for Kidney Stones: Encourage increased water intake (2-3 liters/day) to help flush the urinary system, unless contraindicated by other medical conditions.
  • Low-Oxalate Diet: For calcium oxalate stones, advise reducing intake of high-oxalate foods like spinach, rhubarb, nuts, chocolate, and tea.
  • Sodium Restriction: A low-sodium diet may help reduce calcium excretion in the urine, potentially beneficial for some types of kidney stones.
  • Bland Diet for Nausea: If experiencing nausea or vomiting, suggest small, frequent meals of bland foods (e.g., crackers, toast, clear broths) to maintain some nutritional intake.
  • Avoid Caffeine/Alcohol: These can be dehydrating and may exacerbate discomfort or urinary symptoms in some cases.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Immediate Medical Evaluation: Any patient presenting with acute, severe flank pain, especially with associated systemic symptoms, requires prompt assessment in an emergency setting.
  • Detailed History Taking: Obtain a comprehensive history including pain characteristics (onset, duration, quality, radiation), associated symptoms, past medical history (e.g., kidney stones, UTIs, cardiovascular disease), and recent trauma.
  • Vital Sign Monitoring: Closely monitor blood pressure, heart rate, respiratory rate, and temperature, as abnormalities can indicate serious underlying conditions like sepsis or hemorrhage.
  • Pain Management: Administer appropriate analgesia as prescribed, but ensure it does not obscure critical diagnostic signs before initial assessment.
  • Prepare for Diagnostics: Anticipate orders for urinalysis, blood tests (CBC, BMP, inflammatory markers), and imaging studies (e.g., ultrasound, CT scan of abdomen/pelvis).

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Broad Differential Diagnosis: Always consider a wide range of etiologies, including renal (stones, infection, infarct), musculoskeletal, gastrointestinal (appendicitis, diverticulitis), vascular (AAA), and dermatological (shingles) causes.
  • Imaging is Key: Computed tomography (CT) scan is often the gold standard for evaluating acute flank pain, especially for suspected nephrolithiasis or vascular emergencies. Ultrasound can be useful, particularly in pregnant patients.
  • Urinalysis Importance: A complete urinalysis is crucial to detect hematuria, pyuria, bacteriuria, and assess urine pH, guiding diagnosis of renal or urinary tract pathology.
  • Aggressive Pain Control: Renal colic is notoriously painful; prompt and effective analgesia is paramount while diagnostic workup proceeds.
  • Referral Considerations: Depending on the diagnosis, timely referral to urology, nephrology, general surgery, or vascular surgery may be necessary.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Comprehensive Pain Assessment: Utilize a standardized pain scale and thoroughly document the location, intensity, character, onset, duration, aggravating, and alleviating factors of the flank pain.
  • Fluid Balance Monitoring: Accurately record intake and output, especially if IV fluids are administered or if there's concern for dehydration or urinary obstruction.
  • Urine Specimen Collection: Ensure proper collection of clean-catch urine specimens for urinalysis and culture to prevent contamination and ensure accurate results.
  • Patient Education on Symptoms: Educate patients on warning signs that necessitate immediate return to the emergency department, such as worsening pain, high fever, or inability to urinate.
  • Post-Procedure Care: If diagnostic procedures (e.g., CT with contrast) are performed, monitor for allergic reactions or kidney function changes.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Nephrolithiasis: The medical term for kidney stones, which are solid masses formed in the kidneys from dietary minerals.
  • Pyelonephritis: An acute bacterial infection of the kidney and renal pelvis, often ascending from the lower urinary tract.
  • Costovertebral Angle (CVA) Tenderness: Pain elicited by percussion over the area of the back overlying the kidney, a common sign in pyelonephritis.
  • Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm (AAA): A localized enlargement of the abdominal aorta, which can rupture and cause life-threatening hemorrhage.
  • Hematuria: The presence of red blood cells in the urine, which can be macroscopic (visible) or microscopic.
