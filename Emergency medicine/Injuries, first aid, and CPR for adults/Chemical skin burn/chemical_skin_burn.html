Chemical skin burn

🏠 Home / Emergency medicine / Injuries, first aid, and CPR for adults

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Definition: Chemical burns occur when skin or eyes come into contact with corrosive substances, leading to tissue damage.
  • Severity Factors: The extent of injury depends on the chemical's type, concentration, duration of contact, and the area of the body affected.
  • Immediate Danger: Rapid intervention is crucial to minimize tissue destruction and prevent systemic absorption of harmful chemicals.
  • Common Agents: Acids (e.g., battery acid, rust removers) and alkalis (e.g., drain cleaners, oven cleaners) are frequent culprits, with alkalis often causing deeper, more insidious damage.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Intense Pain: A burning or stinging sensation at the site of contact, though some chemicals can cause numbness.
  • Skin Discoloration: Redness, blistering, blackening, or a grayish appearance of the skin.
  • Tissue Damage: Visible destruction of skin layers, ranging from superficial irritation to deep necrosis.
  • Blister Formation: Fluid-filled sacs on the skin, indicating partial-thickness injury.
  • Numbness or Tingling: Can occur if nerve endings are damaged or if the chemical has an anesthetic effect.
  • Systemic Symptoms: Dizziness, shortness of breath, headache, or collapse if the chemical is absorbed into the bloodstream.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Immediate Irrigation: Flush the affected area with copious amounts of cool running water for at least 20-30 minutes, or until medical help arrives.
  • Remove Contaminated Items: Quickly remove any clothing, jewelry, or accessories that have come into contact with the chemical.
  • Personal Protection: Responders should wear appropriate Personal Protective Equipment (PPE), such as gloves and eye protection, to prevent secondary exposure.
  • Avoid Neutralizers: Do not attempt to neutralize the chemical with other substances unless specifically instructed by poison control or emergency services, as this can generate heat and worsen the burn.
  • Eye Protection: If eyes are affected, flush them continuously with water while holding the eyelids open, and seek immediate ophthalmological evaluation.
  • Prevent Rubbing: Avoid rubbing the affected area, as this can spread the chemical and increase tissue damage.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Avoid Oral Ingestion: Ensure no chemical substances are ingested orally; if suspected, do not induce vomiting unless advised by poison control.
  • Hydration for Healing: Post-stabilization, adequate hydration is vital for overall recovery and supporting the body's healing processes.
  • Nutrient-Rich Diet: A diet rich in protein, vitamins (especially C and A), and minerals (zinc) supports wound healing and tissue regeneration after the acute phase.
  • Fluid Resuscitation: For severe burns, intravenous fluid resuscitation may be initiated to prevent shock and support organ function.
  • Monitor Electrolytes: Close monitoring of electrolyte balance is crucial, especially if significant fluid shifts occur due to extensive burns.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Call Emergency Services: Immediately contact 911 or your local emergency number for professional medical assistance.
  • Identify the Chemical: If safe to do so, try to identify the chemical involved (e.g., by looking at the container) and relay this information to emergency responders.
  • Protect Yourself: Ensure your own safety by wearing gloves and eye protection before assisting the patient.
  • Monitor Vital Signs: Continuously monitor the patient's airway, breathing, and circulation (ABCs) while awaiting medical help.
  • Keep Patient Warm: Cover the patient with a clean sheet or blanket to prevent hypothermia, especially during prolonged irrigation.
  • Do Not Apply Ointments: Avoid applying any creams, ointments, or home remedies to the burn, as this can interfere with medical assessment and treatment.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Thorough Decontamination: Complete removal of the chemical is paramount, often requiring prolonged irrigation and sometimes specialized neutralizing agents in a controlled setting.
  • Pain Management: Aggressive pain control is essential, often involving opioids or regional nerve blocks, to ensure patient comfort and cooperation during treatment.
  • Wound Care: Debridement of necrotic tissue, application of appropriate dressings (e.g., silver sulfadiazine, hydrogels), and prevention of infection are critical.
  • Systemic Toxicity Assessment: Evaluate for signs of systemic absorption, especially with highly toxic chemicals, and manage accordingly (e.g., antidote administration, supportive care).
  • Burn Center Referral: Patients with extensive, deep, or functionally critical area burns (face, hands, feet, perineum) should be referred to a specialized burn center.
  • Tetanus Prophylaxis: Assess tetanus immunization status and administer a booster if indicated, as burns are considered tetanus-prone wounds.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Prioritize Safety: Always ensure personal safety and the safety of the patient by following universal precautions and proper PPE use.
  • Rapid Assessment: Quickly assess the burn's depth, size (using the Rule of Nines for larger burns), and location to guide initial management.
  • Documentation: Meticulously document the type of chemical, duration of exposure, initial treatment, and patient's response.
  • Wound Care Education: Educate patients and caregivers on proper wound care techniques, signs of infection, and the importance of follow-up appointments.
  • Fluid Management: Understand the principles of fluid resuscitation for burn patients, including calculating fluid requirements and monitoring urine output.
  • Pain Assessment: Regularly assess and manage the patient's pain using a validated pain scale, adjusting interventions as needed.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Chemical Classification: Understanding the difference between acids (coagulation necrosis) and alkalis (liquefaction necrosis) is key to predicting burn progression.
  • MSDS (Material Safety Data Sheets): Crucial for identifying specific chemical hazards, first aid measures, and appropriate PPE.
  • Burn Depth: Classification into superficial, partial-thickness (superficial and deep), and full-thickness burns guides prognosis and treatment.
  • Rule of Nines: A standardized method for estimating the percentage of Total Body Surface Area (TBSA) affected by burns in adults.
  • Parkland Formula: Used to calculate fluid resuscitation requirements for burn patients (4mL x kg x %TBSA of crystalloid over 24 hours, half in first 8 hours).
  • Systemic Effects: Awareness of potential systemic effects such as hypocalcemia (hydrofluoric acid), methemoglobinemia (aniline dyes), or renal failure.
