Smoke inhalation

🏠 Home / Emergency medicine / Injuries, first aid, and CPR for adults

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Definition: Smoke inhalation occurs when a person breathes in harmful gases, particulate matter, and chemical irritants produced by a fire.
  • Primary Danger: The primary danger is damage to the respiratory tract, systemic toxicity from gases like carbon monoxide and cyanide, and potential for severe airway swelling.
  • Common Sources: Most commonly results from house fires, industrial accidents, or enclosed space fires where combustion products accumulate.
  • Severity Factors: Severity depends on the duration of exposure, concentration of toxins, and the type of materials burning.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Respiratory Distress: Persistent cough, hoarseness, stridor (high-pitched breathing sound), wheezing, shortness of breath, or rapid breathing.
  • Airway Signs: Soot around the nostrils or mouth, singed nasal hairs, facial burns, or painful swallowing.
  • Neurological Impairment: Headache, dizziness, confusion, disorientation, altered mental status, or loss of consciousness due to systemic toxins.
  • Ocular Irritation: Red, watery eyes, or corneal abrasions from smoke particles.
  • Skin Changes: Cherry-red skin (classic for severe carbon monoxide poisoning) or cyanosis (bluish discoloration) in severe cases.
  • Gastrointestinal: Nausea and vomiting may occur due to systemic effects.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Immediate Evacuation: Ensure the patient is removed from the smoke-filled environment to a safe, fresh-air location immediately.
  • Airway Management: Anticipate rapid airway swelling; early intubation may be necessary even if initial respiratory distress is mild.
  • Oxygen Therapy: Administer 100% high-flow oxygen via a non-rebreather mask or endotracheal tube to all suspected victims.
  • Continuous Monitoring: Closely monitor respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, heart rate, and neurological status.
  • Carbon Monoxide Poisoning: Consider hyperbaric oxygen therapy for severe CO poisoning, especially in pregnant patients, those with neurological deficits, or cardiac ischemia.
  • Cyanide Poisoning: Be prepared to administer cyanide antidotes (e.g., hydroxocobalamin) if cyanide toxicity is suspected, particularly in enclosed space fires.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • NPO Status: Maintain nothing by mouth (NPO) status initially due to the high risk of aspiration and potential need for intubation.
  • Intravenous Hydration: Administer intravenous fluids as needed for resuscitation, but monitor closely to avoid fluid overload and exacerbation of pulmonary edema.
  • Post-Stabilization: Once the airway is secure, respiratory status is stable, and aspiration risk is assessed as low, a gradual reintroduction of clear liquids, followed by a soft diet, may be considered.
  • Avoid Irritants: Avoid foods or drinks that could irritate the throat or induce coughing during recovery.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Call Emergency Services: Immediately call 911 or your local emergency number for any suspected smoke inhalation.
  • Scene Safety: Do not enter a burning building or smoke-filled area unless you are trained and equipped to do so.
  • Positioning: If the person is conscious and breathing, help them to a comfortable position, preferably semi-Fowler's, to aid breathing.
  • Loosen Clothing: Loosen any tight clothing around the neck and chest to ease breathing.
  • Stay with Patient: Remain with the patient and continuously observe for any changes in their condition until medical help arrives.
  • Do Not Induce Vomiting: Never attempt to induce vomiting or give anything by mouth to an unconscious or semi-conscious person.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Rapid Assessment: Prioritize rapid assessment of airway patency, breathing effectiveness, and circulatory stability (ABCs).
  • Diagnostic Workup: Order arterial blood gases (ABGs), carboxyhemoglobin levels, cyanide levels (if indicated), chest X-ray, and consider bronchoscopy for direct visualization of airway injury.
  • Aggressive Airway Management: Maintain a low threshold for early intubation, especially with signs of upper airway injury or progressive respiratory distress.
  • Supportive Care: Provide aggressive supportive care, including oxygen, bronchodilators, and mechanical ventilation as needed.
  • Antidote Administration: Be prepared to administer specific antidotes for carbon monoxide and cyanide poisoning based on clinical suspicion and lab results.
  • Monitor for Complications: Closely monitor for delayed complications such as ARDS, pneumonia, and long-term pulmonary dysfunction.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Airway Patency: Prioritize maintaining a patent airway; have intubation equipment readily available.
  • Oxygen Delivery: Ensure continuous high-flow oxygen administration and monitor SpO2 and respiratory effort.
  • Vital Signs & Neurological Checks: Perform frequent vital sign assessments and neurological checks to detect deterioration early.
  • IV Access: Establish secure intravenous access for fluid administration and medication delivery.
  • Medication Administration: Administer prescribed bronchodilators, steroids (controversial but sometimes used), and antidotes as ordered.
  • Patient Education: Educate the patient and family on signs of worsening respiratory distress and the importance of follow-up care.
  • Documentation: Meticulously document all assessments, interventions, and patient responses.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Pathophysiology: Injury mechanisms include direct thermal injury to the upper airway, chemical irritation from toxins (e.g., acrolein, sulfur dioxide), and systemic toxicity from gases (e.g., carbon monoxide, hydrogen cyanide).
  • Carbon Monoxide (CO): Binds to hemoglobin with 200-250 times the affinity of oxygen, forming carboxyhemoglobin (COHb), leading to tissue hypoxia.
  • Hydrogen Cyanide (HCN): Inhibits cytochrome c oxidase, disrupting cellular respiration and leading to anaerobic metabolism and lactic acidosis.
  • Complications: Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS), pneumonia, bronchiolitis obliterans, pulmonary edema, and long-term restrictive or obstructive lung disease.
  • Diagnostic Markers: Elevated carboxyhemoglobin levels, elevated lactate (suggestive of cyanide poisoning), arterial blood gas abnormalities (metabolic acidosis).
  • Treatment Modalities: Oxygen therapy (normobaric and hyperbaric), mechanical ventilation, bronchodilators, hydroxocobalamin (for cyanide), sodium thiosulfate (for cyanide).
