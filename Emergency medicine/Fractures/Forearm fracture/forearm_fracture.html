Forearm fracture

🏠 Home / Emergency medicine / Fractures

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Definition: A forearm fracture involves a break in one or both of the bones in the forearm, the radius and/or the ulna.
  • Common Causes: Often results from direct trauma, falls onto an outstretched hand, or high-impact sports injuries.
  • Types of Fractures: Can be open (skin broken) or closed (skin intact), displaced or non-displaced, and may include specific patterns like greenstick fractures in children.
  • Functional Impact: Significant impairment of wrist and elbow movement, grip strength, and overall arm function if not properly managed.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Severe Pain: Intense, sharp pain immediately following the injury, exacerbated by any attempt to move the arm.
  • Swelling and Bruising: Rapid onset of localized swelling and discoloration around the fracture site.
  • Deformity: Visible angulation, shortening, or an unnatural bend in the forearm.
  • Inability to Move: Significant difficulty or complete inability to rotate the forearm, bend the wrist, or lift objects.
  • Numbness or Tingling: Sensation changes in the hand or fingers, indicating potential nerve involvement or vascular compromise.
  • Crepitus: A grating or crackling sensation or sound when the injured area is gently palpated or moved.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Immediate Immobilization: Do not attempt to straighten or manipulate the injured limb; immobilize it in the position found using a splint or sling.
  • Neurovascular Assessment: Regularly check for pulse, capillary refill, sensation, and motor function distal to the injury to monitor for compromise.
  • Elevation and Ice: Elevate the forearm above heart level and apply ice packs (wrapped in cloth) to reduce swelling and pain.
  • Pain Management: Administer prescribed analgesics promptly and monitor their effectiveness.
  • Open Wound Care: For open fractures, cover the wound with a sterile dressing without attempting to clean or push bone fragments back in.
  • Compartment Syndrome Watch: Be vigilant for signs of increasing pain disproportionate to the injury, pallor, paresthesia, pulselessness, and paralysis.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Pre-Operative Fasting: If surgical intervention is anticipated, strictly adhere to NPO (nil per os) guidelines as instructed by the medical team.
  • Post-Operative Hydration: Gradually reintroduce clear fluids, then light solids as tolerated after anesthesia, monitoring for nausea.
  • Nutrient-Rich Diet: Encourage a diet rich in calcium, Vitamin D, and protein to support bone healing and overall recovery.
  • Fiber Intake: Increase dietary fiber to prevent constipation, which can be a side effect of immobility and opioid pain medications.
  • Avoid Alcohol and Smoking: Advise against alcohol consumption and smoking, as they can impair bone healing and increase complication risks.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Emergency Transport: Ensure prompt and safe transport of the patient to an emergency medical facility.
  • Stabilization During Transport: Maintain proper immobilization of the forearm during transport to prevent further injury or displacement.
  • Continuous Monitoring: Continuously monitor the patient's vital signs, pain level, and neurovascular status.
  • Clear Communication: Provide a detailed report of the injury mechanism, initial assessment findings, and any interventions performed to receiving medical staff.
  • Patient Reassurance: Offer psychological support and reassurance to the patient, managing anxiety and discomfort.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Diagnostic Imaging: Emphasize the necessity of obtaining at least two orthogonal X-ray views (AP and lateral) to accurately assess fracture type, location, and displacement.
  • Treatment Modalities: Determine the most appropriate treatment, which may range from closed reduction and casting for stable fractures to Open Reduction Internal Fixation (ORIF) for displaced or unstable fractures.
  • Orthopedic Consultation: Recommend early consultation with an orthopedic surgeon for complex, open, or significantly displaced forearm fractures.
  • Rehabilitation Planning: Stress the importance of a structured rehabilitation program, including physical and occupational therapy, post-immobilization to restore function.
  • Complication Awareness: Educate patients on potential complications such as malunion, nonunion, compartment syndrome, nerve injury, and post-traumatic arthritis.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Comprehensive Assessment: Master the skill of performing a thorough neurovascular assessment (the 5 Ps: Pain, Pallor, Paresthesia, Pulselessness, Paralysis) of the affected limb.
  • Pain Management Protocols: Understand and apply various pain management strategies, including pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions, for acute fracture pain.
  • Cast and Splint Care Education: Educate patients and caregivers on proper cast/splint care, signs of complications (e.g., tightness, numbness, foul odor), and when to seek immediate medical attention.
  • Patient Education on Activity Restrictions: Provide clear instructions regarding activity limitations, weight-bearing restrictions, and the importance of adherence to the treatment plan.
  • Documentation Accuracy: Maintain meticulous and accurate documentation of all assessment findings, interventions, patient education, and patient responses.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • ICD-10 Codes: S52.x (Fracture of forearm). Specific codes vary based on the bone (radius, ulna, or both) and the location (e.g., distal, shaft, proximal).
  • Imaging Modalities: Primary diagnosis via plain radiography (X-rays); CT scans may be used for complex intra-articular fractures or surgical planning.
  • Surgical Techniques: Open Reduction Internal Fixation (ORIF) with plates and screws is common; intramedullary nailing for certain shaft fractures.
  • Anatomical Landmarks: Radial head, radial shaft, distal radius (e.g., Colles' fracture, Smith's fracture), ulnar shaft, olecranon.
  • Potential Complications: Compartment syndrome, nonunion, malunion, infection (especially with open fractures), nerve entrapment (median, ulnar, radial), vascular injury, post-traumatic stiffness.
