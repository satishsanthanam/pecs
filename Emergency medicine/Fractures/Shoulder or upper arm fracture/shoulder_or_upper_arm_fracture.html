Shoulder or upper arm fracture

🏠 Home / Emergency medicine / Fractures

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Definition: A shoulder or upper arm fracture involves a break in the bones of the shoulder girdle (scapula, clavicle) or the humerus (upper arm bone).
  • Common Causes: Often results from direct trauma, such as falls onto an outstretched hand, direct impact to the shoulder, or high-energy accidents.
  • Types: Can include proximal humerus fractures (near the shoulder joint), humeral shaft fractures (mid-arm), or less commonly, scapular fractures.
  • Severity: Fractures can range from stable, non-displaced cracks to complex, comminuted breaks requiring surgical intervention.
  • Immediate Action: Prompt medical evaluation is crucial to assess the extent of injury and prevent further damage or complications.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Severe Pain: Intense pain in the shoulder or upper arm, often exacerbated by any attempt to move the limb.
  • Swelling and Bruising: Rapid onset of localized swelling and discoloration around the injured area.
  • Deformity: Visible abnormal angulation, shortening, or displacement of the arm or shoulder contour.
  • Inability to Move: Significant limitation or complete inability to lift, rotate, or move the affected arm.
  • Crepitus: A grinding or crunching sensation or sound when the injured area is gently moved (avoid purposeful movement).
  • Numbness/Tingling: Potential signs of nerve involvement, indicating a need for urgent neurovascular assessment.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Immobilization: Immediately immobilize the injured arm using a sling, splint, or by supporting it against the body to prevent further displacement and pain.
  • Avoid Manipulation: Do not attempt to straighten, realign, or massage the injured limb, as this can worsen the fracture or cause nerve/vascular damage.
  • Pain Management: Administer appropriate analgesia as prescribed, being mindful of potential side effects and monitoring pain levels.
  • Neurovascular Checks: Regularly assess for changes in sensation, motor function, skin color, temperature, and pulse distal to the injury.
  • Compartment Syndrome Watch: Be vigilant for signs of compartment syndrome, characterized by disproportionate pain, pallor, paresthesia, pulselessness, and paralysis.
  • Fall Prevention: Implement measures to prevent falls, especially in elderly patients, to reduce the risk of such fractures.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Protein Intake: Ensure adequate intake of lean proteins (e.g., poultry, fish, legumes) to support tissue repair and bone matrix formation during healing.
  • Calcium & Vitamin D: Encourage consumption of calcium-rich foods (dairy, fortified plant milks, leafy greens) and Vitamin D sources (fatty fish, fortified foods) essential for bone mineralization.
  • Hydration: Maintain optimal fluid intake to support overall metabolic processes and nutrient transport vital for healing.
  • Fiber-Rich Foods: Increase dietary fiber from fruits, vegetables, and whole grains to prevent constipation, especially if opioid pain medications are prescribed.
  • Anti-inflammatory Nutrients: Incorporate foods rich in omega-3 fatty acids (e.g., flaxseed, walnuts) and antioxidants to support the body's natural healing response.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Call Emergency Services: Immediately contact emergency medical services (e.g., 911) or transport the patient to the nearest emergency department.
  • Support the Limb: Gently support the injured arm in a comfortable position, ideally with a sling or improvised support, to minimize movement.
  • Apply Cold Pack: If available, apply a cold pack wrapped in cloth to the injured area for 15-20 minutes at a time to help reduce swelling and pain.
  • Monitor Vitals: Observe the patient for signs of shock (pale skin, rapid pulse, confusion) and provide reassurance.
  • Do Not Offer Food/Drink: Avoid giving the patient food or drink in case surgery or sedation is required.
  • Gather Information: Be prepared to provide details about how the injury occurred, the patient's medical history, and current medications to medical personnel.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Diagnostic Imaging: Initial diagnosis typically involves X-rays (AP, lateral, Y-view) to confirm the fracture type, location, and displacement. CT scans may be necessary for complex fractures or surgical planning.
  • Treatment Modalities: Treatment ranges from conservative management (immobilization with a sling or cast) for stable, non-displaced fractures to surgical intervention (e.g., Open Reduction Internal Fixation - ORIF, intramedullary nailing, arthroplasty) for displaced, unstable, or complex fractures.
  • Orthopedic Consultation: Referral to an orthopedic surgeon is essential for definitive management and to discuss surgical versus non-surgical options.
  • Rehabilitation Plan: A structured physical therapy program is critical post-treatment to restore range of motion, strength, and function, often starting with passive exercises and progressing to active strengthening.
  • Complication Awareness: Potential complications include non-union, malunion, avascular necrosis (especially with proximal humerus fractures), nerve damage (e.g., radial nerve palsy with humeral shaft fractures), and adhesive capsulitis.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Initial Assessment: Perform a thorough neurovascular assessment, including the '5 Ps' (Pain, Pallor, Paresthesia, Pulselessness, Paralysis) distal to the injury site.
  • Pain Management: Administer prescribed analgesics, assess pain levels regularly using a validated scale, and evaluate the effectiveness of interventions.
  • Immobilization Care: Educate the patient on proper sling/cast care, skin integrity assessment under the device, and signs of complications to report.
  • Patient Education: Instruct on activity restrictions, safe transfers, and the importance of adhering to the rehabilitation schedule to prevent stiffness and promote healing.
  • Wound Care (Post-Op): For surgical cases, monitor incision sites for signs of infection (redness, warmth, swelling, purulent drainage) and provide appropriate wound care.
  • Mobility Assistance: Assist with activities of daily living (ADLs) and safe ambulation, considering the limitations imposed by the fracture and immobilization.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • ICD-10 Codes: S42.2 (Fracture of shaft of humerus), S42.0 (Fracture of upper end of humerus), S42.1 (Fracture of lower end of humerus), S42.3 (Fracture of unspecified part of humerus).
  • Imaging Modalities: Standard X-rays (AP, lateral, trans-scapular Y views), CT scan (for complex articular fractures or surgical planning), MRI (to assess soft tissue injury or rotator cuff involvement).
  • Surgical Interventions: Open Reduction Internal Fixation (ORIF) with plates and screws, Intramedullary Nailing, Hemiarthroplasty or Reverse Total Shoulder Arthroplasty (for severe proximal humerus fractures in elderly patients).
  • Common Complications: Non-union, malunion, avascular necrosis (AVN) of the humeral head, radial nerve palsy (with humeral shaft fractures), adhesive capsulitis (frozen shoulder), infection (post-surgical).
  • Rehabilitation Phases: Typically involves an initial immobilization phase, followed by passive range of motion, active assisted range of motion, and progressive strengthening exercises.
  • Anatomical Considerations: Proximity of the axillary nerve (proximal humerus), radial nerve (humeral shaft), and brachial plexus to the fracture sites.
