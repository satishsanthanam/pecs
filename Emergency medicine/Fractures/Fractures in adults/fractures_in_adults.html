Fractures in adults

🏠 Home / Emergency medicine / Fractures

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Definition: A fracture is a break in the continuity of a bone, ranging from a hairline crack to a complete separation.
  • Common Causes: Most adult fractures result from significant trauma such as falls, sports injuries, motor vehicle accidents, or direct impact.
  • Pathological Fractures: These occur in bones weakened by underlying conditions like osteoporosis, tumors, or metabolic bone diseases, often from minimal trauma.
  • Types of Fractures: Can be classified as open (compound, skin broken) or closed (simple, skin intact), complete or incomplete, displaced or non-displaced, and by pattern (e.g., transverse, oblique, spiral, comminuted).
  • Urgency: All suspected fractures require prompt medical evaluation to prevent complications and ensure proper healing.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Severe Pain: Often sharp, localized, and worsens with movement or pressure on the affected area.
  • Swelling and Bruising: Rapid onset of edema and discoloration around the injury site due to internal bleeding.
  • Deformity: Visible angulation, shortening, or abnormal positioning of the limb or body part.
  • Inability to Bear Weight/Move: Significant functional impairment, making it difficult or impossible to use the injured limb.
  • Crepitus: A grating or crunching sensation or sound when the injured area is moved, indicating bone fragments rubbing together.
  • Open Wound: For open fractures, there is a break in the skin, exposing the bone or creating a pathway for infection.
  • Neurovascular Compromise: Numbness, tingling, pallor, or coolness distal to the injury, indicating potential nerve or blood vessel damage.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Immobilization: Immediately stabilize the injured limb in the position found using a splint or sling to prevent further damage and reduce pain.
  • Do Not Realign: Never attempt to straighten or manipulate a visibly deformed limb, as this can worsen injury to nerves, blood vessels, or soft tissues.
  • Control Bleeding: For open fractures, apply direct pressure with a clean dressing to control any external bleeding without pushing exposed bone back into the wound.
  • Monitor Neurovascular Status: Regularly assess the patient for changes in sensation, motor function, skin color, temperature, and capillary refill distal to the injury.
  • Pain Management: Administer appropriate analgesia as soon as possible, once medical assessment allows, to alleviate distress.
  • Tetanus Prophylaxis: For open fractures, ensure tetanus vaccination status is current and administer prophylaxis as indicated.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Adequate Protein Intake: Essential for tissue repair and bone matrix formation; encourage lean meats, poultry, fish, eggs, dairy, and legumes.
  • Calcium and Vitamin D: Crucial for bone mineralization; recommend dairy products, fortified foods, leafy greens, and sun exposure or supplements as advised by a physician.
  • Vitamin C: Important for collagen synthesis, a key component of bone; found in citrus fruits, berries, and bell peppers.
  • Hydration: Maintain ample fluid intake to support overall metabolic processes and nutrient transport for healing.
  • Avoidance of Inhibitors: Limit or avoid alcohol and nicotine, as both can significantly impair bone healing and increase the risk of complications.
  • Balanced Nutrition: Ensure a diet rich in various vitamins and minerals to support the body's increased demands during the healing process.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Call Emergency Services: For any suspected severe fracture, especially with deformity, open wounds, or signs of neurovascular compromise, call emergency medical services immediately.
  • First Aid Principles: Apply RICE (Rest, Ice, Compression, Elevation) as appropriate for initial management, particularly for less severe injuries, while awaiting professional medical help.
  • Patient Comfort: Keep the patient warm and comfortable, reassure them, and minimize unnecessary movement of the injured area.
  • Monitor for Shock: Watch for signs of shock (e.g., rapid pulse, pale skin, confusion) and manage by lying the patient flat and elevating the legs if no spinal injury is suspected.
  • Documentation: Note the time of injury, mechanism, initial symptoms, and any interventions performed before medical professionals arrive.
  • Transport Safely: If self-transporting, ensure the injured limb is well-supported and immobilized throughout the journey to the medical facility.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Accurate Diagnosis: Initial assessment includes physical examination and imaging (X-rays are standard; CT or MRI may be used for complex fractures or soft tissue involvement).
  • Treatment Goals: Aim to achieve anatomical reduction (realigning bone fragments) and stable fixation to promote optimal healing and restore function.
  • Non-Surgical Management: Involves closed reduction and immobilization with casts, splints, or braces for stable, non-displaced, or minimally displaced fractures.
  • Surgical Management: Open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) with plates, screws, rods, or pins, or external fixation, is used for unstable, displaced, or open fractures.
  • Complication Monitoring: Vigilant monitoring for compartment syndrome, infection (especially with open fractures), non-union, malunion, nerve damage, and vascular injury.
  • Rehabilitation: Crucial for recovery, involving physical therapy to regain strength, range of motion, and function after immobilization or surgery.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Neurovascular Assessment (5 P's): Routinely assess Pain, Pallor, Pulselessness, Paresthesia, and Paralysis distal to the injury site to detect compartment syndrome or vascular compromise.
  • Pain Management: Administer prescribed analgesics, assess pain levels regularly, and utilize non-pharmacological interventions (e.g., elevation, ice, distraction).
  • Cast Care Education: Instruct patients on signs of complications (e.g., increased pain, numbness, swelling, foul odor), keeping the cast dry, and avoiding inserting objects into the cast.
  • Wound Care: For open fractures, meticulous aseptic technique for dressing changes and monitoring for signs of infection (redness, warmth, purulent drainage).
  • Mobility Assistance: Educate on safe use of assistive devices (crutches, walker) and proper transfer techniques to prevent falls and further injury.
  • Patient Education: Provide clear instructions on activity restrictions, weight-bearing status, medication adherence, and follow-up appointments.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • AO Foundation/OTA Fracture Classification: A comprehensive system for classifying fractures based on bone, segment, and fracture morphology, aiding in treatment planning and research.
  • Gustilo-Anderson Classification: Used for open fractures, categorizing them by the extent of soft tissue damage, contamination, and fracture pattern, guiding surgical management and antibiotic prophylaxis.
  • Compartment Syndrome: A critical condition caused by increased pressure within a confined fascial compartment, leading to impaired blood flow and potential tissue necrosis.
  • Non-Union/Malunion: Non-union refers to the failure of a fracture to heal within an expected timeframe; malunion is healing in an unacceptable anatomical position.
  • Imaging Modalities: Standard radiographs (X-rays) are primary; Computed Tomography (CT) for complex articular fractures; Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) for soft tissue injuries or occult fractures.
  • Pharmacology: Analgesics (NSAIDs, opioids), antibiotics (for open fractures), tetanus toxoid, and potentially bone-healing adjuncts (e.g., Vitamin D, Calcium supplements).
