Vertebral compression fracture

🏠 Home / Emergency medicine / Fractures

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Definition: A vertebral compression fracture occurs when a bone in the spine (vertebra) collapses, often due to weakening from osteoporosis, trauma, or metastatic disease.
  • Commonality: These are the most common type of osteoporotic fracture, frequently affecting the thoracic and lumbar spine.
  • Mechanism: Often results from axial loading (e.g., falling from a height onto the feet or buttocks) or hyperflexion of the spine, even from minor activities like bending or coughing in severe osteoporosis.
  • Progression: Can lead to progressive spinal deformity, such as kyphosis (hunchback), and chronic pain if not managed appropriately.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Acute Back Pain: Sudden onset of severe back pain, often localized to the fracture site, which may worsen with movement, standing, or coughing.
  • Pain Radiation: Pain can sometimes radiate to the abdomen or flanks, mimicking other conditions.
  • Height Loss: Gradual loss of height over time, especially with multiple compression fractures.
  • Postural Changes: Development of a stooped posture (kyphosis) due to wedging of the fractured vertebrae.
  • Neurological Deficits: Rarely, severe fractures can cause spinal cord compression leading to numbness, weakness, or bowel/bladder dysfunction, requiring urgent medical attention.
  • Abdominal Symptoms: In severe kyphosis, abdominal compression can lead to early satiety, bloating, or constipation.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Activity Modification: Avoid sudden twisting, bending, or lifting heavy objects, especially during the acute healing phase.
  • Pain Management Adherence: Strictly follow the prescribed pain medication regimen to ensure comfort and facilitate mobility.
  • Fall Prevention: Implement comprehensive fall prevention strategies, particularly for elderly or osteoporotic patients, including home safety assessments and assistive devices.
  • Spinal Bracing: If a spinal orthosis or brace is prescribed, ensure correct application and wear as directed to provide support and limit spinal movement.
  • Weight-Bearing Restrictions: Gradually increase activity and weight-bearing as tolerated and advised by your physician to prevent further injury.
  • Bone Health Management: Address underlying osteoporosis with appropriate medications and lifestyle modifications to prevent future fractures.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Calcium & Vitamin D: Ensure adequate intake of calcium and Vitamin D through diet or supplements to support bone health and density, crucial for fracture healing and prevention.
  • Fiber-Rich Foods: Incorporate high-fiber foods (fruits, vegetables, whole grains) and sufficient fluids to prevent constipation, which can be exacerbated by immobility and pain medications.
  • Hydration: Maintain good hydration by drinking plenty of water throughout the day.
  • Balanced Nutrition: Consume a well-balanced diet rich in protein to support tissue repair and overall recovery.
  • Weight Management: Maintain a healthy body weight to reduce excessive load on the spine and support overall musculoskeletal health.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Neurological Changes: Seek immediate medical attention for any new or worsening neurological symptoms, such as numbness, tingling, weakness in the limbs, or changes in bowel/bladder function.
  • Uncontrolled Pain: Report persistent or escalating pain that is not adequately managed by prescribed medications to your healthcare provider.
  • Mobility Decline: Notify your care team if there is a significant decrease in your ability to move, walk, or perform daily activities.
  • Brace Issues: Report any skin irritation, pressure sores, or discomfort caused by a spinal brace, or if the brace feels ill-fitting.
  • Fever or Redness: Watch for signs of infection, such as fever, chills, or redness/swelling at any surgical site if an intervention like vertebroplasty was performed.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Accurate Diagnosis: Confirm the diagnosis with appropriate imaging (X-ray, MRI, CT scan) to assess fracture acuity, stability, and rule out spinal cord involvement.
  • Conservative Management: Initial treatment typically involves pain control, activity modification, and bracing.
  • Interventional Options: Consider vertebroplasty or kyphoplasty for patients with persistent, severe pain despite conservative measures, especially in osteoporotic fractures.
  • Underlying Etiology: Always investigate and manage the underlying cause, such as osteoporosis, with appropriate pharmacotherapy (e.g., bisphosphonates, teriparatide).
  • Rehabilitation: Emphasize early mobilization and physical therapy to restore function, strengthen core muscles, and improve posture once acute pain is controlled.
  • Long-term Follow-up: Regular follow-up is crucial to monitor healing, manage chronic pain, and prevent future fractures.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Pain Assessment: Conduct thorough and frequent pain assessments, utilizing appropriate pain scales, and evaluate the effectiveness of analgesia.
  • Neurological Monitoring: Regularly monitor for any changes in neurological status, including motor strength, sensation, and bowel/bladder function.
  • Patient Education: Educate patients and caregivers on proper body mechanics, safe transfers, fall prevention strategies, and correct use of spinal orthoses.
  • Medication Administration: Administer prescribed analgesics, muscle relaxants, and bone-modifying agents, ensuring patient understanding of their purpose and side effects.
  • Mobility Assistance: Assist with safe ambulation and transfers, utilizing assistive devices as needed, and encourage progressive activity within prescribed limits.
  • Discharge Planning: Collaborate with the interdisciplinary team to ensure comprehensive discharge planning, including home safety assessment, follow-up appointments, and access to rehabilitation services.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Pathophysiology: Characterized by a collapse of the vertebral body, often wedge-shaped, due to axial loading forces exceeding the bone's structural integrity. Predominantly seen in osteoporotic bone.
  • Classification: Can be classified by morphology (wedge, biconcave, crush) and etiology (osteoporotic, traumatic, pathological).
  • Imaging Modalities: Plain radiographs (lateral view for height loss), MRI (to differentiate acute vs. chronic, assess edema, and spinal cord involvement), CT (for detailed bony anatomy and canal compromise).
  • Treatment Modalities: Conservative (analgesia, bracing, rest, physical therapy), minimally invasive (vertebroplasty, kyphoplasty), and rarely open surgical stabilization for instability or neurological deficit.
  • Complications: Chronic back pain, progressive kyphosis, spinal canal stenosis, pseudoarthrosis, and rarely, neurological compromise.
  • Risk Factors: Advanced age, female gender, postmenopausal status, osteoporosis, long-term corticosteroid use, metastatic cancer, and previous fragility fractures.
