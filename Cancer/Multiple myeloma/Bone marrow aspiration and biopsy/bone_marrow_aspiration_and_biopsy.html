Bone marrow aspiration and biopsy

🏠 Home / Cancer / Multiple myeloma

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Procedure Overview: Bone marrow aspiration and biopsy (BMA/B) is a diagnostic procedure to collect samples of bone marrow, the spongy tissue inside bones where blood cells are made.
  • Purpose in Myeloma: Essential for diagnosing, staging, and monitoring multiple myeloma by examining plasma cells, their morphology, and genetic abnormalities.
  • Sample Collection: Involves two parts: aspiration (drawing out liquid marrow) and biopsy (removing a small core of solid marrow tissue).
  • Common Site: Typically performed on the posterior superior iliac crest (hip bone) due to its accessibility and rich marrow content.
  • Anesthesia: Local anesthetic is used to numb the skin and periosteum; sometimes mild sedation is offered for patient comfort.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Bone Pain: Persistent or unexplained bone pain, especially in the back, ribs, or hips, is a common indicator of multiple myeloma.
  • Fatigue & Weakness: Often due to anemia, a frequent complication of myeloma affecting red blood cell production.
  • Recurrent Infections: Impaired immune function from myeloma can lead to frequent bacterial or viral infections.
  • Unexplained Fractures: Pathological fractures resulting from bone lesions caused by myeloma cells.
  • Kidney Problems: Signs of renal dysfunction, such as changes in urination or swelling, can prompt investigation for myeloma.
  • Hypercalcemia: Symptoms like confusion, excessive thirst, or constipation due to high calcium levels in the blood.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Medication Review: Inform your physician about all medications, especially blood thinners (anticoagulants like warfarin, aspirin, NSAIDs) or supplements, as these may need to be paused.
  • Allergy Disclosure: Report any known allergies to local anesthetics, latex, or adhesive tapes.
  • Infection Risk: Maintain strict sterile technique during the procedure to minimize the risk of infection at the biopsy site.
  • Bleeding Risk: Monitor the site closely for excessive bleeding or hematoma formation, particularly in patients with coagulopathies.
  • Patient Positioning: Ensure proper patient positioning (prone or lateral decubitus) for optimal access and comfort, and to prevent injury.
  • Post-Procedure Care: Keep the dressing clean and dry, avoid strenuous activity for 24-48 hours, and report any signs of infection or persistent bleeding.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Pre-Procedure Fasting: If conscious sedation is planned, strict fasting (typically 6-8 hours for solids, 2 hours for clear liquids) is required prior to the procedure.
  • Hydration: Ensure adequate hydration before the procedure, unless otherwise instructed, especially if sedation is not used.
  • Post-Procedure Fluids: Resume normal fluid intake immediately after the procedure, unless advised otherwise due to sedation recovery.
  • Gradual Food Reintroduction: If sedation was administered, start with light, easily digestible foods and gradually return to your regular diet as tolerated.
  • Avoid Alcohol: Refrain from alcohol consumption for at least 24 hours post-procedure, especially if sedatives were used, as it can interact with medications and impair judgment.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Pain Management: Discuss pain management options with the healthcare team before and after the procedure; mild analgesics may be prescribed.
  • Site Monitoring: Observe the biopsy site for signs of complications such as excessive bleeding, swelling, redness, warmth, or pus.
  • Activity Restrictions: Advise patients to avoid heavy lifting, strenuous exercise, or activities that could put pressure on the biopsy site for at least 24-48 hours.
  • When to Seek Help: Instruct patients to contact their doctor immediately if they experience fever, chills, severe or worsening pain, excessive bleeding that soaks through the dressing, or numbness/tingling in the leg.
  • Transportation: If sedation is used, ensure the patient has a responsible adult to drive them home and assist them for the first 24 hours.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Diagnostic Cornerstone: BMA/B is indispensable for the definitive diagnosis of multiple myeloma, allowing for direct visualization of plasma cells and their characteristics.
  • Prognostic Information: The samples provide crucial information for cytogenetic and molecular studies, which are vital for risk stratification and guiding treatment decisions.
  • Treatment Monitoring: Repeat BMA/B may be necessary to assess treatment response, detect minimal residual disease (MRD), or investigate disease progression.
  • Differential Diagnosis: Helps differentiate multiple myeloma from other plasma cell dyscrasias or hematological conditions.
  • Multidisciplinary Approach: Results are integrated with other clinical findings, imaging, and laboratory tests to formulate a comprehensive patient care plan.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Patient Education: Nurses play a key role in educating patients about the procedure, managing expectations, and addressing anxieties.
  • Pre-Procedure Checklist: Verify patient consent, NPO status (if applicable), medication history, and allergy information.
  • Assisting the Physician: Prepare the sterile field, ensure all necessary equipment is available, and assist with patient positioning and comfort during the procedure.
  • Post-Procedure Assessment: Monitor vital signs, assess pain levels, and meticulously check the biopsy site for bleeding or hematoma formation.
  • Wound Care Instruction: Provide clear instructions on dressing changes, hygiene, and signs of complications to report.
  • Pain Management: Administer prescribed analgesics and evaluate their effectiveness, advocating for patient comfort.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Trephine Biopsy: Refers to the collection of a solid core of bone marrow tissue using a specialized needle.
  • Aspirate: The liquid bone marrow sample obtained, used for cytology, flow cytometry, and cytogenetic analysis.
  • Plasma Cells: B-lymphocytes that produce antibodies; their abnormal proliferation is characteristic of multiple myeloma.
  • Monoclonal Gammopathy: The presence of an abnormal protein (M-protein) produced by a single clone of plasma cells.
  • Cytogenetics & FISH: Techniques used to detect chromosomal abnormalities (e.g., deletions, translocations) in myeloma cells, crucial for prognosis.
  • Immunohistochemistry: Staining techniques applied to biopsy samples to identify specific markers on cells, aiding in diagnosis and classification.
  • IMWG Criteria: International Myeloma Working Group criteria for the diagnosis and staging of multiple myeloma.
