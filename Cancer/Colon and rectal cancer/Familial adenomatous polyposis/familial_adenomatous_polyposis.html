Familial adenomatous polyposis

🏠 Home / Cancer / Colon and rectal cancer

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Genetic Inheritance: Familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP) is an autosomal dominant inherited condition caused by a mutation in the APC gene.
  • Polyp Development: Characterized by the development of hundreds to thousands of adenomatous polyps throughout the colon and rectum, typically starting in the teenage years.
  • High Cancer Risk: Untreated FAP carries a nearly 100% lifetime risk of developing colorectal cancer (CRC), often by age 40.
  • Extracolonic Manifestations: FAP can also lead to other growths, including desmoid tumors, osteomas, dental abnormalities, and polyps in the upper gastrointestinal tract (duodenum, stomach).
  • Early Onset: Polyps can begin to form in childhood, necessitating early and regular surveillance.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Rectal Bleeding: Often an early sign of polyps or developing colorectal cancer.
  • Changes in Bowel Habits: Persistent diarrhea, constipation, or alternating patterns may indicate polyp burden or malignancy.
  • Abdominal Pain: Can occur with large polyps, advanced cancer, or desmoid tumors.
  • Anemia: Chronic blood loss from polyps can lead to iron-deficiency anemia, causing fatigue and pallor.
  • Unexplained Weight Loss: A concerning symptom that may suggest advanced disease.
  • Family History: A strong family history of colorectal cancer or polyps diagnosed at a young age is a key indicator prompting investigation for FAP.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Genetic Counseling and Testing: Essential for individuals with a family history of FAP and for newly diagnosed patients to confirm the diagnosis and identify at-risk relatives.
  • Early and Regular Colonoscopy: Surveillance typically begins between ages 10-12, with annual colonoscopies to monitor polyp development.
  • Prophylactic Colectomy: Surgical removal of the colon is often recommended once polyps become numerous or dysplastic, usually in late teens or early twenties, to prevent colorectal cancer.
  • Upper Endoscopy Surveillance: Regular esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) with side-viewing duodenoscopy is crucial to monitor for duodenal and ampullary polyps, which also carry a cancer risk.
  • Desmoid Tumor Monitoring: Awareness and surveillance for desmoid tumors, which can be aggressive and challenging to manage, especially in the abdominal cavity.
  • NSAID Chemoprevention: Discuss with a physician the potential role of certain non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), like celecoxib, in reducing polyp burden, though this is not a substitute for surgery.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Fiber-Rich Diet: Encouraging a diet high in fruits, vegetables, and whole grains to promote regular bowel movements and overall gut health.
  • Limit Red and Processed Meats: General recommendation for colorectal cancer prevention, which may be particularly relevant for individuals with FAP.
  • Adequate Hydration: Maintaining good fluid intake is important for bowel function, especially for those with altered bowel habits or post-colectomy.
  • Post-Colectomy Dietary Adjustments: For patients who undergo colectomy, dietary modifications may be necessary to manage changes in bowel function, such as gradual reintroduction of foods and avoiding irritants.
  • Calcium and Vitamin D Intake: Discuss with a healthcare provider the potential benefits of adequate calcium and vitamin D intake, as some studies suggest a protective role against colorectal cancer.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Strict Adherence to Screening Schedules: Emphasize the critical importance of following the prescribed surveillance schedule for colonoscopies and upper endoscopies.
  • Educate on Symptoms: Inform family members and caregivers about potential symptoms of polyp progression or cancer, such as rectal bleeding, changes in bowel habits, or abdominal pain.
  • Support Genetic Testing for Relatives: Encourage at-risk family members to undergo genetic counseling and testing to identify those who carry the APC gene mutation.
  • Psychosocial Support: Acknowledge and address the significant emotional and psychological impact of living with a high-risk genetic condition, connecting patients and families with support groups if available.
  • Post-Surgical Care: Provide comprehensive guidance on post-operative care for patients undergoing colectomy, including wound care, pain management, and dietary adjustments.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Early Diagnosis is Paramount: Identifying FAP early through genetic testing and initiating surveillance is crucial for preventing invasive colorectal cancer.
  • Multidisciplinary Management: A collaborative approach involving gastroenterologists, colorectal surgeons, geneticists, and oncologists is essential for comprehensive patient care.
  • Individualized Treatment Plans: Management strategies, including the timing of prophylactic colectomy and surveillance intervals, must be tailored to the individual patient's polyp burden, family history, and specific FAP variant.
  • Lifelong Surveillance: Even after colectomy, patients require ongoing surveillance for remaining rectal tissue (if applicable), duodenal polyps, and other extracolonic manifestations.
  • Genetic Counseling Referral: All patients diagnosed with FAP and their first-degree relatives should be referred for genetic counseling to understand inheritance patterns and testing options.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Patient Education on FAP: Nurses play a vital role in educating patients and families about the genetic nature of FAP, the importance of surveillance, and surgical options.
  • Pre- and Post-Operative Care: Providing comprehensive nursing care for patients undergoing colectomy, including preparing them for surgery, managing post-operative pain, monitoring for complications, and assisting with stoma care if applicable.
  • Genetic Pedigree Analysis: Understanding how to construct and interpret a family pedigree to identify individuals at risk for FAP and facilitate genetic testing.
  • Emotional Support and Counseling: Offering empathetic support to patients and families coping with the diagnosis of a high-risk genetic condition and the implications for their health and future.
  • Coordination of Care: Assisting in scheduling and coordinating the multiple surveillance appointments and specialist consultations required for FAP management.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • APC Gene Mutation: The primary genetic defect responsible for Familial Adenomatous Polyposis, located on chromosome 5q21.
  • Attenuated FAP (AFAP): A milder variant of FAP characterized by fewer colorectal polyps (typically 10-100), later onset of polyps and cancer, and often associated with mutations at the 5' and 3' ends of the APC gene.
  • Gardner Syndrome: A phenotypic variant of FAP characterized by the classic FAP features along with extracolonic manifestations such as osteomas (benign bone growths), desmoid tumors, dental anomalies, and epidermoid cysts.
  • Turcot Syndrome: A rare variant associated with FAP (or Lynch syndrome) and primary central nervous system tumors, typically medulloblastoma or glioblastoma.
  • Desmoid Tumors: Aggressive, non-malignant fibrous tumors that can occur in FAP patients, particularly after abdominal surgery, and can be locally invasive.
  • Ampullary Adenomas: Polyps found in the ampulla of Vater in the duodenum, which carry a significant risk of developing into adenocarcinoma and require regular endoscopic surveillance.
