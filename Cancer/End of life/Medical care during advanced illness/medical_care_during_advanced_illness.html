Medical care during advanced illness

🏠 Home / Cancer / End of life

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Focus on Comfort and Quality of Life: Medical care shifts from curative treatments to maximizing comfort and dignity for patients with advanced cancer.
  • Palliative Care Integration: Palliative care is a specialized approach focused on providing relief from the symptoms and stress of a serious illness, aiming to improve quality of life for both the patient and their family.
  • Hospice Care Consideration: Hospice care is a specific type of palliative care for individuals with a life expectancy of six months or less, emphasizing comfort and support at home or in a dedicated facility.
  • Advance Care Planning: Discussions about future medical care, including treatment preferences and end-of-life wishes, are crucial and should be documented (e.g., Advance Directives, POLST).
  • Interdisciplinary Team Approach: Care involves physicians, nurses, social workers, chaplains, and other specialists working together to address physical, emotional, social, and spiritual needs.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Persistent Pain: Often complex, requiring comprehensive assessment and multi-modal management strategies.
  • Profound Fatigue and Weakness (Asthenia): Common and often debilitating, impacting daily activities and quality of life.
  • Nausea and Vomiting: Can be due to disease progression, medications, or metabolic changes, requiring antiemetic interventions.
  • Dyspnea (Shortness of Breath): May result from lung involvement, anemia, or fluid accumulation, managed with medications and comfort measures.
  • Anorexia and Cachexia: Significant loss of appetite and muscle wasting, impacting nutritional status and overall strength.
  • Anxiety and Depression: Emotional distress is prevalent, requiring psychological and sometimes pharmacological support.
  • Delirium and Confusion: Acute changes in mental status, often fluctuating, requiring careful assessment and management of underlying causes.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Avoid Unnecessary Aggressive Interventions: Carefully weigh the benefits and burdens of treatments, prioritizing comfort over interventions unlikely to improve quality or quantity of life.
  • Medication Management: Regularly review all medications to prevent polypharmacy, minimize side effects, and ensure appropriate dosing for symptom control.
  • Prevent Pressure Injuries: Implement frequent repositioning, skin care, and specialized mattresses to prevent bedsores in bed-bound patients.
  • Fall Risk Assessment: Implement measures to prevent falls, especially in patients with weakness, confusion, or those on sedating medications.
  • Infection Control: Maintain good hygiene and monitor for signs of infection, balancing aggressive treatment with comfort goals.
  • Emotional and Spiritual Support: Ensure access to counseling, spiritual care, and bereavement support for both the patient and their family.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Focus on Comfort Feeding: Offer small, frequent portions of preferred foods, prioritizing enjoyment over nutritional completeness.
  • Soft and Easily Digestible Foods: Provide foods that are easy to chew and swallow, especially for patients with dysphagia or oral discomfort.
  • Hydration for Comfort: Offer sips of water, ice chips, or favorite beverages to maintain oral comfort, not necessarily for nutritional hydration.
  • Avoid Force-Feeding: Respect the patient's declining appetite and refusal to eat; forcing food can cause distress and aspiration risk.
  • Manage Nausea and Vomiting: Administer antiemetics as prescribed, and offer bland, low-fat foods if tolerated.
  • Oral Care: Frequent mouth care is essential to prevent dryness, discomfort, and infections, regardless of food intake.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Observe and Report Changes: Carefully monitor for any changes in the patient's symptoms, comfort level, or mental status and communicate promptly with the healthcare team.
  • Provide Gentle Personal Care: Assist with hygiene, repositioning, and dressing with gentleness and respect for the patient's dignity.
  • Maintain a Calm Environment: Create a peaceful and supportive atmosphere, minimizing noise and unnecessary disturbances.
  • Facilitate Communication: Help the patient communicate their needs and wishes to the medical team and family members.
  • Offer Emotional Presence: Be present and offer comfort through touch, listening, and reassurance, respecting periods of quiet.
  • Caregiver Self-Care: Recognize the emotional toll of caregiving and seek support for yourself from family, friends, or support groups.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Early Palliative Care Consultation: Integrate palliative care early in the disease trajectory, not just at the very end of life, to optimize symptom management and support.
  • Shared Decision-Making: Engage patients and families in open, honest discussions about prognosis, treatment options, and goals of care, respecting their values and preferences.
  • Proactive Symptom Management: Anticipate and aggressively manage common symptoms such as pain, dyspnea, and nausea to prevent suffering.
  • Holistic Patient Assessment: Address not only physical symptoms but also psychological, social, and spiritual distress, recognizing their interconnectedness.
  • Ethical Considerations: Navigate complex ethical dilemmas surrounding treatment withdrawal, artificial nutrition, and resuscitation orders with sensitivity and adherence to patient wishes.
  • Support for Families: Provide ongoing communication and support to families, including bereavement care, as they cope with the patient's illness and eventual loss.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Palliative Care Principles: Understand the core tenets of palliative care, including symptom management, communication, and holistic support.
  • Pain Assessment Tools: Utilize appropriate pain assessment scales (e.g., ESAS, visual analog scale) for patients with advanced illness, adapting for cognitive impairment.
  • Communication Skills: Develop empathetic communication techniques for discussing difficult topics like prognosis, end-of-life wishes, and grief with patients and families.
  • Non-Pharmacological Interventions: Learn and apply comfort measures such as massage, aromatherapy, music therapy, and guided imagery to complement pharmacological treatments.
  • Ethical Decision-Making Frameworks: Apply ethical frameworks to navigate complex situations like withholding/withdrawing life-sustaining treatments.
  • Interdisciplinary Collaboration: Recognize the importance of working effectively within an interdisciplinary team to provide comprehensive patient care.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • POLST (Physician Orders for Life-Sustaining Treatment): A medical order that communicates and honors a patient's wishes for medical treatment.
  • ESAS (Edmonton Symptom Assessment System): A validated tool for assessing common symptoms in palliative care patients.
  • Opioid Rotation: A strategy used to manage intractable pain or intolerable side effects by switching to a different opioid analgesic.
  • Cachexia-Anorexia Syndrome: A complex metabolic syndrome characterized by involuntary weight loss, muscle wasting, and loss of appetite, common in advanced cancer.
  • Grief Counseling: Professional support for individuals experiencing loss, helping them process emotions and adapt to life without the deceased.
  • Spiritual Distress: A state of suffering related to the inability to find meaning, purpose, hope, love, peace, comfort, strength, or connection in life.
