Bilirubin test

🏠 Home / Pregnancy and childbirth / Newborns

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Test Purpose: A bilirubin test measures the level of bilirubin in a newborn's blood, a yellow pigment produced during the breakdown of red blood cells.
  • Neonatal Jaundice: Elevated bilirubin levels are the primary cause of neonatal jaundice, characterized by yellowing of the skin and eyes.
  • Types of Bilirubin: The test differentiates between unconjugated (indirect) and conjugated (direct) bilirubin, which helps determine the cause and severity of jaundice.
  • Risk of Kernicterus: Untreated, very high levels of unconjugated bilirubin can lead to kernicterus, a serious type of brain damage.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Visible Jaundice: Yellow discoloration of the skin and sclera (whites of the eyes), typically appearing first on the face and spreading downwards.
  • Lethargy and Poor Feeding: The newborn may appear unusually sleepy, have difficulty waking for feeds, or show poor sucking/latching.
  • Irritability or High-Pitched Cry: In severe cases, these can be signs of neurological involvement.
  • Dark Urine or Pale Stools: These symptoms, though less common, can indicate conjugated hyperbilirubinemia, which requires immediate investigation.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Timely Screening: All newborns should be screened for jaundice within the first 24-72 hours of life, or sooner if visible jaundice is present.
  • Risk Factor Assessment: Clinicians must assess for risk factors such as prematurity, ABO/Rh incompatibility, significant bruising, or sibling history of jaundice.
  • Close Monitoring: Newborns with rising bilirubin levels require frequent monitoring, often with repeat blood tests or transcutaneous bilirubin measurements.
  • Intervention Thresholds: Treatment decisions (e.g., phototherapy, exchange transfusion) are based on bilirubin levels, the newborn's age in hours, and specific risk factors.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Frequent Feeding: Encourage frequent breastfeeding or formula feeding (8-12 times in 24 hours) to promote bowel movements and bilirubin excretion.
  • Adequate Hydration: Ensure the newborn is receiving sufficient fluids through milk intake to aid in bilirubin elimination.
  • Avoid Water Supplementation: Unless medically advised, do not supplement with water as it does not help reduce bilirubin and can decrease caloric intake.
  • Monitor Intake: Observe for signs of adequate milk transfer, such as wet diapers and stool output, especially in breastfed infants.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Visual Assessment: Instruct parents on how to visually check for jaundice by gently pressing on the baby's skin to blanch it and observe for yellowing.
  • Report Worsening Jaundice: Emphasize the importance of immediately reporting any worsening jaundice, increased lethargy, or poor feeding to a healthcare provider.
  • Follow-up Appointments: Stress the necessity of attending all scheduled follow-up appointments for repeat bilirubin checks.
  • Phototherapy Care: If phototherapy is prescribed, educate parents on proper eye protection, frequent feeding, and maintaining the baby's skin exposure to the light.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Individualized Risk Assessment: Evaluate each newborn's unique risk profile for severe hyperbilirubinemia, considering gestational age, birth weight, and clinical course.
  • Utilize Nomograms: Apply age-specific bilirubin nomograms (e.g., Bhutani nomogram) to guide clinical decisions for phototherapy or exchange transfusion.
  • Differential Diagnosis: Consider other potential causes of jaundice, such as sepsis, metabolic disorders, or biliary atresia, especially with conjugated hyperbilirubinemia.
  • Parental Communication: Provide clear, empathetic explanations to parents regarding the diagnosis, treatment plan, and potential risks of hyperbilirubinemia.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Accurate Assessment Skills: Develop proficiency in visual assessment of jaundice and the use of transcutaneous bilirubinometers for initial screening.
  • Specimen Collection: Master the technique for heel stick blood collection, ensuring minimal trauma and accurate sample acquisition for laboratory analysis.
  • Phototherapy Management: Understand the principles of phototherapy, including proper setup, ensuring adequate skin exposure, eye protection, and temperature monitoring.
  • Parental Education and Support: Provide comprehensive education to parents on jaundice, feeding strategies, signs of worsening condition, and emotional support during treatment.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Kernicterus: Irreversible neurological damage caused by the deposition of unconjugated bilirubin in brain tissue.
  • Transcutaneous Bilirubin (TcB): A non-invasive method using a handheld device to estimate bilirubin levels through the skin, often used for screening.
  • Direct vs. Indirect Bilirubin: Direct (conjugated) bilirubin is water-soluble and excreted; indirect (unconjugated) bilirubin is fat-soluble and can cross the blood-brain barrier.
  • Phototherapy Mechanism: Light energy converts unconjugated bilirubin into water-soluble isomers that can be excreted in bile and urine without conjugation in the liver.
