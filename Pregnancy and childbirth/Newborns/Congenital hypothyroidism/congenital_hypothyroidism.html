Congenital hypothyroidism

🏠 Home / Pregnancy and childbirth / Newborns

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Definition: Congenital hypothyroidism is a condition present at birth where the thyroid gland does not produce enough thyroid hormone.
  • Critical Importance: Thyroid hormones are essential for normal brain development and physical growth in infants.
  • Newborn Screening: Universal newborn screening programs are crucial for early detection, typically via a heel prick test.
  • Treatment: Lifelong thyroid hormone replacement therapy (levothyroxine) is the standard treatment, preventing severe developmental delays.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Often Asymptomatic at Birth: Many newborns show no immediate signs due to residual maternal thyroid hormones.
  • Prolonged Jaundice: Yellowing of the skin and eyes that lasts longer than usual.
  • Poor Feeding & Lethargy: Difficulty with sucking, decreased activity, and excessive sleepiness.
  • Constipation: Infrequent or difficult bowel movements.
  • Hypotonia: Decreased muscle tone, often described as a 'floppy baby'.
  • Hoarse Cry: An unusual, low-pitched cry.
  • Umbilical Hernia: An outward bulge around the navel.
  • Macroglossia: An unusually large tongue.
  • Dry, Mottled Skin: Rough, cool, or discolored skin.
  • Large Fontanelles: Larger than expected soft spots on the baby's head.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Mandatory Newborn Screening: Ensure all newborns undergo the heel prick test for TSH and T4 levels within 24-72 hours of birth.
  • Prompt Follow-up: Immediately investigate and confirm any abnormal screening results to initiate treatment without delay.
  • Strict Medication Adherence: Administer prescribed levothyroxine daily without fail, as directed by the physician.
  • Regular Monitoring: Adhere to scheduled blood tests (TSH, free T4) to monitor hormone levels and adjust medication dosage, especially during infancy and childhood.
  • Developmental Surveillance: Closely monitor the infant's growth and neurodevelopmental milestones to assess treatment effectiveness.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Medication Timing: Administer levothyroxine on an empty stomach, typically 30-60 minutes before the first feed of the day.
  • Avoid Soy Products: Soy formula can interfere with levothyroxine absorption; if used, separate administration by several hours.
  • Separate from Iron/Calcium: Iron and calcium supplements can impair absorption; administer levothyroxine at least 4 hours apart from these.
  • Administration Method: Tablets can be crushed and mixed with a small amount of breast milk, formula, or water; do not mix with full feeds.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Consistent Dosing: Emphasize the critical importance of administering the exact prescribed dose of levothyroxine every day at the same time.
  • Medication Storage: Advise parents to store levothyroxine in a cool, dry place, away from direct light and moisture, and out of reach of children.
  • Missed Doses Protocol: Instruct parents on how to manage a missed dose (e.g., give as soon as remembered, but do not double dose).
  • Recognizing Imbalance: Educate parents on signs of over-treatment (e.g., irritability, poor weight gain, rapid heart rate) or under-treatment (e.g., lethargy, constipation, poor feeding) to report to the healthcare provider.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Timely Intervention is Key: Early diagnosis and initiation of levothyroxine treatment within the first few weeks of life are paramount for preventing irreversible intellectual disability.
  • Dynamic Dosage Adjustment: Levothyroxine dosage is weight-based and requires frequent titration, especially during infancy, guided by serial TSH and free T4 measurements.
  • Multidisciplinary Care: A collaborative approach involving pediatric endocrinologists, developmental pediatricians, and nutritionists ensures comprehensive management.
  • Parental Empowerment: Provide thorough education and ongoing support to parents, emphasizing the lifelong nature of the condition and the critical role of adherence.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Newborn Screening Protocols: Understand the local and national guidelines for congenital hypothyroidism screening, including specimen collection and result interpretation.
  • Medication Education Competency: Be proficient in educating parents on proper levothyroxine administration techniques, potential drug interactions, and storage.
  • Early Symptom Recognition: Develop keen assessment skills to identify subtle signs and symptoms of hypothyroidism in neonates and infants, prompting timely referral.
  • Family Support & Resources: Provide empathetic support and connect families with educational resources and support groups for managing a chronic condition.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Etiology: Primarily due to thyroid dysgenesis (agenesis, hypoplasia, ectopy) or dyshormonogenesis (defects in hormone synthesis).
  • Pathophysiology: Insufficient production of thyroid hormones (T4, T3) leads to impaired cellular metabolism, critically affecting brain development and somatic growth.
  • Diagnosis Confirmation: Elevated TSH and low free T4 on newborn screening, confirmed by venous blood draw, often followed by thyroid ultrasound or radionuclide scan to determine etiology.
  • Treatment Regimen: Oral levothyroxine (L-T4) replacement, typically initiated at 10-15 mcg/kg/day, adjusted based on TSH and free T4 levels.
  • Prognosis: Excellent neurodevelopmental outcomes with early and consistent treatment; untreated cases lead to severe intellectual disability (cretinism).
