Acute fatty liver of pregnancy

🏠 Home / Pregnancy and childbirth / Complications

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Rare but Severe Condition: Acute fatty liver of pregnancy (AFLP) is a life-threatening obstetric emergency characterized by microvesicular fatty infiltration of hepatocytes.
  • Typical Onset: Usually manifests in the third trimester of pregnancy or early postpartum period.
  • Underlying Mechanism: Often associated with a fetal genetic defect in fatty acid oxidation, such as long-chain 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase (LCHAD) deficiency.
  • Urgent Intervention Required: Prompt diagnosis and immediate delivery are crucial for maternal and fetal survival.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Early Gastrointestinal Symptoms: Profound nausea, persistent vomiting, and epigastric or right upper quadrant abdominal pain.
  • Systemic Manifestations: Malaise, headache, and progressive fatigue.
  • Liver Dysfunction Signs: Jaundice (yellowing of skin and eyes), dark urine, and pruritus (itching).
  • Neurological Changes: Encephalopathy, confusion, lethargy, and in severe cases, coma.
  • Other Complications: Polyuria, polydipsia (due to diabetes insipidus), acute pancreatitis, and signs of coagulopathy (e.g., easy bruising, bleeding).

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • High Index of Suspicion: Maintain a high clinical suspicion for AFLP in any pregnant woman presenting with new-onset gastrointestinal symptoms, jaundice, or altered mental status in late pregnancy.
  • Prompt Diagnostic Workup: Immediately initiate laboratory investigations including liver function tests, coagulation profile, renal function, electrolytes, and blood glucose.
  • Differential Diagnosis: Carefully differentiate AFLP from other conditions like HELLP syndrome, severe pre-eclampsia, and viral hepatitis, as management differs.
  • Continuous Monitoring: Ensure continuous maternal vital sign monitoring, neurological assessment, and fetal surveillance (cardiotocography).
  • Avoid Liver Biopsy if Coagulopathic: Liver biopsy, while definitive, is often deferred if significant coagulopathy is present due to bleeding risk; clinical and laboratory criteria usually suffice.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • NPO Status: Patients are typically kept nil per os (NPO) due to severe nausea, vomiting, and the high likelihood of requiring urgent delivery or surgical intervention.
  • Intravenous Hydration: Aggressive intravenous fluid resuscitation with dextrose-containing solutions is essential to correct dehydration and manage hypoglycemia.
  • Electrolyte Management: Close monitoring and correction of electrolyte imbalances, particularly potassium and phosphate.
  • Post-Delivery Reintroduction: Gradual reintroduction of oral fluids and a light diet may commence only after clinical stabilization and improvement in liver function, under strict medical supervision.
  • Nutritional Support: In cases of prolonged liver dysfunction or severe catabolism, parenteral nutrition may be required.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Multidisciplinary Team: Management requires a coordinated effort from obstetricians, intensivists, hepatologists, neonatologists, and anesthesiologists.
  • Expedited Delivery: Once diagnosed or strongly suspected, immediate delivery of the fetus is the definitive treatment, regardless of gestational age.
  • Coagulopathy Management: Be prepared to administer fresh frozen plasma (FFP), cryoprecipitate, and platelets to correct coagulopathy and prevent hemorrhage.
  • Hypoglycemia Protocol: Implement a strict protocol for monitoring and managing hypoglycemia, which can be profound and lead to neurological damage.
  • Postpartum Surveillance: Close monitoring in an intensive care setting is critical for several days postpartum, as liver function may continue to deteriorate before improvement.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Diagnostic Confirmation: Diagnosis relies on a constellation of clinical symptoms, characteristic laboratory abnormalities (e.g., elevated transaminases, bilirubin, prolonged PT/INR, hypoglycemia), and exclusion of other conditions.
  • Management Priority: The primary goals are to stabilize the mother, correct metabolic derangements, and achieve prompt delivery to halt disease progression.
  • Fetal Considerations: Fetal distress is common due to maternal illness; neonatal risks include prematurity, hypoglycemia, and potential LCHAD deficiency.
  • Recurrence Risk: The risk of recurrence in subsequent pregnancies is low unless an underlying genetic defect (e.g., LCHAD) is identified in the mother or father, necessitating genetic counseling.
  • Long-term Follow-up: Maternal liver function typically recovers completely, but long-term follow-up is important to ensure full resolution and screen for any residual complications.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Comprehensive Assessment: Perform frequent neurological assessments for signs of encephalopathy, monitor for bleeding, and assess abdominal pain characteristics.
  • Fluid and Electrolyte Balance: Meticulously track intake and output, monitor urine specific gravity, and administer IV fluids and electrolytes as prescribed.
  • Medication Administration: Administer dextrose infusions, vitamin K, and blood products (FFP, platelets) accurately and monitor for adverse reactions.
  • Patient and Family Support: Provide clear, empathetic communication to the patient and family regarding the severity of the condition and the urgent need for intervention.
  • Postpartum Care: Continue close monitoring of vital signs, liver function, and signs of recovery or complications; support breastfeeding if the mother is stable and desires.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Pathophysiology: Characterized by microvesicular steatosis of hepatocytes, often linked to defects in mitochondrial beta-oxidation of fatty acids, particularly LCHAD deficiency.
  • Swansea Criteria: A set of diagnostic criteria (e.g., vomiting, abdominal pain, polydipsia/polyuria, encephalopathy, jaundice, hypoglycemia, elevated AST/ALT, bilirubin, PT, urea, leukocytosis, ascites, bright liver on ultrasound) used to aid in diagnosis.
  • Key Complications: Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), acute renal failure, pancreatitis, hepatic encephalopathy, and high maternal and fetal mortality rates without timely intervention.
  • Genetic Linkage: Consider genetic testing for LCHAD deficiency in the infant and parents, as it can identify carriers and inform future pregnancy risks.
  • Management Principles: Aggressive supportive care, correction of metabolic derangements, and immediate delivery are the cornerstones of treatment.
