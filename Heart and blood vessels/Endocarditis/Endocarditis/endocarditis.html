Endocarditis

🏠 Home / Heart and blood vessels / Endocarditis

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Definition: Endocarditis is an inflammation of the inner lining of the heart chambers and heart valves (endocardium), most commonly caused by bacterial infection.
  • Primary Cause: Infective endocarditis typically results from bacteria entering the bloodstream and attaching to damaged heart valves or prosthetic heart devices.
  • Key Risk Factors: Include pre-existing heart valve conditions (e.g., rheumatic heart disease, bicuspid aortic valve), prosthetic heart valves, intravenous drug use, congenital heart defects, and poor dental hygiene.
  • Serious Complications: Can lead to severe heart valve damage, heart failure, systemic embolization (e.g., stroke, organ infarction), and abscess formation within the heart.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Persistent Fever and Chills: Often accompanied by night sweats and general malaise.
  • New or Changing Heart Murmur: A crucial clinical sign indicating potential valve involvement.
  • Fatigue and Weakness: Unexplained and progressive tiredness.
  • Petechiae and Splinter Hemorrhages: Small red spots on skin/mucous membranes and tiny red streaks under fingernails/toenails, respectively.
  • Osler's Nodes and Janeway Lesions: Painful, tender nodules on fingertips/toes (Osler's) and non-tender hemorrhagic macules on palms/soles (Janeway's).
  • Embolic Phenomena: Symptoms related to blood clots traveling to other organs, such as stroke-like symptoms, abdominal pain, or limb pain.
  • Shortness of Breath and Edema: Indicative of developing heart failure due to valve damage.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Antibiotic Prophylaxis: Essential for high-risk individuals (e.g., those with prosthetic valves, previous endocarditis) undergoing specific dental procedures or invasive medical interventions.
  • Meticulous Oral Hygiene: Regular dental check-ups and proper brushing/flossing to minimize bacteremia from oral sources.
  • Avoidance of IV Drug Use: If unavoidable, strict sterile injection practices are paramount to prevent bacterial introduction.
  • Aseptic Catheter Care: Strict adherence to sterile techniques for insertion and maintenance of central venous catheters and other indwelling medical devices.
  • Prompt Treatment of Infections: Early and effective treatment of any bacterial infections, especially skin infections or dental abscesses, to prevent bloodstream dissemination.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Adequate Hydration: Maintain good fluid intake, especially during febrile episodes or if on certain medications.
  • Nutritional Support: Focus on a balanced, nutrient-dense diet to support recovery and combat the catabolic state associated with chronic infection.
  • Sodium Restriction (if Heart Failure): If endocarditis leads to heart failure, dietary sodium restriction may be necessary to manage fluid retention.
  • Potassium Monitoring: If diuretics are prescribed for heart failure, monitor potassium levels and adjust dietary intake as advised by a physician.
  • Avoidance of Alcohol: Especially if liver function is compromised or if interacting with medications.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Seek Immediate Medical Attention: For unexplained fever, chills, new heart murmur, or signs of embolic events (e.g., sudden weakness, vision changes, severe pain).
  • Strict Adherence to Antibiotic Regimen: Complete the full course of prescribed antibiotics, even if symptoms improve, to ensure complete eradication of the infection.
  • Monitor for Complications: Be vigilant for signs of worsening heart failure (e.g., increasing shortness of breath, swelling), new neurological deficits, or persistent fever.
  • Regular Follow-up: Attend all scheduled follow-up appointments, including echocardiograms and blood tests, to monitor valve function and ensure infection clearance.
  • Educate Family Members: Inform close contacts about the condition and signs to watch for, especially during recovery.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Diagnostic Imperative: Early diagnosis relies on a high index of suspicion, multiple blood cultures, and comprehensive echocardiography (transthoracic and transesophageal).
  • Treatment Cornerstone: Prolonged courses of intravenous antibiotics are the mainstay of treatment, often requiring hospitalization and close monitoring.
  • Surgical Intervention: Indicated for severe valve dysfunction, uncontrolled infection despite antibiotics, large vegetations with high embolic risk, or recurrent emboli.
  • Risk Stratification: Crucial to identify patients at high risk for endocarditis to implement appropriate prophylactic strategies.
  • Multidisciplinary Approach: Management often requires collaboration between cardiologists, infectious disease specialists, and cardiac surgeons.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Patient Education: Instruct patients on the importance of medication adherence, recognizing symptoms of complications, and maintaining good hygiene.
  • IV Access Management: Meticulous care of central venous catheters for long-term antibiotic administration, including dressing changes and flush protocols.
  • Vital Sign Monitoring: Frequent assessment for fever, tachycardia, hypotension, and signs of hemodynamic instability.
  • Emboli Assessment: Perform regular neurological checks, assess peripheral pulses, and monitor for signs of organ ischemia (e.g., abdominal pain, flank pain).
  • Fluid Balance and Renal Function: Monitor intake and output, and assess for signs of fluid overload or renal impairment, especially with heart failure or nephrotoxic antibiotics.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Duke Criteria: A set of diagnostic criteria used to classify infective endocarditis as definite, possible, or rejected based on clinical, microbiological, and imaging findings.
  • Vegetations: Fibrin, platelet, and microbial aggregates that form on heart valves, characteristic of infective endocarditis, visible on echocardiography.
  • Bacteremia: The presence of bacteria in the bloodstream, a critical precursor for the development of infective endocarditis.
  • Prophylaxis Guidelines: Recommendations from professional bodies (e.g., AHA, ESC) outlining specific patient groups and procedures warranting antibiotic prophylaxis.
  • Transesophageal Echocardiography (TEE): A highly sensitive imaging modality for detecting vegetations, abscesses, and valve damage in endocarditis, especially when transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) is inconclusive.
