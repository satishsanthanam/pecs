Heart block in children

🏠 Home / Heart and blood vessels / Irregular heartbeat

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Definition: Heart block in children refers to a disruption in the electrical signals that control the heart's rhythm, leading to a slower-than-normal heart rate (bradycardia).
  • Types: It can be congenital (present at birth) or acquired later in life due to various causes.
  • Severity: Classified into first, second (Mobitz Type I or II), and third-degree (complete) blocks, with third-degree being the most severe.
  • Impact: A slow heart rate can reduce the heart's ability to pump enough blood to the body, affecting energy levels and organ function.
  • Congenital Heart Block: Often associated with maternal autoimmune diseases, such as lupus, where antibodies cross the placenta and damage the fetal heart's conduction system.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Infants: Poor feeding, lethargy, excessive sleeping, pale or bluish skin (cyanosis), difficulty breathing, and poor weight gain.
  • Older Children: Chronic fatigue, dizziness, lightheadedness, fainting spells (syncope), shortness of breath, and reduced exercise tolerance.
  • Palpitations: May occur, especially with intermittent or less severe forms of heart block, or if compensatory arrhythmias develop.
  • Prenatal Detection: Fetal bradycardia (slow heart rate) may be detected during routine prenatal ultrasound examinations.
  • Activity Intolerance: Children may struggle to keep up with peers during physical activities due to insufficient cardiac output.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Regular Cardiology Follow-ups: Adhere strictly to scheduled appointments with a pediatric cardiologist for ongoing monitoring and management.
  • Medication Review: Carefully review all medications with the cardiologist, as some drugs (e.g., certain beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers) can worsen bradycardia.
  • Symptom Monitoring: Parents and caregivers must be vigilant for any new or worsening symptoms, such as increased fatigue, fainting, or breathing difficulties.
  • Activity Restrictions: Depending on the severity and presence of symptoms, certain strenuous activities or contact sports may need to be limited or avoided.
  • Pacemaker Care: If a pacemaker is implanted, follow all instructions regarding device care, avoiding strong magnetic fields, and attending regular device checks.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Balanced Nutrition: Ensure a nutrient-rich diet to support overall growth, development, and energy levels, which are crucial for children with chronic conditions.
  • Adequate Hydration: Maintain good fluid intake to support cardiovascular function and overall well-being.
  • Electrolyte Balance: Focus on a diet that supports balanced electrolytes (e.g., potassium, magnesium) important for heart health, especially if on medications that affect these levels.
  • Avoid Stimulants: Limit or avoid caffeine and other stimulants that can affect heart rhythm or mask underlying symptoms.
  • Weight Management: Encourage a healthy weight to reduce additional strain on the heart.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Emergency Preparedness: Understand the signs of a cardiac emergency (e.g., prolonged syncope, severe cyanosis) and know when to seek immediate medical attention.
  • Medication Adherence: Administer all prescribed medications exactly as directed, understanding their purpose and potential side effects.
  • Medical Identification: Ensure the child wears a medical alert bracelet or carries an identification card detailing their heart condition and any implanted devices.
  • Educate Caregivers: Inform school staff, coaches, and other caregivers about the child's condition, symptoms to watch for, and emergency protocols.
  • Regular Diagnostic Tests: Adhere to schedules for ECGs, Holter monitors, echocardiograms, and pacemaker checks to assess heart function and device performance.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Early Diagnosis: Prompt identification, often prenatally or in infancy, is crucial for timely intervention and management planning.
  • Individualized Management: Treatment strategies vary widely based on the type and severity of heart block, presence of symptoms, and underlying cause.
  • Pacemaker Indication: Permanent pacemaker implantation is often necessary for symptomatic second-degree Mobitz Type II and all third-degree heart blocks to maintain adequate heart rate.
  • Underlying Causes: Investigate potential underlying causes such as maternal autoimmune disease, structural heart defects, or post-surgical complications.
  • Long-term Monitoring: Children with heart block require lifelong cardiology follow-up to monitor progression, device function, and overall cardiac health.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Cardiac Conduction System: Review the normal electrical pathway of the heart (SA node, AV node, Bundle of His, Purkinje fibers) and how heart block disrupts this.
  • ECG Interpretation: Learn to identify different degrees of AV block on an electrocardiogram, focusing on PR interval, P wave-QRS relationship, and QRS duration.
  • Nursing Assessment: Prioritize thorough assessment of vital signs (especially apical heart rate), perfusion status (capillary refill, skin color), and activity tolerance.
  • Patient Education: Educate families on the disease process, medication administration, symptom recognition, and care for implanted devices like pacemakers.
  • Post-operative Care: For pacemaker implantation, focus on incision site care, monitoring for infection, and educating on activity restrictions and device precautions.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Types of AV Block: First-degree AV block, Second-degree AV block (Mobitz Type I/Wenckebach, Mobitz Type II), Third-degree (Complete) AV block.
  • Etiology: Congenital (maternal anti-Ro/La antibodies, structural heart disease), Acquired (post-cardiac surgery, myocarditis, Lyme disease, certain medications).
  • Diagnostic Modalities: Electrocardiogram (ECG), Holter monitor, Event recorder, Echocardiogram, Fetal echocardiogram, Electrophysiology study (rarely in children).
  • Management Strategies: Observation, pharmacotherapy (e.g., isoproterenol for acute severe bradycardia), permanent pacemaker implantation.
  • Associated Conditions: Systemic lupus erythematosus (maternal), congenital heart defects (e.g., L-transposition of the great arteries), viral infections.
