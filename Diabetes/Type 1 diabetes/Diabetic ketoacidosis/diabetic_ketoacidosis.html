Diabetic ketoacidosis

🏠 Home / Diabetes / Type 1 diabetes

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Definition: Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) is a serious, life-threatening complication of diabetes, primarily Type 1, characterized by hyperglycemia, ketosis, and metabolic acidosis.
  • Cause: Occurs when the body lacks sufficient insulin to allow glucose into cells for energy, leading to the breakdown of fat for fuel and the production of acidic ketones.
  • Triggers: Common triggers include acute illness (e.g., infection), missed or insufficient insulin doses, new diagnosis of Type 1 diabetes, trauma, surgery, or certain medications.
  • Urgency: DKA is a medical emergency requiring immediate hospitalization and aggressive treatment to prevent severe complications or death.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Early Symptoms: Increased thirst (polydipsia), frequent urination (polyuria), and fatigue.
  • Gastrointestinal Distress: Nausea, vomiting, and severe abdominal pain are common due to ketosis and acidosis.
  • Respiratory Changes: Deep, rapid breathing (Kussmaul respirations) as the body attempts to expel excess carbon dioxide to correct acidosis.
  • Fruity Breath Odor: A distinct sweet or fruity smell on the breath, caused by the exhalation of acetone, a type of ketone.
  • Dehydration Signs: Dry mouth, sunken eyes, decreased skin turgor, and reduced urine output.
  • Neurological Impairment: Lethargy, confusion, altered mental status, and in severe cases, coma.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Insulin Adherence: Never skip insulin doses, even when feeling unwell or unable to eat. Consult a healthcare provider for sick day management.
  • Blood Glucose Monitoring: Regularly monitor blood glucose levels as advised by your physician, especially during illness or stress.
  • Ketone Testing: Test for ketones in urine or blood when blood glucose levels are consistently high (e.g., >250-300 mg/dL) or during illness.
  • Hydration: Maintain adequate fluid intake, particularly water, to help flush out ketones and prevent dehydration.
  • Sick Day Rules: Follow a pre-established sick day plan with your healthcare team, which includes guidelines for insulin adjustment, fluid intake, and when to seek medical help.
  • Early Recognition: Educate yourself and your family on the signs and symptoms of DKA to ensure prompt medical attention.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Initial Management: During acute DKA, oral intake is typically restricted (NPO) to prevent aspiration and allow for intravenous fluid and insulin therapy.
  • Fluid Resuscitation: Intravenous fluids are critical for rehydration and electrolyte balance; oral fluids are not usually permitted until stable.
  • Gradual Reintroduction: Once DKA is resolving and the patient is stable, clear liquids may be gradually introduced, progressing to a soft diet as tolerated.
  • Electrolyte Balance: Dietary considerations post-DKA focus on maintaining electrolyte balance, often with medical supervision for potassium and phosphate levels.
  • Post-DKA Diet: Resume a regular, balanced diabetes meal plan, emphasizing consistent carbohydrate intake and healthy food choices to prevent future episodes.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Emergency Response: If DKA is suspected, immediately call emergency services (e.g., 911) or transport the patient to the nearest emergency department.
  • Vital Monitoring: Continuously monitor the patient's level of consciousness, breathing pattern, and vital signs while awaiting medical help.
  • Fluid Access: Do not attempt to give oral fluids to an unconscious or severely nauseated patient due to aspiration risk.
  • Medication Information: Have all current medication lists, insulin types, and recent blood glucose/ketone readings readily available for medical personnel.
  • Emotional Support: Provide reassurance and comfort to the patient, who may be distressed, confused, or anxious.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Diagnostic Confirmation: DKA is diagnosed by hyperglycemia (>250 mg/dL), ketonemia/ketonuria, and metabolic acidosis (pH <7.3, bicarbonate <18 mEq/L).
  • Treatment Pillars: Management involves aggressive intravenous fluid resuscitation, continuous intravenous insulin infusion, and careful electrolyte replacement, particularly potassium.
  • Identify Precipitant: Crucial to identify and treat the underlying cause of DKA, such as infection, to prevent recurrence.
  • Monitor for Complications: Watch for potential complications like cerebral edema (especially in children), hypokalemia, and acute kidney injury.
  • Patient Education: Emphasize comprehensive education on sick day management, insulin adherence, and ketone monitoring to empower patients in preventing future episodes.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Pathophysiology Review: Understand the cascade of insulin deficiency leading to increased counter-regulatory hormones, lipolysis, ketogenesis, and subsequent metabolic acidosis.
  • Prioritized Assessment: Focus on ABCs (Airway, Breathing, Circulation), neurological status, hydration status, and frequent vital sign monitoring.
  • Intervention Protocol: Administer prescribed IV fluids (e.g., 0.9% NS initially, then D5NS once glucose is <200-250 mg/dL) and initiate continuous IV insulin infusion per protocol.
  • Electrolyte Management: Closely monitor serum potassium, phosphate, and magnesium levels, anticipating replacement as needed, even if initial potassium is normal or high.
  • Patient Education Role: Provide thorough education on sick day management, proper insulin administration, blood glucose and ketone monitoring, and when to seek urgent medical care.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Diagnostic Criteria: Blood glucose >250 mg/dL, arterial pH <7.3, serum bicarbonate <18 mEq/L, and moderate to large ketonuria or ketonemia.
  • Insulin Infusion Rate: Typically initiated at 0.1 units/kg/hour IV, adjusted to decrease blood glucose by 50-75 mg/dL/hour.
  • Fluid Resuscitation: Initial bolus of 0.9% NaCl (10-20 mL/kg over 1 hour), followed by continuous infusion, transitioning to D5NS when glucose reaches 200-250 mg/dL.
  • Potassium Replacement: Initiate potassium replacement when serum K+ is <5.0-5.2 mEq/L, even if initial levels are normal or high, due to anticipated intracellular shift with insulin therapy.
  • Anion Gap Calculation: (Na+) - (Cl- + HCO3-). An elevated anion gap (>12 mEq/L) is characteristic of DKA.
