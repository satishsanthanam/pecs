Giving your child insulin

🏠 Home / Diabetes / Diabetes in children

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Purpose of Insulin: Insulin is a vital hormone that helps your child's body use glucose (sugar) for energy. In Type 1 diabetes, the pancreas does not produce insulin, requiring daily replacement.
  • Types of Insulin: Understand the different types your child is prescribed, such as rapid-acting (taken before meals), long-acting (provides basal coverage), or intermediate-acting, and their respective onset, peak, and duration.
  • Consistent Administration: Insulin must be given regularly as prescribed by the healthcare team to maintain blood glucose levels within the target range and prevent complications.
  • Injection Sites: Insulin is administered subcutaneously (under the skin). Common sites include the abdomen, thighs, buttocks, and upper arms.
  • Rotation of Sites: Regularly rotate injection sites to prevent lipohypertrophy (fatty lumps) or lipoatrophy (indentations), which can affect insulin absorption.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Signs of Hypoglycemia (Low Blood Sugar): Watch for shakiness, sweating, rapid heartbeat, hunger, dizziness, confusion, irritability, or sudden mood changes.
  • Signs of Hyperglycemia (High Blood Sugar): Observe for increased thirst, frequent urination, fatigue, blurred vision, headache, or unexplained weight loss.
  • Symptoms of Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA): Be alert for severe abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, deep and rapid breathing (Kussmaul respirations), fruity-smelling breath, and lethargy.
  • Unexplained Fatigue: Persistent tiredness or lack of energy can indicate blood glucose levels are consistently out of target range.
  • Frequent Infections: Recurrent skin infections, yeast infections, or slow-healing sores can be a sign of poorly controlled diabetes.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Insulin Storage: Unopened insulin should be refrigerated (not frozen). Once opened, insulin pens or vials can be kept at room temperature for a specified period (check manufacturer guidelines, typically 28 days).
  • Dosage Accuracy: Always double-check the insulin dose with another adult if possible, especially for young children, to prevent medication errors.
  • Correct Syringe/Pen Needle: Use the correct type and size of syringe or pen needle as recommended by your healthcare provider. Never reuse needles.
  • Emergency Glucagon: Keep an emergency glucagon kit readily available and ensure all caregivers know how and when to administer it for severe hypoglycemia.
  • Travel Preparedness: Always carry extra insulin, syringes/pens, blood glucose monitoring supplies, and a doctor's note when traveling.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Carbohydrate Counting: Learn to accurately count carbohydrates in foods to match insulin doses, especially for rapid-acting insulin given before meals.
  • Consistent Meal Times: Strive for consistent meal and snack times, particularly if your child is on fixed insulin doses, to help prevent blood sugar fluctuations.
  • Balanced Nutrition: Focus on a diet rich in whole grains, lean proteins, fruits, and vegetables. Limit sugary drinks, processed foods, and excessive saturated fats.
  • Hydration: Encourage adequate water intake, especially during periods of high blood sugar, to help prevent dehydration.
  • Sick Day Management: During illness, continue insulin administration, monitor blood glucose and ketones more frequently, and ensure adequate fluid and carbohydrate intake to prevent DKA.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Comprehensive Training: Ensure all individuals responsible for your child's care (e.g., school nurses, grandparents, babysitters) are thoroughly trained in insulin administration and hypoglycemia management.
  • Emergency Action Plan: Develop a clear, written emergency action plan for hypoglycemia and hyperglycemia, including contact information for the healthcare team.
  • Blood Glucose Monitoring: Regularly monitor blood glucose levels as instructed by the healthcare provider, documenting results to guide insulin adjustments.
  • Ketone Testing: Test for ketones in urine or blood during periods of high blood sugar or illness to detect and prevent DKA.
  • Communication with School: Provide the school nurse and teachers with detailed information about your child's diabetes management plan, including insulin doses, meal times, and emergency protocols.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Individualized Treatment Plan: Every child's insulin regimen is unique and will be adjusted based on growth, activity, diet, and blood glucose patterns. Regular follow-ups are crucial.
  • Empowerment Through Education: Active participation of the child and family in learning about diabetes management is key to successful long-term outcomes.
  • Psychosocial Support: Acknowledge the emotional burden of diabetes. Seek support from diabetes educators, social workers, or support groups to help your child and family cope.
  • Regular A1C Monitoring: Hemoglobin A1C tests provide an average blood glucose level over 2-3 months, offering a valuable indicator of long-term control.
  • Preventing Complications: Strict adherence to the insulin regimen and blood glucose targets significantly reduces the risk of long-term diabetes complications affecting eyes, kidneys, and nerves.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Insulin Injection Technique: Master proper subcutaneous injection technique, including site selection, skin preparation, needle insertion angle, and safe disposal of sharps.
  • Patient and Family Education: Provide clear, age-appropriate education on insulin types, administration, storage, blood glucose monitoring, and sick day rules.
  • Hypoglycemia Management Protocol: Understand and teach the 'Rule of 15' for treating mild to moderate hypoglycemia (15g fast-acting carbohydrate, recheck in 15 minutes).
  • Insulin Pump Therapy: Familiarize yourself with the basics of continuous subcutaneous insulin infusion (insulin pumps) and continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) systems.
  • Growth and Development Considerations: Recognize how growth spurts, puberty, and varying activity levels impact insulin requirements in pediatric patients.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Pharmacokinetics of Insulin: Understand the onset, peak, and duration of action for various insulin preparations (e.g., Lispro, Aspart, Glargine, Detemir) to optimize dosing schedules.
  • Insulin Sensitivity Factor (ISF): The amount blood glucose drops per unit of insulin, crucial for correction doses.
  • Carbohydrate-to-Insulin Ratio (CIR): The number of grams of carbohydrates covered by one unit of rapid-acting insulin, essential for mealtime dosing.
  • Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA) Pathophysiology: Understand the cascade of insulin deficiency leading to hyperglycemia, ketogenesis, and metabolic acidosis.
  • Lipohypertrophy and Lipoatrophy: Pathological changes at injection sites due to repeated injections, impairing insulin absorption and requiring site rotation.
