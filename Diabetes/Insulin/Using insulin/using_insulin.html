Using insulin

🏠 Home / Diabetes / Insulin

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Insulin's Role: Insulin is a vital hormone that helps your body use glucose (sugar) for energy. In diabetes, the body either doesn't produce enough insulin or can't use it effectively.
  • Types of Insulin: Insulin comes in various forms, including rapid-acting, short-acting, intermediate-acting, and long-acting, each with different onset, peak, and duration of action.
  • Administration: Insulin is typically administered via subcutaneous injection using a syringe, insulin pen, or an insulin pump.
  • Purpose of Therapy: Insulin therapy aims to mimic the body's natural insulin production, helping to maintain blood glucose levels within a target range and prevent complications.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Hyperglycemia Indicators: Symptoms prompting insulin initiation often include excessive thirst (polydipsia), frequent urination (polyuria), increased hunger (polyphagia), unexplained weight loss, and blurred vision.
  • Hypoglycemia Symptoms: If too much insulin is administered or meals are delayed, symptoms like shakiness, sweating, dizziness, confusion, rapid heartbeat, and extreme hunger may occur.
  • Ketoacidosis Signs: Severe insulin deficiency can lead to diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), characterized by nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, deep rapid breathing (Kussmaul respirations), and fruity-smelling breath.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Storage Guidelines: Store unopened insulin vials or pens in the refrigerator. Once opened, store at room temperature and discard after 28-30 days (check specific product instructions).
  • Injection Site Rotation: Rotate injection sites (abdomen, thighs, upper arms, buttocks) to prevent lipohypertrophy (fat lumps) and ensure consistent absorption.
  • Blood Glucose Monitoring: Regularly monitor blood glucose levels as advised by your healthcare provider to adjust insulin doses and prevent hypo- or hyperglycemia.
  • Checking Expiration: Always check the expiration date on insulin products before use. Discard any insulin that appears cloudy (unless it's NPH insulin), discolored, or has particles.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Carbohydrate Counting: Learn to count carbohydrates in your meals and snacks, as this directly impacts the amount of rapid-acting or short-acting insulin needed.
  • Consistent Meal Timing: Maintain consistent meal and snack times, especially with intermediate or long-acting insulin, to prevent blood glucose fluctuations.
  • Avoid Skipping Meals: Never skip meals, particularly after taking insulin, as this significantly increases the risk of hypoglycemia.
  • Alcohol Consumption: Alcohol can lower blood glucose levels, especially when combined with insulin. Consume alcohol in moderation and always with food, monitoring blood glucose closely.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Hypoglycemia Treatment: If blood glucose drops below 70 mg/dL, consume 15 grams of fast-acting carbohydrates (e.g., 4 oz juice, 3-4 glucose tablets), wait 15 minutes, and recheck. Repeat if necessary.
  • Never Share Needles: Insulin pens and syringes are for single-patient use only. Sharing needles or pens can transmit infections.
  • Proper Disposal: Dispose of used needles and syringes in a sharps container or a hard plastic container (e.g., laundry detergent bottle) with a tight-fitting lid.
  • Medical Identification: Always carry medical identification (e.g., bracelet, wallet card) indicating you have diabetes and use insulin.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Individualized Dosing: Insulin regimens are highly individualized. Adhere strictly to your prescribed dose and schedule, and never adjust without consulting your physician.
  • Regular Follow-ups: Consistent follow-up appointments are crucial for monitoring blood glucose control, assessing for complications, and making necessary adjustments to your insulin therapy.
  • Comprehensive Education: Understand the different types of insulin you use, their onset and duration, and how they interact with food, exercise, and other medications.
  • Recognize Complications: Be vigilant for signs of both acute (hypoglycemia, DKA) and chronic (neuropathy, retinopathy) complications, reporting any concerns promptly.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Patient Education Priority: Nurses play a critical role in educating patients on proper insulin administration technique, site rotation, storage, and recognition/management of hypo/hyperglycemia.
  • Assessment and Documentation: Thoroughly assess patient's understanding and ability to self-administer insulin. Document blood glucose readings, insulin doses, and patient responses accurately.
  • Sick Day Management: Educate patients on sick day rules for diabetes, emphasizing continued insulin use, frequent blood glucose monitoring, and hydration, even if appetite is poor.
  • Psychosocial Support: Acknowledge the psychological impact of insulin therapy. Provide support and resources to help patients integrate insulin into their daily lives effectively.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Pharmacokinetics: Understand the distinct pharmacokinetic profiles (onset, peak, duration) of various insulin analogs (e.g., Lispro, Aspart, Glargine, Detemir, Degludec) to optimize dosing strategies.
  • Basal-Bolus Regimens: Familiarity with basal-bolus insulin regimens, which mimic physiological insulin secretion, involving a long-acting basal insulin and rapid-acting bolus insulin with meals.
  • Insulin Pump Therapy: Knowledge of continuous subcutaneous insulin infusion (CSII) via insulin pumps, including programming, site management, and troubleshooting.
  • C-Peptide Levels: C-peptide levels can help differentiate between Type 1 and Type 2 diabetes by indicating endogenous insulin production.
  • Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA) Management: Protocols for managing DKA, including intravenous insulin infusions, fluid resuscitation, and electrolyte correction.
