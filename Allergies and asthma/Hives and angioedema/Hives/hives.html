Hives

🏠 Home / Allergies and asthma / Hives and angioedema

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Definition: Hives, medically known as urticaria, are intensely itchy, raised, red, or skin-colored welts (wheals) that appear on the skin.
  • Appearance: They can vary in size, from small spots to large patches, and often blanch (turn white) when pressed.
  • Duration: Acute hives last less than six weeks, often resolving within hours to days. Chronic hives persist for more than six weeks, sometimes for months or years.
  • Causes: Triggers can be allergic (e.g., foods, medications, insect stings) or non-allergic (e.g., stress, heat, cold, pressure, infections, autoimmune conditions).
  • Angioedema: Hives can sometimes be accompanied by angioedema, which is swelling in the deeper layers of the skin, often around the eyes, lips, or genitals.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Intense Itching: The primary and most bothersome symptom, often worse at night.
  • Raised Welts (Wheals): Red or skin-colored bumps that can appear anywhere on the body, often changing shape and migrating.
  • Blanching: Welts typically turn white when pressure is applied to their center.
  • Angioedema: Swelling of the lips, eyelids, tongue, throat, hands, feet, or genitals, which may be painful or cause a burning sensation rather than itching.
  • Systemic Symptoms (Severe Cases): Difficulty breathing, wheezing, dizziness, rapid heart rate, low blood pressure, or fainting, indicating a severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis).
  • Trigger-Specific Symptoms: Depending on the cause, other symptoms like gastrointestinal upset (nausea, vomiting, diarrhea) may occur with food allergies.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Identify and Avoid Triggers: Keep a detailed diary of symptoms, potential exposures, and diet to help pinpoint and subsequently avoid specific triggers.
  • Avoid Scratching: Resist the urge to scratch, as this can worsen itching, damage the skin, and increase the risk of infection.
  • Monitor for Angioedema: Be vigilant for signs of deeper swelling, especially around the face or throat, which requires immediate medical attention.
  • Anaphylaxis Preparedness: If prescribed an epinephrine auto-injector for severe allergic reactions, ensure it is always accessible and that you know how to use it.
  • Wear Loose Clothing: Tight clothing can irritate the skin and exacerbate hives. Opt for loose-fitting, breathable fabrics.
  • Avoid Heat: Hot showers, baths, or excessive heat can worsen itching and trigger hives in some individuals. Use lukewarm water.
  • Stress Management: High stress levels can be a trigger or exacerbate existing hives; practice stress-reduction techniques.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Common Food Allergens: Be aware of common food allergens such as peanuts, tree nuts, shellfish, milk, eggs, soy, and wheat, which can trigger acute hives.
  • Food Additives: Certain food additives, preservatives (e.g., sulfites, benzoates), and artificial colors (e.g., tartrazine) can provoke hives in sensitive individuals.
  • Histamine-Rich Foods: Some people with chronic hives may benefit from avoiding or limiting foods high in histamine, such as aged cheeses, fermented foods, processed meats, some fish (e.g., tuna, mackerel), spinach, tomatoes, and alcohol.
  • Pseudoallergens: Certain natural food components, sometimes referred to as pseudoallergens (e.g., salicylates in fruits and vegetables), can trigger non-allergic histamine release.
  • Elimination Diet: Under medical supervision, a structured elimination diet can help identify specific food triggers for chronic hives.
  • Hydration: Maintain adequate hydration with plain water, avoiding very hot beverages that might increase skin temperature and worsen itching.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Emergency Care: Seek immediate emergency medical attention if hives are accompanied by difficulty breathing, swelling of the tongue or throat, dizziness, or a sudden drop in blood pressure.
  • Chronic Hives Evaluation: For hives lasting longer than six weeks, consult a physician or allergist for a thorough evaluation to identify underlying causes and develop a management plan.
  • Medication Adherence: Follow prescribed medication regimens, such as antihistamines, exactly as directed by your healthcare provider.
  • Educate Caregivers: Inform family members, school staff, or caregivers about your condition, potential triggers, and the emergency action plan, especially if an epinephrine auto-injector is prescribed.
  • Symptom Diary: Maintain a detailed diary of when hives appear, their severity, potential triggers, and any medications taken to aid diagnosis and management.
  • Avoid Self-Medication: Do not introduce new medications or supplements without consulting a healthcare professional, as some can worsen hives.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Diagnosis: Diagnosis is primarily clinical, based on patient history and physical examination of the skin lesions. Allergy testing may be performed to identify specific triggers.
  • First-Line Treatment: Non-sedating H1 antihistamines are the cornerstone of treatment for both acute and chronic hives, often requiring higher doses for chronic cases.
  • Corticosteroids: Oral corticosteroids may be prescribed for severe acute flares or exacerbations of chronic hives, typically for short durations due to potential side effects.
  • Omalizumab: For chronic idiopathic urticaria unresponsive to high-dose antihistamines, omalizumab (an anti-IgE antibody) may be considered.
  • Underlying Conditions: A thorough workup may be necessary to rule out underlying infections, autoimmune diseases, or other medical conditions contributing to chronic hives.
  • Referral: Patients with persistent, severe, or atypical hives, or those with angioedema, should be referred to an allergist or dermatologist for specialized care.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Pathophysiology: Hives result from the release of histamine and other inflammatory mediators from mast cells and basophils in the skin, leading to vasodilation and increased vascular permeability.
  • Nursing Assessment: Conduct a comprehensive skin assessment (location, size, morphology, blanching, presence of angioedema), vital signs, and airway assessment, especially if anaphylaxis is suspected.
  • Patient Education: Educate patients on trigger identification and avoidance, proper use of antihistamines and emergency medications (e.g., epinephrine auto-injector), and the importance of a symptom diary.
  • Differential Diagnosis: Consider other dermatological conditions such as contact dermatitis, insect bites, drug eruptions, and erythema multiforme in the differential diagnosis.
  • Psychosocial Support: Acknowledge the significant impact of chronic itching and visible skin lesions on quality of life and provide resources for psychosocial support.
  • Medication Management: Instruct on the timing and potential side effects of antihistamines, and the importance of adherence, even when symptoms improve.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Urticaria: The medical term for hives, characterized by transient, pruritic, erythematous, and edematous wheals.
  • Angioedema: Swelling of the deeper dermal and subcutaneous tissues, often associated with urticaria, and potentially life-threatening if affecting the airway.
  • Anaphylaxis: A severe, potentially fatal systemic allergic reaction involving multiple organ systems, requiring immediate medical intervention.
  • Histamine: A chemical mediator released by mast cells and basophils, primarily responsible for the itching and swelling in hives.
  • Antihistamines (H1 Blockers): Medications that block histamine receptors, reducing itching and swelling; first-line treatment for urticaria.
  • Chronic Idiopathic Urticaria (CIU): Hives lasting more than six weeks without an identifiable external cause, also known as chronic spontaneous urticaria.
  • Mast Cells: Immune cells found in connective tissues, particularly in the skin, that release histamine and other inflammatory mediators.
  • Dermographism: A type of physical urticaria where hives appear in response to firm stroking or scratching of the skin.
