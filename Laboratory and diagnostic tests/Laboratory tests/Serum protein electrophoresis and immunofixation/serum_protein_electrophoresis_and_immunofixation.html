Serum protein electrophoresis and immunofixation

🏠 Home / Laboratory and diagnostic tests / Laboratory tests

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Purpose of SPEP: Serum protein electrophoresis (SPEP) separates serum proteins into distinct fractions (albumin, alpha-1, alpha-2, beta, gamma globulins) to detect and quantify abnormal proteins.
  • Purpose of Immunofixation (IFE): Immunofixation electrophoresis (IFE) is performed when an abnormal protein (M-spike) is detected by SPEP to identify the specific type of monoclonal immunoglobulin (IgG, IgA, IgM, kappa, lambda light chains).
  • Primary Indication: These tests are crucial for the diagnosis and monitoring of monoclonal gammopathies, such as multiple myeloma, Waldenström's macroglobulinemia, and amyloidosis.
  • M-protein Detection: The presence of a monoclonal protein (M-protein or paraprotein) is a hallmark of these conditions, indicating an overproduction of a single clone of plasma cells.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Unexplained Bone Pain: Especially in the back, ribs, or long bones, often a symptom of multiple myeloma.
  • Persistent Fatigue and Weakness: Commonly due to anemia, which can be a complication of monoclonal gammopathies.
  • Recurrent Infections: Impaired normal immune function due to the proliferation of abnormal plasma cells.
  • Unexplained Weight Loss: A general symptom of underlying systemic illness.
  • Neuropathy: Tingling, numbness, or weakness in extremities, sometimes associated with amyloidosis or other monoclonal gammopathies.
  • Kidney Dysfunction: Manifesting as elevated creatinine, proteinuria, or signs of renal failure.
  • Hypercalcemia Symptoms: Confusion, constipation, increased thirst, and frequent urination, due to bone destruction.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Sample Hemolysis: Avoid hemolysis during blood collection as it can interfere with protein separation and quantification.
  • Lipemic Samples: Severe lipemia can affect the accuracy of protein measurements; fasting may be recommended by some laboratories.
  • Recent Immunizations: Recent vaccinations can cause a transient polyclonal increase in gamma globulins, potentially complicating interpretation.
  • Acute Inflammatory States: Active infections or inflammatory conditions can elevate acute phase reactants and polyclonal immunoglobulins, which may mask or mimic monoclonal spikes.
  • Medication Interference: Certain medications, such as corticosteroids or immunosuppressants, can alter protein levels and should be noted on the requisition.
  • Hydration Status: Severe dehydration can lead to falsely elevated protein concentrations; ensure the patient is adequately hydrated.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Fasting Requirements: Generally, fasting is not strictly required for SPEP and IFE, but some laboratories may recommend an overnight fast (8-12 hours) to minimize lipemia.
  • Hydration: Maintain normal hydration levels; avoid excessive fluid intake or severe dehydration before the test, as it can impact protein concentration.
  • Alcohol Consumption: Avoid excessive alcohol intake prior to the test, as it can affect liver function and protein synthesis.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Patient Identification: Meticulously verify patient identity using at least two identifiers before blood collection.
  • Venipuncture Technique: Use standard aseptic technique for venipuncture to collect a venous blood sample.
  • Sample Collection: Collect blood in a serum separator tube (SST) or a plain red-top tube.
  • Sample Processing: Allow the blood to clot completely (typically 20-30 minutes at room temperature) before centrifuging. Separate serum promptly.
  • Sample Storage: If not processed immediately, refrigerate the serum sample at 2-8°C. For longer delays, freezing at -20°C or colder is recommended.
  • Labeling: Ensure all tubes are accurately labeled with patient name, date of birth, date and time of collection, and collector's initials.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Diagnostic Cornerstone: SPEP and IFE are indispensable for the diagnosis, classification, and monitoring of monoclonal gammopathies.
  • Interpretation of M-spike: The presence of an M-spike on SPEP, followed by specific identification via IFE, is critical for confirming a monoclonal gammopathy.
  • Clinical Correlation: Results must always be interpreted in conjunction with the patient's clinical presentation, symptoms, and other laboratory findings (e.g., complete blood count, calcium, creatinine, quantitative immunoglobulins).
  • Monitoring Disease Activity: Serial SPEP measurements are used to track M-protein levels, assess response to treatment, and detect disease progression or relapse.
  • Differential Diagnosis: These tests help differentiate between benign conditions like MGUS (monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance) and malignant conditions such as multiple myeloma or Waldenström's macroglobulinemia.
  • Further Evaluation: Abnormal results often necessitate further investigations, including bone marrow biopsy, imaging studies, and urine protein electrophoresis/immunofixation.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Patient Education: Educate patients about the purpose of the tests, the blood collection procedure, and any pre-test instructions (e.g., fasting if required).
  • Pre-analytical Error Prevention: Understand the importance of proper sample collection, handling, and storage to prevent pre-analytical errors that can affect test results.
  • Recognizing Clinical Indications: Be aware of the symptoms and clinical scenarios that prompt a physician to order SPEP and IFE, such as unexplained bone pain or recurrent infections.
  • Post-Procedure Care: Provide standard post-venipuncture care, including applying pressure to the site and observing for hematoma formation.
  • Communication: Effectively communicate any patient concerns, medication history, or potential pre-analytical issues to the laboratory or ordering physician.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Monoclonal Gammopathy: A condition characterized by the overproduction of a single type of immunoglobulin (M-protein) by a clonal population of plasma cells.
  • M-protein (Monoclonal Protein/Paraprotein): An abnormal, homogeneous immunoglobulin or immunoglobulin fragment produced by a single clone of B lymphocytes or plasma cells.
  • Albumin: The most abundant plasma protein, responsible for maintaining oncotic pressure and transporting various substances.
  • Gamma Globulins: A protein fraction primarily composed of immunoglobulins (antibodies), crucial for immune defense.
  • Kappa and Lambda Light Chains: Components of immunoglobulins; an imbalance or excess of free light chains can indicate a monoclonal gammopathy.
  • Polyclonal Gammopathy: An increase in multiple types of immunoglobulins, typically seen in chronic inflammation, infection, or autoimmune diseases.
  • Multiple Myeloma: A malignant plasma cell disorder characterized by the proliferation of monoclonal plasma cells in the bone marrow, leading to M-protein production and organ damage.
  • Waldenström's Macroglobulinemia: A rare, slow-growing type of non-Hodgkin lymphoma characterized by the overproduction of IgM monoclonal protein.
