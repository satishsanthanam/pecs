Gastric sleeve – Discharge instructions

🏠 Home / Surgery / Weight loss surgery

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Adherence to Post-Operative Plan: Strict compliance with dietary stages, medication schedules, and activity restrictions is paramount for successful recovery and weight loss.
  • Follow-Up Appointments: Regular visits with your surgeon, dietitian, and bariatric team are essential for monitoring progress, adjusting care, and addressing any concerns.
  • Hydration is Key: Sip fluids constantly throughout the day to prevent dehydration, which is a common post-surgical risk. Avoid drinking with meals.
  • Medication Management: Take all prescribed medications as directed, including pain relievers, anti-nausea drugs, and acid suppressants.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Fever and Chills: Report any temperature above 101°F (38.3°C) or persistent chills, as these can indicate infection.
  • Severe Abdominal Pain: Unrelenting or worsening abdominal pain, especially if radiating to the shoulder, could signal a leak or other serious complication.
  • Persistent Nausea or Vomiting: Inability to keep fluids down for more than 24 hours, or frequent vomiting, requires immediate medical attention due to dehydration risk.
  • Redness, Swelling, or Discharge at Incision Sites: These are signs of potential wound infection and should be reported to your surgical team.
  • Shortness of Breath or Chest Pain: Seek emergency care immediately for any difficulty breathing, chest discomfort, or calf pain/swelling, which could indicate a blood clot.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Activity Restrictions: Avoid heavy lifting (over 10-15 lbs) and strenuous activities for at least 4-6 weeks post-surgery to prevent hernia formation and promote healing.
  • Wound Care: Keep incision sites clean and dry. Follow specific instructions for dressing changes. Do not submerge wounds in baths or pools until cleared by your surgeon.
  • Avoid NSAIDs: Refrain from using Non-Steroidal Anti-Inflammatory Drugs (NSAIDs) like ibuprofen or naproxen, as they can irritate the gastric pouch and increase ulcer risk.
  • No Smoking or Alcohol: Absolutely no smoking, vaping, or alcohol consumption, as these can impair healing, increase complication risk, and contribute to nutritional deficiencies.
  • Driving Restrictions: Do not drive while taking narcotic pain medication or if you are still experiencing significant pain or weakness.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Staged Diet Progression: Strictly follow the prescribed post-operative diet, typically progressing from clear liquids to full liquids, pureed foods, soft foods, and then regular solid foods over several weeks.
  • Small, Frequent Meals: Eat very small portions (e.g., 2-4 ounces) slowly, taking 20-30 minutes per meal, to prevent discomfort, nausea, and vomiting.
  • Protein First: Prioritize protein intake with every meal to preserve muscle mass and promote satiety. Aim for 60-80 grams of protein daily.
  • Separate Fluids from Meals: Do not drink fluids 30 minutes before, during, or 30 minutes after meals to prevent diluting digestive enzymes and causing discomfort or dumping syndrome.
  • Vitamin and Mineral Supplementation: Take prescribed daily bariatric vitamins, calcium with vitamin D, and vitamin B12 supplements for life to prevent nutritional deficiencies.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Monitor for Warning Signs: Be vigilant for any signs of complications such as fever, severe pain, persistent vomiting, or changes in mental status, and know when to contact the medical team.
  • Assist with Medication Management: Help the patient adhere to their medication schedule, ensuring correct dosages and timings.
  • Support Dietary Adherence: Encourage the patient to follow the strict dietary progression, prepare appropriate meals, and remind them about fluid intake.
  • Emotional Support: Provide emotional encouragement and understanding, as the post-operative period can be challenging both physically and psychologically.
  • Emergency Contacts: Keep a list of emergency contact numbers for the surgical team, dietitian, and primary care physician readily accessible.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Long-Term Commitment: Gastric sleeve surgery is a tool, not a cure. Long-term success hinges on sustained lifestyle modifications, including diet, exercise, and behavioral changes.
  • Psychological Adjustment: Be prepared for significant psychological adjustments. Seek support from bariatric support groups or mental health professionals if needed.
  • Regular Monitoring: Lifelong follow-up with your bariatric team is crucial for monitoring nutritional status, managing potential complications, and ensuring sustained weight loss.
  • Realistic Expectations: Understand that weight loss will be rapid initially but will slow down. Focus on health improvements rather than just the number on the scale.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Patient Education Reinforcement: Nurses play a critical role in reinforcing discharge instructions, especially regarding diet progression, medication adherence, and warning signs.
  • Pain Management Assessment: Continuously assess and manage post-operative pain, educating patients on appropriate pain medication use and non-pharmacological interventions.
  • Dehydration Prevention: Emphasize the importance of small, frequent sips of fluid and educate on signs of dehydration (e.g., dry mouth, decreased urine output, dizziness).
  • Wound Assessment: Instruct patients and caregivers on proper wound care techniques and signs of infection (e.g., erythema, purulent discharge, increased warmth).
  • Nutritional Counseling: Provide practical advice on meal preparation, portion control, and the importance of protein and vitamin supplementation.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Gastric Leak: A serious complication where digestive fluids leak from the staple line, often presenting with severe abdominal pain, fever, and tachycardia. Requires immediate intervention.
  • Dumping Syndrome: Occurs when food, especially high-sugar items, moves too quickly from the stomach into the small intestine, causing symptoms like nausea, cramping, diarrhea, and dizziness.
  • Marginal Ulcer: An ulcer that forms at the connection between the gastric pouch and the small intestine (though less common in sleeve gastrectomy, still a risk).
  • Dehydration: A common post-operative risk due to reduced fluid intake capacity and potential vomiting. Symptoms include dry mucous membranes, decreased urine output, and lightheadedness.
  • Nutritional Deficiencies: Long-term risk of deficiencies in vitamins (B12, D, folate) and minerals (iron, calcium) due to reduced absorption and intake, necessitating lifelong supplementation.
