Tracheostomy

🏠 Home / Ear, nose, and throat / Tracheostomy

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Definition: A tracheostomy is a surgically created opening (stoma) in the trachea (windpipe) to establish a direct airway.
  • Primary Purpose: It bypasses upper airway obstructions, provides a secure airway for prolonged mechanical ventilation, and facilitates the removal of respiratory secretions.
  • Indications: Common reasons include severe airway obstruction (e.g., tumors, trauma, swelling), chronic respiratory failure requiring long-term ventilation, and conditions impairing the ability to clear secretions or protect the airway from aspiration.
  • Types: Can be temporary (e.g., post-surgery, acute respiratory distress) or permanent, depending on the underlying medical condition.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Pre-Tracheostomy Indications: Patients often present with severe dyspnea, stridor, signs of respiratory distress (e.g., retractions, accessory muscle use), cyanosis, inability to protect the airway, or recurrent aspiration.
  • Post-Tracheostomy Complications: Watch for signs of infection (redness, swelling, purulent discharge at the stoma site), bleeding, difficulty breathing (indicating tube obstruction or displacement), excessive coughing, or signs of aspiration.
  • Airway Obstruction: Symptoms like increased work of breathing, noisy breathing, or changes in oxygen saturation can indicate a partially or fully obstructed tracheostomy tube.
  • Granulation Tissue: Persistent coughing, bleeding, or difficulty with tube changes may suggest the presence of granulation tissue around the stoma.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Airway Patency: Ensure regular suctioning to prevent mucous plugging and maintain a clear airway. Humidification is vital to prevent secretions from becoming thick and difficult to clear.
  • Infection Control: Strict aseptic technique must be used during stoma care, dressing changes, and suctioning to prevent local and systemic infections. Hand hygiene is paramount.
  • Tube Security: Always ensure the tracheostomy tube ties or straps are secure but not overly tight, allowing for two fingers to fit underneath. Prevent accidental decannulation, especially in new tracheostomies.
  • Emergency Equipment: Keep essential emergency supplies at the bedside, including a spare tracheostomy tube (same size and one size smaller), obturator, suction equipment, and a manual resuscitation bag (Ambu bag).
  • Skin Integrity: Regularly assess the skin around the stoma for pressure areas, irritation, or breakdown, adjusting ties and dressings as needed.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Swallowing Assessment: A comprehensive swallow evaluation by a speech-language pathologist is crucial before initiating oral intake, as tracheostomy can affect swallowing mechanics and increase aspiration risk.
  • NPO Status: Patients are typically kept NPO (nil per os) immediately post-procedure until a swallow assessment confirms safety.
  • Modified Diets: If oral feeding is permitted, patients may require thickened liquids and soft, pureed, or minced foods to reduce aspiration risk. Avoid crumbly or sticky textures.
  • Hydration: Ensure adequate hydration, which may initially be provided via intravenous fluids or enteral feeding tubes until safe oral intake is established.
  • Feeding Techniques: Patients may benefit from specific feeding strategies, such as small bites, slow pace, and chin tuck maneuvers, as advised by a speech-language pathologist.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Emergency Decannulation: Caregivers must be trained on immediate steps for accidental decannulation, including how to reinsert the tube or manage the airway until professional help arrives.
  • Suctioning Protocol: Adhere strictly to the prescribed suctioning frequency and technique, including proper catheter size and depth, to prevent tracheal trauma and maintain airway clearance.
  • Stoma Care: Regular cleaning of the stoma site and changing of dressings are essential to prevent infection and skin breakdown. Observe for any signs of redness, swelling, or discharge.
  • Humidification Management: Ensure continuous humidification of inspired air, either through a heat and moisture exchanger (HME) or a humidified collar, to prevent drying of secretions and mucous plugging.
  • Communication Methods: Establish and practice alternative communication methods (e.g., writing, communication boards, speaking valves if appropriate) to reduce patient anxiety and facilitate interaction.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Patient Selection: Careful consideration of indications, contraindications, and patient prognosis is vital for appropriate tracheostomy placement.
  • Complication Management: Proactive monitoring and prompt management of potential complications such as hemorrhage, infection, pneumothorax, and tracheal stenosis are critical for patient safety.
  • Decannulation Planning: A clear decannulation protocol, involving multidisciplinary assessment of airway patency, secretion management, and respiratory drive, is essential for successful removal of the tracheostomy tube.
  • Multidisciplinary Approach: Optimal patient outcomes require collaboration among ENT surgeons, pulmonologists, critical care specialists, speech-language pathologists, respiratory therapists, and nursing staff.
  • Long-Term Care: Physicians must educate patients and caregivers on long-term tracheostomy care, potential complications, and when to seek urgent medical attention.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Assessment: Regularly assess respiratory status, oxygen saturation, stoma site integrity, presence and character of secretions, and patient comfort level.
  • Tracheostomy Care: Master the skills of sterile suctioning, inner cannula cleaning/changing, stoma dressing changes, and tracheostomy tie replacement.
  • Patient Education: Educate patients and families on the purpose of the tracheostomy, daily care routines, emergency procedures, and signs of complications.
  • Communication Strategies: Facilitate effective communication for patients with tracheostomies, especially those unable to vocalize, using whiteboards, picture boards, or speaking valves as appropriate.
  • Humidification: Understand the importance of humidification and proper use of humidifiers or heat and moisture exchangers (HMEs) to prevent airway drying and mucous plugging.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Tube Types: Familiarity with various tracheostomy tube types (e.g., cuffed, uncuffed, fenestrated, adjustable flange) and their specific indications and care requirements.
  • Complications: Knowledge of acute (e.g., hemorrhage, pneumothorax, tube displacement) and chronic (e.g., infection, granulation tissue, tracheal stenosis, tracheoesophageal fistula) complications.
  • Speaking Valves: Understanding the criteria for fitting and managing speaking valves (e.g., Passy-Muir valve) to facilitate vocalization and improve quality of life.
  • Decannulation Criteria: Clinical guidelines for assessing readiness for decannulation, including cuff deflation trials, capping trials, and endoscopic evaluation of the upper airway.
  • Emergency Protocols: Standardized protocols for managing tracheostomy emergencies, such as accidental decannulation, tube obstruction, and respiratory distress.
