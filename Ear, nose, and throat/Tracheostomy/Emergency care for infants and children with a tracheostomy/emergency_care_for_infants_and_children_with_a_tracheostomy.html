Emergency care for infants and children with a tracheostomy

🏠 Home / Ear, nose, and throat / Tracheostomy

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Life-Threatening Emergencies: Tracheostomy emergencies in infants and children, such as tube obstruction or dislodgement, are critical and require immediate, skilled intervention to prevent severe respiratory compromise or death.
  • Preparedness is Key: Families and caregivers must have a comprehensive emergency plan, including readily accessible emergency equipment (spare tubes, suction, ambu bag) and knowledge of CPR and tracheostomy tube changes.
  • Common Complications: Be vigilant for signs of mucus plugging, tube dislodgement, infection at the stoma site, or accidental decannulation, which are the most frequent causes of emergency.
  • Emergency Contact: Always have emergency medical services (EMS) contact information readily available and inform them immediately about the child's tracheostomy when calling.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Increased Work of Breathing: Rapid, shallow breathing, nasal flaring, retractions (suprasternal, intercostal, subcostal), grunting, or head bobbing.
  • Cyanosis: Bluish discoloration of the lips, tongue, or nail beds, indicating severe oxygen deprivation.
  • Audible Stridor or Wheezing: High-pitched sounds during breathing, especially if new or worsening, suggesting airway obstruction.
  • Decreased Oxygen Saturation: A sudden drop in SpO2 readings on a pulse oximeter, indicating inadequate oxygenation.
  • Absent or Diminished Breath Sounds: On auscultation, this can indicate complete airway obstruction or tube dislodgement.
  • Changes in Mental Status: Lethargy, irritability, unresponsiveness, or extreme agitation.
  • Coughing or Gagging: Persistent or forceful coughing, especially during or after feeding, may indicate aspiration or tube irritation.
  • Signs of Infection: Redness, swelling, pus, or foul odor around the tracheostomy stoma, accompanied by fever.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Regular Suctioning: Perform suctioning as needed to clear secretions and prevent mucus plugs, ensuring proper technique and sterile equipment.
  • Secure Tube Placement: Always ensure tracheostomy ties or fasteners are snug but not too tight, preventing accidental decannulation while allowing for comfort.
  • Emergency Equipment Availability: Keep a complete emergency kit with spare tracheostomy tubes (same size and one size smaller), suction catheters, a manual resuscitation bag (Ambu bag), and saline at all times.
  • Stoma Care: Maintain meticulous stoma hygiene to prevent infection, cleaning daily with prescribed solutions and changing dressings as instructed.
  • Avoid Water Submersion: Prevent water from entering the tracheostomy tube during bathing or swimming, as this can lead to aspiration and severe respiratory distress.
  • Humidification: Ensure adequate humidification of inspired air to prevent drying of secretions and crusting within the tracheostomy tube.
  • Trained Caregivers: Ensure all primary caregivers are thoroughly trained and competent in tracheostomy care, suctioning, and emergency tube changes.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Aspiration Risk: Infants and children with tracheostomies are at increased risk for aspiration due to altered airway protection; careful assessment of swallowing function is crucial.
  • Feeding Position: Always feed the child in an upright position to minimize the risk of aspiration, maintaining this position for at least 30 minutes after feeding.
  • Thickened Liquids: Depending on swallowing assessment, thickened liquids or pureed foods may be necessary to facilitate safer swallowing and reduce aspiration risk.
  • Monitor for Aspiration Signs: Observe for coughing, choking, gagging, changes in breathing, or cyanosis during or after feeding, which indicate aspiration.
  • Gastrostomy Tube Feeding: For children with significant aspiration risk or inability to meet nutritional needs orally, a gastrostomy tube (G-tube) may be indicated for safe and adequate nutrition.
  • Oral Hygiene: Maintain excellent oral hygiene even if the child is not feeding orally, to prevent oral infections.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Stay Calm and Act Quickly: In an emergency, maintain composure. Your calm demeanor helps the child and allows for effective action.
  • Call for Help Immediately: If the child is in distress, call 911 or your local emergency number and clearly state that the child has a tracheostomy.
  • Follow Emergency Protocol: If trained, immediately initiate your child's specific emergency protocol, which typically involves suctioning, attempting to re-insert the tube, or performing a tube change.
  • Perform CPR if Necessary: If the child becomes unresponsive and is not breathing, begin CPR immediately while awaiting emergency medical services.
  • Never Leave Unattended: A child with a tracheostomy should never be left unsupervised, especially during sleep or playtime, due to the constant risk of airway compromise.
  • Communicate Clearly: Provide clear and concise information to emergency responders about the child's condition, tracheostomy type, and recent events.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Comprehensive Caregiver Training: It is paramount that all primary caregivers receive thorough, hands-on training from a multidisciplinary team on routine tracheostomy care and emergency management.
  • Personalized Emergency Plan: Every child with a tracheostomy requires a detailed, written emergency plan developed in collaboration with their medical team, including specific steps for various scenarios.
  • Regular Clinical Follow-ups: Consistent follow-up appointments with ENT, pulmonology, and other specialists are essential to monitor tube integrity, stoma health, and overall respiratory status.
  • Early Recognition of Distress: Physicians emphasize the importance of educating caregivers to recognize subtle signs of respiratory distress or impending complications, not just overt emergencies.
  • Proactive Management of Secretions: Aggressive and appropriate management of airway secretions is crucial to prevent mucus plugging, a common cause of acute obstruction.
  • Psychosocial Support: Acknowledge the significant burden on families and ensure access to psychosocial support services to aid in coping and long-term care.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Anatomy and Physiology Review: Understand the altered airway anatomy in a child with a tracheostomy and the physiological implications for breathing, speech, and swallowing.
  • Tracheostomy Tube Types: Familiarize yourself with different types of tracheostomy tubes (cuffed vs. uncuffed, fenestrated vs. unfenestrated, single vs. double lumen) and their indications.
  • Sterile Suctioning Technique: Master the sterile technique for tracheostomy suctioning, including appropriate catheter size, depth, and duration to prevent trauma and infection.
  • Stoma Site Assessment: Learn to meticulously assess the tracheostomy stoma for signs of infection, skin breakdown, granulation tissue, or tube migration.
  • Emergency Decannulation Protocol: Understand and practice the emergency decannulation protocol, including immediate reinsertion techniques and alternative airway management.
  • Family Education and Empowerment: Recognize the critical role of nurses in educating and empowering families to confidently manage tracheostomy care at home and during emergencies.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Indications for Tracheostomy: Review common indications in pediatric patients, such as congenital airway anomalies, subglottic stenosis, prolonged mechanical ventilation, or severe obstructive sleep apnea.
  • Tracheostomy Tube Sizing: Understand the principles of appropriate tracheostomy tube sizing based on age, weight, and tracheal diameter to ensure optimal ventilation and minimize complications.
  • Complications of Tracheostomy: Be aware of potential long-term complications including granulation tissue formation, tracheomalacia, tracheo-innominate fistula, and stomal stenosis.
  • Ventilator Management: For ventilator-dependent children, understand basic ventilator settings, alarm management, and troubleshooting in the context of a tracheostomy.
  • Capnography Monitoring: Utilize capnography for continuous monitoring of end-tidal CO2 in critically ill children with tracheostomies to assess ventilation effectiveness.
  • Bronchoscopy for Airway Assessment: Recognize the role of flexible or rigid bronchoscopy in assessing the airway above and below the tracheostomy, especially in cases of persistent symptoms or complications.
