Tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy in children – Discharge instructions

🏠 Home / Ear, nose, and throat / Tonsils and adenoids

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Understanding Recovery: Your child will experience discomfort and fatigue for several days following the surgery. This is a normal part of the healing process.
  • Pain Management is Key: Consistent administration of prescribed pain medication is crucial for your child's comfort and to encourage adequate fluid intake.
  • Hydration Priority: Preventing dehydration is the most critical aspect of post-operative care. Offer fluids frequently, even if in small amounts.
  • Bleeding Vigilance: Be alert for any signs of bleeding, especially within the first 24 hours and again around 7-10 days post-surgery when scabs may begin to detach.
  • Follow-up Appointments: Ensure you attend all scheduled post-operative appointments with the surgeon to monitor healing and address any concerns.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Sore Throat: Expected and will be managed with prescribed pain relief. The severity will gradually decrease over 7-10 days.
  • Ear Pain (Referred Otalgia): Common due to shared nerve pathways; it does not necessarily indicate an ear infection.
  • Low-Grade Fever: A temperature up to 101°F (38.3°C) is common for the first few days post-surgery.
  • Bad Breath: Normal for several days due to the healing tissue and scabs in the throat.
  • Nausea and Vomiting: May occur shortly after surgery due to anesthesia or swallowed blood; usually resolves quickly.
  • Snoring or Nasal Congestion: Common temporarily due to swelling in the throat and nasal passages, especially after adenoidectomy.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Activity Restrictions: Avoid strenuous activity, rough play, and contact sports for at least 10-14 days to prevent bleeding and promote healing.
  • Pain Medication Schedule: Administer pain medication as prescribed, even if the child appears comfortable, to maintain consistent pain control.
  • Environmental Irritants: Keep your child away from smoke, dust, and other airborne irritants that can irritate the healing throat.
  • Medication Avoidance: Do NOT give aspirin or NSAIDs (e.g., ibuprofen, naproxen) unless specifically instructed by the surgeon, as they can increase the risk of bleeding. Acetaminophen is generally safe.
  • Hydration Monitoring: Continuously offer small, frequent sips of clear fluids to prevent dehydration.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Soft, Bland Foods: Begin with clear liquids, then gradually introduce soft, bland foods such as popsicles, ice cream, yogurt, mashed potatoes, scrambled eggs, and well-cooked pasta.
  • Avoid Irritating Foods: Steer clear of acidic foods (citrus juices, tomatoes), spicy foods, and crunchy or sharp items (chips, toast, crackers) that can irritate the throat or dislodge scabs.
  • Frequent Small Meals: Encourage small, frequent intake of fluids and soft foods rather than large meals to ease swallowing and maintain nutrition.
  • Prioritize Hydration: Emphasize water, clear broths, diluted juices, and electrolyte solutions to ensure adequate fluid intake.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Bright Red Bleeding: Any bright red blood from the mouth or nose, especially if more than a few streaks, requires immediate medical attention. Contact your surgeon or go to the nearest emergency room.
  • Difficulty Breathing: Labored breathing, gasping, stridor (noisy breathing), or blue lips are medical emergencies. Seek immediate help.
  • Signs of Severe Dehydration: Decreased urination (fewer wet diapers for infants/toddlers), dry mouth, absence of tears, or significant lethargy warrant urgent medical evaluation.
  • High or Unresponsive Fever: A fever above 102°F (38.9°C) that does not respond to acetaminophen or is accompanied by other concerning symptoms.
  • Uncontrolled Pain: Pain that is not relieved by prescribed medication or is worsening significantly.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Adherence to Instructions: Meticulously following all discharge instructions is paramount for a smooth and uncomplicated recovery.
  • Close Monitoring: Parents should closely observe their child for any concerning symptoms and trust their parental instincts.
  • Open Communication: Do not hesitate to contact your healthcare provider if you have any questions or concerns, no matter how minor they may seem.
  • Gradual Recovery: Full recovery typically takes 10-14 days, with gradual improvement in pain and energy levels.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Comprehensive Patient Education: Reinforce understanding of pain management strategies, hydration goals, and critical warning signs with parents/guardians.
  • Discharge Checklist Verification: Ensure all necessary prescriptions, follow-up appointment details, and emergency contact numbers are clearly provided and understood.
  • Pain Assessment Education: Instruct parents on using age-appropriate pain scales (e.g., FLACC, Wong-Baker FACES) to accurately assess and report their child's pain level.
  • Fluid Intake Monitoring Guidance: Advise parents on practical methods to monitor for adequate hydration, such as tracking wet diapers/urine frequency and observing mucous membranes.
  • Post-Operative Bleeding Differentiation: Educate on distinguishing minor blood-tinged saliva from active, bright red bleeding requiring immediate intervention.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Post-Operative Hemorrhage: The most serious complication, occurring in 1-5% of cases, with peak incidence within 24 hours and again around 7-10 days post-op due to scab separation.
  • Fibrin Scabs: The white patches observed in the tonsillar fossae are normal healing tissue (fibrin) and not indicative of infection; they will gradually slough off.
  • Referred Otalgia: Ear pain is a common phenomenon due to the shared innervation of the pharynx and ear via the glossopharyngeal nerve (CN IX).
  • Dehydration Risk: Children are particularly vulnerable to dehydration post-tonsillectomy due to pain-related decreased oral intake.
  • Adenoidectomy Specifics: Temporary nasal congestion, hyponasal speech, and occasional ear fullness are common after adenoid removal due to swelling and changes in airflow.
