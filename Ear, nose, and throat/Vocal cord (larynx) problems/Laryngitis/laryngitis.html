Laryngitis

🏠 Home / Ear, nose, and throat / Vocal cord (larynx) problems

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Definition: Laryngitis is the inflammation of the voice box (larynx), leading to hoarseness or complete loss of voice.
  • Common Causes: Most frequently caused by viral infections (e.g., common cold, flu), but can also result from bacterial infections, vocal strain, irritants (smoke, allergens), or gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD).
  • Acute vs. Chronic: Acute laryngitis is typically short-lived (less than 3 weeks) and often viral. Chronic laryngitis persists longer and is usually due to ongoing irritation or underlying conditions.
  • Vocal Cord Impact: Inflammation causes the vocal cords to swell, altering the sound produced as air passes over them.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Hoarseness: The primary symptom, ranging from a rough voice to complete aphonia (loss of voice).
  • Sore Throat: A scratchy or raw sensation in the throat.
  • Dry Cough: Often a persistent, non-productive cough.
  • Tickling Sensation: An irritating feeling in the back of the throat or larynx.
  • Difficulty Swallowing: Less common, but can occur if inflammation is severe.
  • Fever: May accompany viral or bacterial infections causing laryngitis.
  • Swollen Lymph Nodes: Palpable tenderness in the neck, especially with infectious causes.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Strict Vocal Rest: Minimize talking, avoid whispering (which can strain vocal cords more than normal speech), and refrain from shouting to allow vocal cords to heal.
  • Adequate Hydration: Drink plenty of fluids, especially water and warm liquids, to keep vocal cords moist and soothe the throat.
  • Avoid Irritants: Steer clear of cigarette smoke (both active and passive), excessive alcohol, and environmental pollutants that can further irritate the larynx.
  • Humidify Air: Use a humidifier in your home or workplace, especially during dry seasons, to prevent vocal cord dryness.
  • Manage GERD: If acid reflux is a contributing factor, adhere to prescribed medications and dietary modifications to reduce acid exposure to the larynx.
  • Monitor Symptoms: Seek medical attention if hoarseness persists beyond two weeks, if you experience severe pain, difficulty breathing, stridor (a high-pitched breathing sound), or a high fever.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Warm Liquids: Consume soothing warm beverages like herbal teas (non-caffeinated), warm water with honey, or clear broths to alleviate throat discomfort and maintain hydration.
  • Avoid Dehydrating Beverages: Limit or avoid caffeinated drinks and alcohol, as they can contribute to dehydration and further dry out the vocal cords.
  • Soft, Non-Irritating Foods: Opt for soft, easy-to-swallow foods if your throat is sore. Avoid crunchy, sharp, or very hot foods that might cause additional irritation.
  • GERD-Friendly Diet: If reflux is implicated, restrict acidic foods (citrus, tomatoes), spicy foods, fatty foods, and chocolate, especially before bedtime.
  • Stay Hydrated: Prioritize plain water intake throughout the day to ensure optimal vocal cord lubrication.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • No Whispering: Instruct patients that whispering can cause more strain on the vocal cords than speaking softly, and should be avoided during vocal rest.
  • Smoking Cessation: Strongly advise patients who smoke to cease, as smoking is a major irritant and can lead to chronic laryngitis and more serious laryngeal conditions.
  • Environmental Control: Educate on minimizing exposure to dust, chemical fumes, and allergens, which can exacerbate laryngeal inflammation.
  • Medication Adherence: Ensure patients understand the correct use of prescribed medications, such as antibiotics for bacterial infections, antacids for GERD, or short courses of corticosteroids for severe inflammation.
  • Voice Hygiene Practices: Counsel on proper voice use techniques once symptoms subside to prevent recurrence, especially for individuals who use their voice professionally.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Clinical Diagnosis: Laryngitis is primarily diagnosed based on patient history and a physical examination, often including a visual inspection of the throat.
  • Laryngoscopy Consideration: For persistent hoarseness (over 2-3 weeks), a direct or indirect laryngoscopy may be performed to visualize the vocal cords and rule out other pathologies like polyps, nodules, or malignancy.
  • Treatment Strategy: Management focuses on symptomatic relief for viral cases (vocal rest, hydration). Specific treatments are directed at underlying causes, such as antibiotics for bacterial infections or proton pump inhibitors for GERD.
  • Referral Indications: Referral to an otolaryngologist (ENT specialist) is warranted for chronic laryngitis, suspicion of vocal cord lesions, or any red flag symptoms like stridor or unexplained weight loss.
  • Differential Diagnosis: It is crucial to differentiate laryngitis from other causes of hoarseness, including vocal cord paralysis, benign lesions, or laryngeal cancer.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Patient Education Priority: Nurses play a vital role in educating patients on the importance of vocal rest, adequate hydration, and avoidance of laryngeal irritants.
  • Symptom Assessment: Regularly assess for worsening symptoms such as increased difficulty breathing, stridor, or signs of systemic infection (e.g., high fever, chills).
  • Medication Teaching: Provide clear instructions on the administration and purpose of prescribed medications, including antibiotics, antacids, or analgesics.
  • Humidifier Use: Instruct patients on the proper and safe use of humidifiers, emphasizing regular cleaning to prevent bacterial growth.
  • Post-Laryngoscopy Care: If a laryngoscopy is performed, monitor for post-procedure complications such as throat discomfort or minor bleeding, and provide appropriate comfort measures.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Etiology: Predominantly viral (Rhinovirus, Influenza, Parainfluenza, Adenovirus), bacterial (Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae), fungal (Candida), chemical irritants, vocal abuse, GERD.
  • Pathophysiology: Inflammation of the laryngeal mucosa leads to edema of the vocal folds, impairing their vibratory function and resulting in dysphonia.
  • Diagnostic Modalities: Clinical history, physical examination, indirect laryngoscopy (mirror or flexible fiberoptic), videostroboscopy for detailed vocal fold analysis, pH monitoring for suspected GERD.
  • Pharmacology: NSAIDs for pain/inflammation, corticosteroids (short course for severe cases), antibiotics (only for confirmed bacterial infection), proton pump inhibitors/H2 blockers for reflux laryngitis.
  • Complications: Chronic dysphonia, vocal fold nodules/polyps, granulomas, rarely acute airway obstruction in severe pediatric cases (croup).
