Inducible laryngeal obstruction (ILO)

🏠 Home / Ear, nose, and throat / Vocal cord (larynx) problems

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Understanding ILO: Inducible laryngeal obstruction (ILO), also known as paradoxical vocal fold movement (PVFM) or vocal cord dysfunction (VCD), is a condition where the vocal cords inappropriately close or adduct during inspiration, leading to difficulty breathing.
  • Key Characteristic: Unlike asthma, which affects the lower airways, ILO primarily involves the upper airway (larynx) and often presents with inspiratory stridor.
  • Episodic Nature: Episodes are typically sudden in onset and resolution, triggered by various factors including exercise, irritants, reflux, or emotional stress.
  • Misdiagnosis Risk: ILO is frequently misdiagnosed as asthma due to overlapping symptoms, leading to ineffective treatment if not correctly identified.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Inspiratory Stridor: A high-pitched, harsh sound heard predominantly during inhalation, indicating upper airway obstruction.
  • Dyspnea: Sudden onset of difficulty breathing or shortness of breath, often described as a feeling of choking or suffocation.
  • Throat Tightness: A sensation of constriction or a lump in the throat, distinct from chest tightness.
  • Cough: A persistent or paroxysmal cough may accompany or precede an ILO episode.
  • Hoarseness: While less common, some individuals may experience voice changes during or after an episode.
  • Anxiety/Panic: The acute sensation of not being able to breathe can induce significant anxiety or panic during an episode.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Trigger Identification: Meticulously identify and avoid personal triggers such as specific odors, exercise intensities, or emotional stressors.
  • Breathing Techniques: Learn and practice specific breathing exercises (e.g., pursed-lip breathing, diaphragmatic breathing, sniff-exhale technique) taught by a speech-language pathologist to regain laryngeal control during an episode.
  • Emergency Plan: Develop a clear action plan with your healthcare provider for managing acute episodes, including when to seek emergency care.
  • GERD Management: If gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) is a trigger, adhere strictly to prescribed medications and lifestyle modifications to minimize reflux events.
  • Avoid Irritants: Minimize exposure to environmental irritants like smoke, strong perfumes, cleaning agents, and cold air, which can provoke laryngeal spasms.
  • Stress Reduction: Implement stress management techniques, as emotional stress and anxiety can exacerbate or trigger ILO episodes.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Reflux-Friendly Diet: If GERD is a contributing factor, avoid foods known to trigger reflux, such as acidic foods (citrus, tomatoes), spicy foods, chocolate, peppermint, and high-fat meals.
  • Caffeine and Alcohol: Limit or avoid caffeine and alcohol, as they can relax the lower esophageal sphincter, potentially worsening reflux.
  • Smaller, Frequent Meals: Opt for smaller, more frequent meals rather than large ones to reduce pressure on the stomach and minimize reflux.
  • Avoid Late-Night Eating: Refrain from eating for at least 2-3 hours before bedtime to allow for proper digestion and reduce nocturnal reflux.
  • Hydration: Maintain adequate hydration with non-irritating fluids like water, which can help soothe the throat and thin mucus.
  • Identify Food Triggers: Keep a food diary to identify any specific foods that consistently precede or worsen ILO symptoms.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Remain Calm: During an ILO episode, the most crucial step is to remain calm and reassure the individual, as panic can worsen symptoms.
  • Guide Breathing: Gently guide the person to use their practiced breathing techniques, such as pursed-lip breathing or sniffing in and blowing out slowly.
  • Positioning: Encourage the person to sit upright and lean forward slightly, which can sometimes ease breathing.
  • Avoid Forceful Interventions: Do not attempt to force air into the person or administer asthma medications unless specifically prescribed for co-existing asthma.
  • Monitor for Worsening: Watch for signs of severe distress, cyanosis, or unresponsiveness to breathing techniques, which warrant immediate emergency medical attention.
  • Know the Action Plan: Be familiar with the individual's personalized ILO action plan, including contact information for their healthcare provider.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Accurate Diagnosis: A definitive diagnosis of ILO requires direct visualization of paradoxical vocal cord adduction during a symptomatic episode, often via flexible laryngoscopy.
  • Differential Diagnosis: It is critical to differentiate ILO from other conditions causing dyspnea and stridor, such as asthma, anaphylaxis, foreign body aspiration, or angioedema.
  • Multidisciplinary Approach: Effective management often involves a team approach, including an otolaryngologist, pulmonologist, speech-language pathologist, and sometimes a psychologist or gastroenterologist.
  • Speech Therapy: Laryngeal control therapy, delivered by a speech-language pathologist, is the cornerstone of treatment, focusing on breathing retraining and laryngeal relaxation techniques.
  • Pharmacological Management: While there is no specific medication for ILO, co-existing conditions like GERD, anxiety, or asthma should be managed pharmacologically as appropriate.
  • Patient Education: Thorough patient education on the nature of ILO, triggers, and self-management strategies is paramount for long-term control.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Pathophysiology: Understand that ILO involves the inappropriate, involuntary adduction of the true vocal folds during inspiration, leading to airflow obstruction at the glottic level.
  • Assessment: Focus on a detailed history of breathing difficulty, including onset, triggers, associated symptoms, and response to previous treatments. Observe for inspiratory stridor and paradoxical chest/abdominal movement.
  • Nursing Interventions: Educate patients on trigger avoidance, demonstrate and reinforce breathing techniques, and provide emotional support during acute episodes.
  • Emergency Preparedness: Be prepared to assist with flexible laryngoscopy for diagnosis and to manage acute respiratory distress, differentiating ILO from other causes.
  • Patient Advocacy: Advocate for patients to receive appropriate referrals to speech-language pathology and other specialists for comprehensive management.
  • Documentation: Accurately document episode characteristics, triggers, interventions, and patient response to guide ongoing care.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Synonyms: Paradoxical Vocal Fold Movement (PVFM), Vocal Cord Dysfunction (VCD), Functional Laryngeal Obstruction.
  • Diagnostic Gold Standard: Flexible fiberoptic laryngoscopy during a symptomatic episode, demonstrating vocal cord adduction on inspiration.
  • Key Differential Diagnoses: Asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), laryngeal edema, foreign body aspiration, anaphylaxis, vocal cord paralysis.
  • Primary Treatment Modality: Speech-language pathology (SLP) for laryngeal control therapy, including breathing retraining and biofeedback.
  • Associated Conditions: Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), anxiety disorders, asthma, upper airway hypersensitivity.
  • ICD-10 Codes: J38.5 (Diseases of vocal cords, unspecified), R06.02 (Shortness of breath, unspecified), R06.1 (Stridor).
