Parotitis

🏠 Home / Ear, nose, and throat / Head and neck infections

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Definition: Parotitis is the inflammation of one or both parotid glands, the largest salivary glands located in front of and below the ears.
  • Common Causes: Can be viral (e.g., mumps, influenza, HIV), bacterial (e.g., Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus pyogenes), obstructive (e.g., sialolithiasis or salivary stones), or autoimmune (e.g., Sjögren's syndrome).
  • Risk Factors: Dehydration, poor oral hygiene, recent surgery, immunosuppression, and certain medications can increase susceptibility.
  • Complications: Potential for abscess formation, spread of infection, facial nerve palsy (rare), and systemic complications like orchitis or pancreatitis in mumps.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Glandular Swelling: Painful enlargement of the parotid gland(s), typically anterior to the earlobe and extending to the angle of the jaw.
  • Pain: Tenderness and pain, often exacerbated by chewing, swallowing, or opening the mouth.
  • Fever: Common, especially in acute viral or bacterial infections.
  • Malaise: General feeling of discomfort, fatigue, and body aches.
  • Headache: Frequently accompanies viral parotitis, such as mumps.
  • Dysphagia/Odynophagia: Difficulty or pain with swallowing due to swelling and discomfort.
  • Trismus: Limited ability to open the mouth fully.
  • Erythema: Redness of the skin overlying the affected gland.
  • Pus Drainage: In bacterial parotitis, purulent discharge may be observed from Stensen's duct (the opening of the parotid gland into the mouth).

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Vaccination Status: Ensure up-to-date MMR (Measles, Mumps, Rubella) vaccination to prevent mumps parotitis.
  • Oral Hygiene: Maintain meticulous oral hygiene, including regular brushing and flossing, to reduce bacterial load and prevent ascending infections.
  • Adequate Hydration: Ensure sufficient fluid intake to prevent salivary stasis, particularly in elderly, debilitated, or post-operative patients.
  • Avoid Dehydration: Monitor fluid balance closely, especially in patients with NPO status or systemic illness, as dehydration is a significant risk factor.
  • Early Medical Consultation: Seek prompt medical attention for any new or worsening glandular swelling, pain, or fever to prevent complications.
  • Infection Control: For suspected viral parotitis (e.g., mumps), implement appropriate droplet precautions to prevent transmission to others.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Soft Diet: Consume soft, easily chewable foods to minimize pain and discomfort during mastication.
  • Hydration: Encourage frequent sips of water, clear broths, or electrolyte solutions to maintain hydration and promote salivary flow.
  • Avoid Acidic Foods: Limit intake of citrus fruits, vinegars, and other highly acidic foods that can stimulate salivary glands and exacerbate pain.
  • Avoid Dry/Hard Foods: Steer clear of foods that require extensive chewing or are dry and crumbly, as they can worsen discomfort and potentially irritate the gland.
  • Lukewarm Liquids: Opt for lukewarm beverages rather than very hot or very cold, which might irritate the inflamed gland.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Monitor for Abscess Formation: Closely observe for signs of increasing localized pain, redness, warmth, fluctuance, or persistent high fever, which may indicate an abscess requiring drainage.
  • Systemic Complications: Be vigilant for symptoms suggesting systemic spread or complications, such as testicular pain (orchitis), abdominal pain (pancreatitis), or neurological changes (meningitis/encephalitis) in cases of mumps.
  • Facial Nerve Palsy: Promptly report any signs of facial weakness or paralysis, as this can be a rare but serious complication due to the facial nerve's proximity to the parotid gland.
  • Dehydration Risk: Continuously assess hydration status, especially in patients whose pain limits oral intake, and intervene with intravenous fluids if necessary.
  • Medication Adherence: Ensure strict adherence to prescribed antibiotics for bacterial parotitis and pain management regimens to optimize recovery and prevent recurrence.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Differential Diagnosis: Always consider a broad differential including viral, bacterial, obstructive, autoimmune, and neoplastic etiologies when evaluating parotid swelling.
  • Diagnostic Approach: Utilize clinical examination, salivary gland ultrasound, CT scan, or MRI as indicated to confirm diagnosis, identify complications like abscesses, or rule out obstruction/tumor. Serological tests for mumps or HIV may be necessary.
  • Tailored Treatment: Treatment must be specific to the underlying cause: antibiotics for bacterial infections, supportive care for viral cases, sialogogues and massage for obstructive causes, and immunosuppressants for autoimmune conditions.
  • Surgical Consultation: Refer to an ENT specialist for cases involving abscess formation, recurrent parotitis, suspected sialolithiasis requiring removal, or concerns for malignancy.
  • Patient Education: Emphasize the importance of hydration, oral hygiene, and recognizing warning signs of complications to all patients.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Comprehensive Assessment: Conduct a thorough head and neck assessment, including palpation of the parotid glands, inspection of Stensen's duct for discharge, and evaluation of facial nerve function.
  • Pain Management: Administer prescribed analgesics and implement non-pharmacological interventions such as warm compresses to the affected area to alleviate discomfort.
  • Hydration Monitoring: Closely monitor fluid intake and output, assess skin turgor and mucous membranes, and actively encourage oral fluid consumption.
  • Infection Control: Implement appropriate isolation precautions (e.g., droplet precautions for mumps) based on the suspected or confirmed etiology to prevent disease transmission.
  • Patient Education: Educate patients and caregivers on medication administration, symptom management, dietary modifications, and when to seek urgent medical attention.
  • Oral Care Assistance: Provide assistance with regular oral hygiene, especially for patients experiencing significant pain or trismus that limits self-care.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Etiological Agents: Mumps virus (Paramyxoviridae), Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus species, HIV, Coxsackievirus, Epstein-Barr virus.
  • Anatomical Considerations: Parotid gland, Stensen's duct, Facial nerve (CN VII), Masseter muscle.
  • Pathophysiology: Inflammation leading to glandular edema, ductal obstruction, and potential secondary bacterial infection or viral replication within glandular tissue.
  • Diagnostic Modalities: Clinical examination, Salivary gland ultrasound, CT scan with contrast, MRI, Sialography, Mumps serology (IgM/IgG), Culture of ductal exudate.
  • Pharmacological Interventions: Antibiotics (e.g., clindamycin, amoxicillin-clavulanate), Analgesics (NSAIDs, acetaminophen), Antipyretics, Sialogogues (e.g., pilocarpine for Sjögren's).
  • Potential Complications: Parotid abscess, Facial nerve palsy, Mumps orchitis, Mumps pancreatitis, Mumps meningitis/encephalitis, Chronic recurrent parotitis.
