Inducible laryngeal obstruction (ILO)

🏠 Home / Ear, nose, and throat / Paradoxical vocal fold motion

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Definition: Inducible laryngeal obstruction (ILO), formerly known as paradoxical vocal fold motion (PVFM), is a condition characterized by inappropriate adduction (closing) of the vocal folds during inspiration, leading to partial or complete airway obstruction.
  • Misdiagnosis Risk: Often mistaken for asthma due to similar symptoms like shortness of breath and wheezing, but ILO does not respond to typical bronchodilator therapy.
  • Triggers: Common triggers include intense exercise, exposure to irritants (e.g., smoke, strong odors, fumes), emotional stress, gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), and post-nasal drip.
  • Clinical Presentation: Patients typically experience sudden onset of inspiratory dyspnea, stridor, throat tightness, and a sensation of choking, often resolving spontaneously or with specific breathing techniques.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Inspiratory Dyspnea: Sudden and severe difficulty breathing in, often described as feeling unable to get enough air.
  • Inspiratory Stridor: A high-pitched, harsh sound heard primarily during inhalation, indicating upper airway obstruction.
  • Throat Tightness: A constricting sensation in the throat, often accompanied by a feeling of choking or suffocation.
  • Cough: A persistent, often dry or hacking cough that may accompany or follow an obstructive episode.
  • Hoarseness or Voice Changes: While less common, some individuals may experience transient changes in voice quality during or immediately after an episode.
  • Wheezing: Can be present, but typically inspiratory and unresponsive to asthma medications, differentiating it from asthmatic wheezing.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Trigger Identification and Avoidance: Meticulously identify and minimize exposure to personal triggers such as specific irritants, intense exercise patterns, or stressful situations.
  • Emergency Action Plan: Develop and communicate a clear action plan for acute episodes, including specific breathing techniques and when to seek immediate medical attention.
  • Breathing Retraining: Practice diaphragmatic breathing and laryngeal relaxation techniques taught by a speech-language pathologist to regain control during an episode.
  • GERD and LPR Management: Aggressively manage underlying conditions like gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) or laryngopharyngeal reflux (LPR) through dietary modifications and prescribed medications.
  • Avoid Panic: Educate patients and caregivers that panic can exacerbate symptoms; maintaining calm and focusing on controlled breathing is paramount during an episode.
  • Medication Review: Regularly review all medications with a physician to ensure no drugs are contributing to reflux or laryngeal irritation.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Reflux-Triggering Foods: Restrict or avoid foods and beverages known to exacerbate GERD or LPR, such as acidic foods (citrus, tomatoes), spicy foods, caffeine, chocolate, peppermint, and fatty meals.
  • Meal Timing: Avoid large meals close to bedtime (within 2-3 hours) to reduce the risk of nocturnal reflux.
  • Hydration: Maintain adequate hydration by drinking plenty of water throughout the day to keep vocal folds moist and reduce irritation.
  • Alcohol Consumption: Limit or avoid alcohol, as it can relax the lower esophageal sphincter, promoting reflux, and may also irritate the laryngeal mucosa.
  • Portion Control: Opt for smaller, more frequent meals rather than large, heavy ones to minimize gastric distension and reflux potential.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Recognize Acute Episodes: Be vigilant for sudden onset of inspiratory stridor, severe shortness of breath, and throat tightness, especially during or after known triggers.
  • Maintain Calm Demeanor: Reassure the patient and encourage them to remain calm, as anxiety can worsen laryngeal spasm.
  • Guide Breathing Techniques: Prompt the patient to initiate learned breathing exercises, such as diaphragmatic breathing or pursed-lip breathing, focusing on slow, controlled exhalation.
  • Monitor for Worsening: Continuously assess respiratory effort, skin color, and level of consciousness. If symptoms do not improve rapidly or worsen, prepare for emergency medical intervention.
  • Avoid Unnecessary Interventions: Do not administer bronchodilators or steroids unless specifically directed by a physician for a co-existing condition, as they are ineffective for ILO and may delay appropriate management.
  • Educate Others: Ensure family members, coaches, or close contacts are aware of the condition and the appropriate response during an episode.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Accurate Diagnosis: Emphasize the critical need for direct visualization of vocal fold adduction during a symptomatic episode, typically via flexible fiberoptic laryngoscopy, to differentiate ILO from asthma or other respiratory conditions.
  • Multidisciplinary Management: Advocate for a collaborative approach involving otolaryngologists, pulmonologists, speech-language pathologists, and potentially psychologists or psychiatrists, given the frequent association with anxiety or stress.
  • Laryngeal Control Therapy: Prescribe and refer patients for laryngeal control therapy with a speech-language pathologist, focusing on breathing retraining, laryngeal relaxation, and desensitization techniques.
  • Trigger Identification: Guide patients in identifying and managing their specific triggers, which may include environmental irritants, exercise, or emotional stressors.
  • Co-morbidity Treatment: Address and treat co-existing conditions such as GERD, laryngopharyngeal reflux (LPR), or anxiety disorders, as these can significantly contribute to ILO symptoms.
  • Patient Education: Provide comprehensive education to patients and their families regarding the nature of ILO, its benign prognosis with proper management, and the importance of adherence to therapeutic strategies.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Patient Assessment: Focus on distinguishing inspiratory stridor and dyspnea from expiratory wheezing, noting the lack of response to bronchodilators in ILO episodes.
  • Breathing Technique Reinforcement: Actively teach and reinforce diaphragmatic breathing and other laryngeal relaxation techniques to patients during non-acute periods and guide them during episodes.
  • Trigger Education: Educate patients on identifying and avoiding personal triggers, including environmental factors, dietary irritants, and stress management strategies.
  • Psychological Support: Recognize and address the significant anxiety and panic that can accompany ILO episodes, providing reassurance and guidance on coping mechanisms.
  • Documentation: Accurately document the characteristics of ILO episodes, including triggers, duration, severity, patient response to interventions, and any associated psychological factors.
  • Referral Pathways: Understand the importance of referring patients to speech-language pathology for laryngeal control therapy and to gastroenterology or mental health services as indicated.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Pathophysiology: Characterized by paradoxical adduction of the true vocal folds, and sometimes the false vocal folds, during inspiration, leading to dynamic upper airway obstruction.
  • Diagnostic Gold Standard: Continuous laryngoscopy during exercise (CLE) or flexible fiberoptic laryngoscopy during a symptomatic episode to directly visualize vocal fold movement.
  • Differential Diagnoses: Key differentiators include asthma, vocal cord paralysis, subglottic stenosis, angioedema, and foreign body aspiration.
  • Associated Conditions: Frequently co-occurs with gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), laryngopharyngeal reflux (LPR), anxiety disorders, panic attacks, and asthma.
  • Management Modalities: Primary treatment involves laryngeal control therapy (LCT) delivered by a speech-language pathologist, biofeedback, psychological intervention, and management of underlying co-morbidities.
  • Pharmacological Role: No specific pharmacological treatment for ILO itself; medications are used to manage associated conditions like GERD (e.g., PPIs) or anxiety (e.g., anxiolytics).
