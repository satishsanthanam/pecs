Peritonsillar abscess

🏠 Home / Sleep / Tonsils and adenoids

💡 What You Need to Know

  • Definition: A peritonsillar abscess (PTA), also known as quinsy, is a collection of pus that forms behind one of the palatine tonsils.
  • Etiology: It typically develops as a complication of acute tonsillitis when infection spreads from the tonsil into the surrounding peritonsillar space.
  • Urgency: This condition requires prompt medical attention due to the risk of airway obstruction and spread of infection to deeper neck spaces.
  • Symptoms Onset: Symptoms often worsen rapidly over a few days, leading to severe discomfort and functional impairment.

🤒 Associated Symptoms

  • Severe Unilateral Sore Throat: Intense pain, usually on one side of the throat, often radiating to the ear.
  • Dysphagia & Odynophagia: Extreme difficulty and pain with swallowing, leading to drooling.
  • Trismus: Spasm of the jaw muscles, making it difficult or impossible to open the mouth fully.
  • "Hot Potato" Voice: A muffled, thick, or gargled voice quality due to swelling in the throat.
  • Fever & Chills: Systemic signs of infection, often accompanied by malaise.
  • Uvular Deviation: The uvula (fleshy projection at the back of the soft palate) is pushed away from the affected side.
  • Neck Pain & Lymphadenopathy: Pain and tenderness in the neck, with swollen and tender lymph nodes.

🛡 Crucial Precautions

  • Immediate Medical Consultation: Seek urgent medical care if you suspect a peritonsillar abscess, especially with worsening throat pain, difficulty swallowing, or breathing issues.
  • Airway Monitoring: Be vigilant for signs of airway compromise, such as stridor (a high-pitched breathing sound), severe shortness of breath, or cyanosis.
  • Avoid Self-Treatment: Do not attempt to self-treat with unprescribed antibiotics or home remedies, as this can delay appropriate medical intervention.
  • Complete Antibiotic Course: If antibiotics are prescribed, ensure the full course is completed, even if symptoms improve, to prevent recurrence and resistance.
  • Hydration: Maintain adequate fluid intake, even if painful, to prevent dehydration; intravenous fluids may be necessary in severe cases.

🍽 Dietary Directions & Restrictions

  • Hydration Focus: Prioritize frequent sips of cool water, clear broths, or electrolyte solutions to prevent dehydration, especially with fever and difficulty swallowing.
  • Soft & Bland Foods: Consume only soft, non-acidic, and easy-to-swallow foods such as pureed soups, yogurt, mashed potatoes, applesauce, and smoothies.
  • Avoid Irritants: Strictly avoid hot, spicy, acidic (e.g., citrus juices), crunchy, or rough-textured foods that can further irritate the inflamed throat.
  • Post-Drainage Progression: After incision and drainage, gradually reintroduce a normal diet as tolerated, starting with clear liquids and progressing to soft, then solid foods.
  • Small, Frequent Meals: Opt for smaller, more frequent meals to minimize discomfort during swallowing.

⚠️ Attendant Guidelines

  • Airway Assessment: Continuously monitor the patient's respiratory status for any signs of airway obstruction, such as increased work of breathing, stridor, or changes in voice.
  • Pain Management: Administer prescribed analgesics promptly and effectively to manage severe throat pain and trismus, which can significantly impact comfort and oral intake.
  • Hydration Support: Assist the patient with oral fluid intake, and be prepared for intravenous fluid administration if oral intake is insufficient due to pain or dysphagia.
  • Positioning: Encourage an upright or semi-Fowler's position to facilitate breathing and reduce discomfort from throat swelling.
  • Post-Procedure Observation: After incision and drainage, closely observe for any signs of bleeding, recurrence of swelling, or worsening symptoms.

🩺 Physician's Perspective

  • Diagnosis: Clinical diagnosis is often made based on characteristic symptoms and physical examination, including visualization of uvular deviation and tonsillar bulging. Imaging (e.g., CT scan) may be used to confirm the diagnosis and rule out other deep neck infections.
  • Treatment Modalities: Primary treatment involves drainage of the abscess via needle aspiration or incision and drainage (I&D), combined with broad-spectrum antibiotics targeting common oral flora.
  • Potential Complications: Untreated or inadequately treated PTAs can lead to severe complications, including airway obstruction, spread of infection to the parapharyngeal space, mediastinitis, internal jugular vein thrombosis (Lemierre's syndrome), and sepsis.
  • Recurrence & Prevention: Patients with recurrent peritonsillar abscesses or multiple episodes of severe tonsillitis may be candidates for elective tonsillectomy to prevent future occurrences.
  • Differential Diagnosis: Consider other conditions such as severe tonsillitis, epiglottitis, retropharyngeal abscess, and dental abscesses in the differential diagnosis.

🎓 Academic & Nursing Corner

  • Key Assessment Findings: Focus on unilateral throat pain, trismus, muffled voice, uvular deviation, and signs of systemic infection (fever, chills). Assess for signs of respiratory distress.
  • Nursing Interventions: Prepare for and assist with abscess drainage procedures. Administer prescribed intravenous antibiotics and analgesics. Monitor vital signs, pain levels, and airway patency closely.
  • Patient Education: Educate the patient on the importance of completing the full course of antibiotics, recognizing signs of worsening infection or recurrence, and proper pain management.
  • Post-Procedure Care: Monitor for post-drainage bleeding, ensure adequate pain control, encourage oral hydration, and facilitate a gradual return to a normal diet.
  • Documentation: Accurately document all assessment findings, interventions, patient responses, and any changes in the patient's condition, especially related to airway and pain.

🔬 Clinical Reference Index

  • Pathophysiology: Peritonsillar abscesses typically arise from a bacterial infection (often polymicrobial, including *Streptococcus pyogenes* and anaerobic bacteria) that extends from a crypt of the palatine tonsil into the peritonsillar space.
  • Anatomical Location: The peritonsillar space is a potential space located between the tonsillar capsule and the superior constrictor muscle of the pharynx.
  • ICD-10 Code: The primary diagnostic code for peritonsillar abscess is J36.
  • Pharmacological Management: Common antibiotic regimens include amoxicillin-clavulanate, clindamycin, or penicillin G with metronidazole, chosen based on local resistance patterns and patient allergies.
  • Surgical Considerations: Incision and drainage (I&D) or needle aspiration are the definitive treatments, often performed by an otolaryngologist. Tonsillectomy (quinsy tonsillectomy) may be performed acutely or electively.
